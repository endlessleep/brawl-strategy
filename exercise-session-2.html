Exercise 1

Nash with Three Actions

The Game

Player 1 chooses $T$ or $B$. Player 2 chooses $L$, $M$ or $R$.

Player 1: rows · Player 2: columns · Payoffs: (Player 1, Player 2)
$L$$M$$R$
$T$$(7,2)$$(2,7)$$(3,6)$
$B$$(2,7)$$(7,2)$$(4,5)$
Exercise 1 · Question 1
Find the complete set of Nash equilibria and their payoffs.
1
Singleton supports for Player 2.

Let $p=\Pr_1(T)$ and $\sigma_1=pT+(1-p)B$. Support = actions used with positive probability. Try Player 2’s seven supports.

$\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{L\}$:

$$BR_1(L)=\{T\},\qquad L\notin BR_2(T)=\{M\}.\quad\times$$

$\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{M\}$:

$$BR_1(M)=\{B\},\qquad M\notin BR_2(B)=\{L\}.\quad\times$$

$\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{R\}$:

$$BR_1(R)=\{B\},\qquad R\notin BR_2(B)=\{L\}.\quad\times$$

Each case admits a profitable deviation. Against either pure strategy of Player 1, Player 2 has a unique pure best response. Player 1 must therefore mix: $p\in(0,1)$.

2
Support $\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{L,M\}$.
$$\sigma_2=qL+(1-q)M,\qquad q\in(0,1).$$

Player 2’s indifference condition:

$$\begin{aligned} q\in(0,1)&\Rightarrow g_2(\sigma_1,L)=g_2(\sigma_1,M)\\ &\iff 2p+7(1-p)=7p+2(1-p)\\ &\iff 7-5p=2+5p\\ &\iff p=\frac12\in(0,1). \end{aligned}$$

Player 1’s indifference condition:

$$\begin{aligned} p\in(0,1)&\Rightarrow g_1(T,\sigma_2)=g_1(B,\sigma_2)\\ &\iff 7q+2(1-q)=2q+7(1-q)\\ &\iff 2+5q=7-5q\\ &\iff q=\frac12\in(0,1). \end{aligned}$$

Candidate:

$$\sigma=\left(\frac12T+\frac12B,\ \frac12L+\frac12M\right).$$

Check the unused strategy $R$:

$$g_2(\sigma)=g_2(\sigma_1,L)=\frac12(2)+\frac12(7)=\frac92,$$ $$g_2(\sigma_1,R)=\frac12(6)+\frac12(5)=\frac{11}{2}.$$ $$\frac92<\frac{11}{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\text{profitable deviation to }R.$$

Reject this candidate.

3
Support $\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{L,R\}$.
$$\sigma_2=qL+(1-q)R,\qquad q\in(0,1).$$

Player 2’s indifference determines $p$:

$$\begin{aligned} q\in(0,1)&\Rightarrow g_2(\sigma_1,L)=g_2(\sigma_1,R)\\ &\iff 2p+7(1-p)=6p+5(1-p)\\ &\iff 7-5p=5+p\\ &\iff p=\frac13\in(0,1). \end{aligned}$$

Player 1’s indifference determines $q$:

$$\begin{aligned} p\in(0,1)&\Rightarrow g_1(T,\sigma_2)=g_1(B,\sigma_2)\\ &\iff 7q+3(1-q)=2q+4(1-q)\\ &\iff 3+4q=4-2q\\ &\iff q=\frac16\in(0,1). \end{aligned}$$

Candidate:

$$\sigma=\left(\frac13T+\frac23B,\ \frac16L+\frac56R\right).$$

Check the unused strategy $M$:

$$g_2(\sigma)=g_2(\sigma_1,L)=\frac13(2)+\frac23(7)=\frac{16}{3},$$ $$g_2(\sigma_1,M)=\frac13(7)+\frac23(2)=\frac{11}{3}.$$ $$\boxed{g_2(\sigma)=\frac{16}{3}>\frac{11}{3}=g_2(\sigma_1,M).}$$

Supported actions give equal expected payoffs; unused $M$ gives less. ✓ No profitable deviation: this is an NE.

4
Support $\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{M,R\}$.

Suppose Player 2 uses only $M$ and $R$. In both columns, Player 1 prefers $B$:

$$g_1(B,M)=7>2=g_1(T,M),$$ $$g_1(B,R)=4>3=g_1(T,R).$$

So Player 1 chooses $B$. Against $B$, Player 2 prefers $R$:

$$g_2(B,R)=5>2=g_2(B,M).$$

Player 2 therefore cannot use both $M$ and $R$. Even $(B,R)$ fails: choosing $L$ instead gives more:

$$g_2(B,R)=5<7=g_2(B,L).$$

Reject this support.

5
Support $\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{L,M,R\}$.
$$\sigma_2=qL+rM+(1-q-r)R,$$ $$q>0,\qquad r>0,\qquad q+r<1.$$

To use all three actions, Player 2 must get the same payoff from each:

$$g_2(\sigma_1,L)=g_2(\sigma_1,M)=g_2(\sigma_1,R)$$ $$\iff 2p+7(1-p)=7p+2(1-p)=6p+5(1-p).$$

But:

$$\begin{aligned} g_2(\sigma_1,L)=g_2(\sigma_1,M)&\iff p=\frac12,\\ g_2(\sigma_1,L)=g_2(\sigma_1,R)&\iff p=\frac13. \end{aligned}$$

$p$ cannot equal both values. Reject this support.

✓
Equilibrium and payoffs.

Only $\{L,R\}$ remains. By indifference, use one supported action to calculate each expected payoff:

$$g_1(\sigma)=g_1(T,\sigma_2)=7\cdot\frac16+3\cdot\frac56=\frac{11}{3},$$ $$g_2(\sigma)=g_2(\sigma_1,L)=2\cdot\frac13+7\cdot\frac23=\frac{16}{3}.$$

Here $p=\Pr_1(T)$, $q=\Pr_2(L)$ and $\Pr_2(M)=0$:

$$\begin{array}{rcl} NE_{(p,q)}=\{ & (\tfrac13,\tfrac16) & \}\\[2pt] &\downarrow&\\[2pt] \text{Payoffs:}&(\tfrac{11}{3},\tfrac{16}{3})& \end{array}$$

Corresponding strategies $(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)$:

$$\left(\frac13,\frac16\right)\longleftrightarrow \left(\frac13T+\frac23B,\ \frac16L+\frac56R\right).$$

Full probability vectors in the original orders $(T,B)$ and $(L,M,R)$:

$$\sigma_1=(\tfrac13,\tfrac23),\qquad\sigma_2=(\tfrac16,0,\tfrac56).$$
Exercise 2

The Volunteer’s Dilemma

The Game

$N\geq2$ witnesses hear a cry for help. Each can answer the call or ignore it. A witness who answers receives $1-c$, with $0<c<1$.

If at least one witness answers, anyone who ignored the call receives $1$. If nobody answers, everyone receives $0$.

Exercise 2 · Question 1
Write the game in normal form.
1
Actions and payoffs.

Players: $N$ witnesses. Strategies: $R$ (respond), $I$ (ignore):

$$S_i=\{R,I\},\qquad i=1,\ldots,N.$$

Responding costs $c$. Ignoring gives a positive payoff only if another witness responds:

$$g_i(R,s_{-i})=1-c,$$ $$g_i(I,s_{-i})=\begin{cases} 1,&\text{if some }j\neq i\text{ chooses }R,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$
2
Probabilities and expected payoffs.

$x_i$: probability of responding; $1-x_i$: probability of ignoring:

$$\sigma_i=x_iR+(1-x_i)I,\qquad x_i\in[0,1].$$

$x=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)$ is the probability profile; $x_{-i}$ excludes player $i$. By independence:

$$\Pr(\text{all other witnesses ignore})=\prod_{j\neq i}(1-x_j).$$

$\prod$ denotes a product. Ignoring gives $1$ unless all other witnesses ignore:

$$g_i(R,x_{-i})=1-c,$$ $$g_i(I,x_{-i})=1-\prod_{j\neq i}(1-x_j).$$

A mixing player must be indifferent between both pure strategies.

Exercise 2 · Question 2
Determine the complete set of Nash equilibria as a function of $c$ and $N$.
1
Pure-strategy equilibria.

All players choose $I$: deviating to $R$ increases the payoff:

$$0<1-c.$$

At least two choose $R$: a player choosing $R$ gains by deviating to $I$, since another still responds:

$$1-c<1.$$

Exactly one chooses $R$: deviating to $I$ gives $0$ instead of $1-c$. Others receive $1$; responding gives $1-c$. No profitable deviation exists.

There are $N$ pure NE: exactly one player chooses $R$.

2
Possible mixed-strategy supports.

$N$ is the number of players; $m$ is the number using a non-pure mixed strategy. Label the latter $1,\ldots,m$:

$$x_i\in(0,1)\quad(i\leq m),\qquad x_i\in\{0,1\}\quad(i>m).$$

Other players choose $I$. If anyone chose $R$ with probability $1$, everyone else would strictly prefer $I$ ($1>1-c$), ruling out mixing:

$$x_i=0\qquad(i>m).$$

$m=1$ is impossible: with everyone else choosing $I$, player 1’s unique best response is $R$: $BR_1(I,\ldots,I)=\{R\}$.

So $m\geq2$. The actions used are:

$$\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_i)=\begin{cases} \{R,I\},&i\leq m,\\ \{I\},&i>m. \end{cases}$$
3
Apply the strong indifference principle.

For each mixing player $i\leq m$, $R$ and $I$ must give equal expected payoffs:

$$g_i(R,x_{-i})=g_i(I,x_{-i})$$ $$\iff 1-c=1-\prod_{j\neq i}(1-x_j).$$

For $j>m$, $x_j=0$, so $1-x_j=1$. Indifference requires probability $c$ that all other mixing players ignore:

$$\boxed{c=\prod_{\substack{j\leq m\\j\neq i}}(1-x_j)\qquad(i=1,\ldots,m).}$$
4
Multiply the equations.

Multiply the $m$ indifference equations:

$$\begin{aligned} c^m &=\prod_{i'=1}^{m}\left[\prod_{\substack{j\leq m\\j\neq i'}}(1-x_j)\right]\\ &=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-x_j)^{m-1}\\ &=\left[\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-x_j)\right]^{m-1}. \end{aligned}$$

Each factor appears $m-1$ times, missing only from its own witness’s equation. Take the $(m-1)$th root:

$$\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-x_j)=c^{m/(m-1)}.$$
5
Determine each individual probability.

Divide by witness $i$’s equation ($=c$). All other factors cancel; $1-x_i$ remains:

$$\begin{aligned} 1-x_i &=\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-x_j)}{\prod_{\substack{j\leq m\\j\neq i}}(1-x_j)}\\ &=\frac{c^{m/(m-1)}}{c} =c^{1/(m-1)}. \end{aligned}$$ $$\boxed{x_i=1-c^{1/(m-1)}\in(0,1)\qquad(i\leq m).}$$

Candidate:

$$\sigma_i=\begin{cases} \left(1-c^{1/(m-1)}\right)R+c^{1/(m-1)}I,&i\leq m,\\ I,&i>m. \end{cases}$$
6
Check the unused strategy of each remaining player.

For $i>m$, deviating from $I$ to $R$ must not increase the payoff:

$$g_i(I,x_{-i})\geq g_i(R,x_{-i}).$$

Player $i>m$ receives $1$ unless all $m$ mixing players choose $I$:

$$g_i(I,x_{-i})=1-\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-x_j)=1-c^{m/(m-1)},$$ $$g_i(R,x_{-i})=1-c.$$

Since $0<c<1$ and $m/(m-1)>1$:

$$c^{m/(m-1)}<c\quad\Rightarrow\quad 1-c^{m/(m-1)}>1-c.$$

Thus $I$ is a strict best response. Mixing players are indifferent. ✓ No profitable deviation remains: this is an NE. If $m=N$, there is no unused strategy.

✓
All equilibria.

$e_k$: player $k$ chooses $R$; all others choose $I$. $x^M$: $m\geq2$ players in $M$ mix; all others choose $I$. Their probabilities of responding are:

$$x_i^M=\begin{cases} 1-c^{1/(m-1)},&i\in M,\\ 0,&i\notin M. \end{cases}$$ $$\boxed{NE_x=\{e_k:k=1,\ldots,N\}\ \cup\ \{x^M:|M|\geq2\}.}$$
$$\begin{array}{rcc} \text{NE:}&e_k&x^M\quad(|M|=m\geq2)\\[2pt] &\downarrow&\downarrow\\[2pt] \text{Payoff of }i\text{:}& \begin{cases}1-c,&i=k,\\1,&i\neq k\end{cases}& \begin{cases}1-c,&i\in M,\\1-c^{m/(m-1)},&i\notin M\end{cases} \end{array}$$

In actions:

$$\begin{aligned} e_k:&\quad\sigma_k=R,\quad\sigma_i=I\ (i\neq k),\\[4pt] x^M:&\quad\sigma_i=\begin{cases} (1-c^{1/(m-1)})R+c^{1/(m-1)}I,&i\in M,\\ I,&i\notin M. \end{cases} \end{aligned}$$

Each nonempty group gives one NE; $\binom Nm$ counts groups of size $m$:

$$\boxed{|NE|=N+\sum_{m=2}^{N}\binom Nm=\sum_{m=1}^{N}\binom Nm=2^N-1.}$$

When everyone mixes, $m=N$:

$$x_1=\cdots=x_N=1-c^{1/(N-1)}.$$
Exercise 2 · Question 3
Compute the probability that the victim is saved at equilibrium. Comment on the effects of cost and group size.
1
Probability that the victim is saved.

At least one response is the complement of all players ignoring. Thus:

$$\Pr(\text{saved})=1-\prod_{i=1}^{N}(1-x_i).$$

Pure NE: one player responds with probability $1$:

$$\boxed{\Pr(\text{saved}\mid e_k)=1.}$$

$m\geq2$ mixing players: each ignores with probability $c^{1/(m-1)}$. The victim is not saved if all $m$ ignore:

$$\begin{aligned} \Pr(\text{saved}\mid x^M) &=1-\left(c^{1/(m-1)}\right)^m\\ &=\boxed{1-c^{m/(m-1)}}. \end{aligned}$$

Across all NE, the possible rescue probabilities are:

$$\{1\}\ \cup\ \left\{1-c^{m/(m-1)}:m=2,\ldots,N\right\}.$$
2
Effect of the number of mixing players.

Pure NE guarantee rescue. In mixed NE, each player relies on the others. As $m$ increases, the exponent

$$\frac{m}{m-1}=1+\frac1{m-1}$$

decreases. Since $0<c<1$, a smaller exponent gives a larger $c^{m/(m-1)}$: everyone ignoring becomes more likely.

For fixed $m$, higher $c$ reduces individual response probabilities and the probability that the victim is saved.

3
Lowest equilibrium probability of rescue.

This occurs when everyone mixes, so $m=N$:

$$\boxed{\min_{x\in NE_x}\Pr(\text{saved}\mid x)=1-c^{N/(N-1)}.}$$

Additional witnesses can choose $I$, preserving existing NE, or join the mixing group, reducing the probability of rescue. When all $N$ mix:

$$\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(1-c^{N/(N-1)}\right)=1-c<1.$$

Even with many witnesses, rescue is not certain at this NE. Pure NE guarantee rescue: exactly one player chooses $R$.

Exercise 3

Inspection Game

The Game

A boss can inspect an employee at cost $h$, or choose not to inspect. The employee can work or shirk. Work costs the employee $g$ and produces value $v$ for the boss.

The boss pays wage $w$ unless inspection catches the employee shirking. Assume $0<h<g<w$.

Exercise 3 · Question 1
Describe this situation as a normal-form game.
1
Players and strategy sets.

Player 1 is the employee: work ($W$) or shirk ($S$). Player 2 is the boss: inspect ($I$) or not inspect ($N$).

$$S_1=\{W,S\},\qquad S_2=\{I,N\}.$$
2
Payoff matrix.

Working gives the employee wage minus effort cost, $w-g$. The boss gets value minus wage, $v-w$, and pays $h$ if inspecting. A shirker caught by inspection gets no wage.

Employee: rows · Boss: columns · Payoffs: (employee, boss)
$I$$N$
$W$$(w-g,\ v-w-h)$$(w-g,\ v-w)$
$S$$(0,-h)$$(w,-w)$

At $(S,I)$, no work is done and no wage is paid: the employee gets $0$; inspection costs the boss $h$.

Exercise 3 · Question 2
Find all Nash equilibria. Explain how equilibrium behavior changes with the parameters.
1
Pure-strategy equilibria.

Every pure profile admits a profitable deviation:

  • $(W,I)$: the boss deviates to $N$, since $v-w>v-w-h$.
  • $(W,N)$: the employee deviates to $S$, since $w>w-g$.
  • $(S,N)$: the boss deviates to $I$, since $-h>-w$.
  • $(S,I)$: the employee deviates to $W$, since $w-g>0$.

Equivalently:

$$BR_1(I)=\{W\},\qquad BR_1(N)=\{S\},$$ $$BR_2(W)=\{N\},\qquad BR_2(S)=\{I\}.$$

No pure NE. Every pure action has a unique pure best response. Thus both players must mix.

2
Employee’s indifference condition.

$p$ is the employee’s probability of working; $q$ is the boss’s probability of inspecting:

$$\sigma_1=pW+(1-p)S,\qquad\sigma_2=qI+(1-q)N.$$

Both mix: $p,q\in(0,1)$. Working and shirking must give equal expected payoffs:

$$p\in(0,1)\Rightarrow g_1(W,\sigma_2)=g_1(S,\sigma_2).$$

Work:

$$g_1(W,\sigma_2)=q(w-g)+(1-q)(w-g)=w-g.$$

Shirk:

$$g_1(S,\sigma_2)=q(0)+(1-q)w.$$

Therefore:

$$w-g=(1-q)w\iff qw=g\iff\boxed{q=\frac gw\in(0,1).}$$
3
Boss’s indifference condition.
$$q\in(0,1)\Rightarrow g_2(\sigma_1,I)=g_2(\sigma_1,N).$$

Inspect:

$$\begin{aligned} g_2(\sigma_1,I)&=p(v-w-h)+(1-p)(-h)\\ &=p(v-w)-h. \end{aligned}$$

Do not inspect:

$$\begin{aligned} g_2(\sigma_1,N)&=p(v-w)+(1-p)(-w)\\ &=pv-w. \end{aligned}$$

Therefore:

$$\begin{aligned} p(v-w)-h=pv-w &\iff pw=w-h\\ &\iff\boxed{p=1-\frac hw\in(0,1).} \end{aligned}$$

Candidate:

$$\sigma=\left(\left(1-\frac hw\right)W+\frac hw S,\ \frac gw I+\left(1-\frac gw\right)N\right).$$

Since $0<h<g<w$, both probabilities are valid. Each player is indifferent between both actions. ✓ This is the unique NE.

4
Equilibrium and payoffs.

Either action gives the same payoff. Use $W$ for the employee, $N$ for the boss:

$$g_1(\sigma)=g_1(W,\sigma_2)=w-g,$$ $$g_2(\sigma)=g_2(\sigma_1,N)=pv-w=v\left(1-\frac hw\right)-w.$$
$$\begin{array}{rcl} NE_{(p,q)}=\{&(1-\tfrac hw,\tfrac gw)&\}\\[2pt] &\downarrow&\\[2pt] \text{Payoffs:}&\left(w-g,\ v(1-\tfrac hw)-w\right)& \end{array}$$

Corresponding strategies (employee, boss):

$$\left(1-\frac hw,\frac gw\right)\longleftrightarrow \left(\left(1-\frac hw\right)W+\frac hw S,\ \frac gw I+\left(1-\frac gw\right)N\right).$$
5
Higher costs or wage.

Change one parameter at a time, keeping $0<h<g<w$:

  • Inspection becomes more costly: $h\uparrow\Rightarrow p=1-h/w\downarrow$. The employee works less; $q$ is unchanged.
  • Work becomes more costly: $g\uparrow\Rightarrow q=g/w\uparrow$. The boss inspects more; $p$ is unchanged.
  • The wage increases: $w\uparrow\Rightarrow p\uparrow,\ q\downarrow$. The employee works more and the boss inspects less.

Both boss payoffs contain $pv$: it cancels, so $v$ changes neither probability.

Exercise 4

Teamwork

The Game

Two individuals can produce a good that is shared equally. Each simultaneously chooses no effort ($P$) or effort ($E$). Effort costs $c\in(1/2,1)$.

Each person’s payoff is half of the total output, minus $c$ if that person exerted effort. The two questions use different production rules.

Exercise 4 · Question 1
Total output is 2 if both exert effort, 1 if only one does, and 0 otherwise. Give the normal form, identify the type of game, and find all Nash equilibria.
1
Payoff matrix.

Split the output equally, then subtract $c$ from each worker’s payoff.

Player 1: rows · Player 2: columns · Payoffs: (Player 1, Player 2)
$P$$E$
$P$$(0,0)$$(\tfrac12,\ \tfrac12-c)$
$E$$(\tfrac12-c,\ \tfrac12)$$(1-c,\ 1-c)$
2
Strict dominance.

Compare no effort ($P$) with effort ($E$) in each column:

$$g_1(P,P)=0>\frac12-c=g_1(E,P),$$ $$g_1(P,E)=\frac12>1-c=g_1(E,E).$$

Since $c>1/2$, $P$ always gives a higher payoff. By symmetry, $P$ is strictly dominant for both players.

If Player 2 works with probability $q$, write $\sigma_2=qE+(1-q)P$. Then:

$$g_1(P,\sigma_2)=\frac q2,$$ $$\begin{aligned} g_1(E,\sigma_2)&=q(1-c)+(1-q)\left(\frac12-c\right)\\ &=\frac12+\frac q2-c. \end{aligned}$$ $$g_1(P,\sigma_2)-g_1(E,\sigma_2)=c-\frac12>0.$$

Even against a mixture, effort gives less. Neither player works at an NE: only $(P,P)$ remains.

✓
Equilibrium and social optimum.

Let $p=\Pr_1(E)$ and $q=\Pr_2(E)$:

$$\sigma_1=pE+(1-p)P,\qquad\sigma_2=qE+(1-q)P.$$
$$\begin{array}{rcl} NE_{(p,q)}=\{&(0,0)&\}\\[2pt] &\downarrow&\\[2pt] \text{Payoffs:}&(0,0)& \end{array}$$

Corresponding strategies:

$$ (0,0)\longleftrightarrow(P,P). $$

Each prefers not to work, but both would gain $1-c>0$ by working together: a prisoner’s dilemma.

Working together maximizes total payoff:

$$\underbrace{2(1-c)}_{(E,E)}>\underbrace{1-c}_{(E,P)\text{ or }(P,E)}>\underbrace{0}_{(P,P)}.$$
Exercise 4 · Question 2
Total output is 2 if both exert effort, and 0 otherwise. Give the normal form, identify the game, find all Nash equilibria, and explain how effort in the completely mixed equilibrium changes with $c$.
1
Payoff matrix.

Both must work to produce anything. A lone worker pays $c$ for no output.

Player 1: rows · Player 2: columns · Payoffs: (Player 1, Player 2)
$P$$E$
$P$$(0,0)$$(0,-c)$
$E$$(-c,0)$$(1-c,\ 1-c)$

This is a coordination game.

2
Pure-strategy supports.

The best response to $P$ is $P$, since $0>-c$. The best response to $E$ is $E$, since $1-c>0$.

$$\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{P\}\Rightarrow BR_1(P)=\{P\},\quad BR_2(P)=\{P\}.$$

✓ $(P,P)$ is an NE.

$$\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{E\}\Rightarrow BR_1(E)=\{E\},\quad BR_2(E)=\{E\}.$$

✓ $(E,E)$ is an NE.

The asymmetric profiles admit profitable deviations. Every pure action has a unique best response, ruling out exactly one mixing player.

3
Completely mixed equilibrium.

Let $p=\Pr_1(E)$ and $q=\Pr_2(E)$:

$$\sigma_1=pE+(1-p)P,\qquad\sigma_2=qE+(1-q)P.$$

With $p,q\in(0,1)$, $E$ and $P$ must give each player equal payoffs:

Player 1:

$$p\in(0,1)\Rightarrow g_1(E,\sigma_2)=g_1(P,\sigma_2).$$ $$g_1(E,\sigma_2)=q(1-c)+(1-q)(-c)=q-c,$$ $$g_1(P,\sigma_2)=q(0)+(1-q)(0)=0.$$ $$q-c=0\iff q=c.$$

Player 2:

$$q\in(0,1)\Rightarrow g_2(\sigma_1,E)=g_2(\sigma_1,P).$$ $$g_2(\sigma_1,E)=p(1-c)+(1-p)(-c)=p-c,$$ $$g_2(\sigma_1,P)=p(0)+(1-p)(0)=0.$$ $$p-c=0\iff p=c.$$

Candidate:

$$\sigma=\left(cE+(1-c)P,\ cE+(1-c)P\right).$$

Since $c\in(1/2,1)$, probabilities are valid. Both players are indifferent. ✓ No profitable deviation: this is an NE.

4
Equilibria and payoffs.

At the mixed NE, not working gives $0$. Working must therefore also give $0$:

$$c(1-c)+(1-c)(-c)=0.$$
$$\begin{array}{rcccl} NE_{(p,q)}=\{&(0,0),&(1,1),&(c,c)&\}\\[2pt] &\downarrow&\downarrow&\downarrow&\\[2pt] \text{Payoffs:}&(0,0)&(1-c,1-c)&(0,0)& \end{array}$$

Corresponding strategies:

$$\begin{aligned} (0,0)&\longleftrightarrow(P,P),\\[4pt] (1,1)&\longleftrightarrow(E,E),\\[4pt] (c,c)&\longleftrightarrow\left(cE+(1-c)P,\ cE+(1-c)P\right). \end{aligned}$$
5
When the cost increases.
$$\boxed{p=q=c\quad\text{at the completely mixed NE}.}$$

Higher cost lowers the payoff from working. To keep it equal to not working, the opponent must work more often:

$$g_1(E,\sigma_2)=q-c=0\iff q=c.$$

Likewise, $p=c$. So both probabilities of exerting effort increase with $c$ at the mixed NE.

As $c\to1^{-}$:

$$(p,q)=(c,c)\longrightarrow(1,1)\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad(E,E).$$

The mixed NE converges to the pure equilibrium $(E,E)$. For $c\in(1/2,1)$, the three NE remain distinct.
