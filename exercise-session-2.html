Exercise 1

Nash with Three Actions

The Game

Player 1 chooses $T$ with probability $p$ and $B$ with probability $1-p$: $\sigma_1=pT+(1-p)B$. Player 2 has three actions, so we must also decide which columns receive positive probability.

For a two-column support, $q$ is the probability of the first named column and $1-q$ that of the second; the unused column receives zero. Thus $q$ refers to $L$ in $\{L,M\}$ and $\{L,R\}$, but to $M$ in $\{M,R\}$.

Player 1: rows · Player 2: columns · Payoffs: (Player 1, Player 2)
$L$$M$$R$
$T$$(7,2)$$(2,7)$$(3,6)$
$B$$(2,7)$$(7,2)$$(4,5)$
Exercise 1 · Question 1
Find the complete set of Nash equilibria and their payoffs.
1
First: why we cannot simply eliminate a column.
Player 1's best responses: $BR_1(L)=\{T\}$, $BR_1(M)=\{B\}$, $BR_1(R)=\{B\}$. Player 2's: $BR_2(T)=\{M\}$ and $BR_2(B)=\{L\}$.

Column $R$ is never best against a pure row, but could be best against a mixture. To test whether we can eliminate it, try $\tau_2=aL+(1-a)M$:
  • Against $T$: $7-5a>6$, so $a<1/5$.
  • Against $B$: $2+5a>5$, so $a>3/5$.
No probability satisfies both conditions. No mixture of $L$ and $M$ strictly dominates $R$, so we keep it and check every support.
2
Singleton supports for Player 2: $\{L\},\{M\},\{R\}$.
$\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{L\}$: $BR_1(L)=\{T\}$, but $L\notin BR_2(T)=\{M\}$. Reject.
$\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{M\}$: $BR_1(M)=\{B\}$, but $M\notin BR_2(B)=\{L\}$. Reject.
$\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{R\}$: $BR_1(R)=\{B\}$, but $R\notin BR_2(B)=\{L\}$. Reject.
Therefore no equilibrium has Player 2 playing a pure strategy.

Could Player 1 be pure while Player 2 mixes? If Player 1 plays $T$, Player 2 uniquely prefers $M$, against which Player 1 prefers $B$. If Player 1 plays $B$, Player 2 uniquely prefers $L$, against which Player 1 prefers $T$. Both cases fail.

Player 1 must therefore mix between $T$ and $B$. For Player 2, we still need to test $\{L,M\}$, $\{L,R\}$, $\{M,R\}$ and $\{L,M,R\}$.
3
Support $\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{L,M\}$ — reject.
Write $\sigma_1=pT+(1-p)B$ and $\sigma_2=qL+(1-q)M$, with $q\in(0,1)$. Since Player 2 mixes: $$g_2(\sigma_1,L)=g_2(\sigma_1,M)$$ $$2p+7(1-p)=7p+2(1-p)\iff\boxed{p=\tfrac12\in(0,1).}$$ Since Player 1 also mixes, impose: $$g_1(T,\sigma_2)=g_1(B,\sigma_2)$$ $$7q+2(1-q)=2q+7(1-q)\iff\boxed{q=\tfrac12\in(0,1).}$$ This gives a candidate $(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)=(\tfrac12T+\tfrac12B,\tfrac12L+\tfrac12M)$, but check the unused column R: $$g_2(\sigma_1,L)=g_2(\sigma_1,M)=\tfrac92\quad<\quad g_2(\sigma_1,R)=\tfrac{11}{2}.$$ Player 2 would deviate to R. Reject $\{L,M\}$.
4
Support $\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{L,R\}$ — valid equilibrium.
Let $\sigma_2=qL+(1-q)R$, $q\in(0,1)$. Player 2 is indifferent when: $$g_2(\sigma_1,L)=g_2(\sigma_1,R)$$ $$2p+7(1-p)=6p+5(1-p)\iff7-5p=5+p\iff\boxed{p=\tfrac13.}$$ Check unused M: $g_2(\sigma_1,M)=\tfrac{11}{3}<\tfrac{16}{3}=g_2(\sigma_1,L)=g_2(\sigma_1,R)$, so M is not a profitable deviation.

Player 1 must also be indifferent: $$g_1(T,\sigma_2)=g_1(B,\sigma_2)$$ $$7q+3(1-q)=2q+4(1-q)\iff\boxed{q=\tfrac16.}$$ Both probabilities are strictly between 0 and 1. This support gives a Nash equilibrium.
5
Support $\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{M,R\}$ — reject.
Set $\sigma_2=qM+(1-q)R$, with $0<q<1$. Indifference for Player 2 requires $$g_2(\sigma_1,M)=g_2(\sigma_1,R)$$ $$7p+2(1-p)=6p+5(1-p)$$ $$2+5p=5+p\iff\boxed{p=\tfrac34\in(0,1).}$$ Player 1 would therefore have to mix. But her payoffs against this support are $$g_1(T,\sigma_2)=2q+3(1-q)=3-q,$$ $$g_1(B,\sigma_2)=7q+4(1-q)=4+3q.$$ For every $q\in(0,1)$, $g_1(B,\sigma_2)-g_1(T,\sigma_2)=1+4q>0$. Equating the two payoffs would give $q=-1/4$, which is not an admissible probability. Player 1 cannot mix as required by $p=3/4$. Reject.
6
Support $\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{L,M,R\}$ — reject.
All three columns would have to give Player 2 the same payoff. Equality between L and M forces $p=1/2$, but at that probability $g_2(L)=g_2(M)=9/2<11/2=g_2(R)$. Reject.
✓
Complete answer and NE payoffs.
Exactly one support works: $\{L,R\}$, with $$\boxed{\sigma_1=\tfrac13T+\tfrac23B,\qquad\sigma_2=\tfrac16L+0M+\tfrac56R.}$$ Since Player 1 is indifferent, calculate from T; since Player 2 is indifferent, calculate from L: $$g_1=7\cdot\tfrac16+3\cdot\tfrac56=\boxed{\tfrac{11}{3}},$$ $$g_2=2\cdot\tfrac13+7\cdot\tfrac23=\boxed{\tfrac{16}{3}}.$$
$$\boxed{NE=\left\{\left(\tfrac13T+\tfrac23B,\,\tfrac16L+\tfrac56R\right)\right\},\quad\text{NE payoffs}=\left(\tfrac{11}{3},\tfrac{16}{3}\right).}$$
Exam method: list supports → impose indifference → check probabilities → check unused actions → state NE and NE payoffs.
Exercise 2

Inspection Game

The Game

A boss can inspect an employee at cost $h$, or choose not to inspect. The employee can work or shirk. Work costs the employee $g$ and produces value $v$ for the boss.

The boss pays wage $w$ unless inspection catches the employee shirking. Assume $0<h<g<w$.

Exercise 2 · Question 1
Describe this situation as a normal-form game.
1
Specify the players and actions.

Player 1 is the boss: inspect ($I$) or do not inspect ($D$). Player 2 is the employee: work ($W$) or shirk ($S$).

$$S_1=\{I,D\},\qquad S_2=\{W,S\}.$$
2
Calculate payoffs in each outcome.
  • $(I,W)$: the boss receives $v$, pays $w$, and incurs inspection cost $h$. The employee receives $w$ and pays effort cost $g$.
  • $(I,S)$: no work is produced and no wage is paid. The boss still pays inspection cost $h$.
  • $(D,W)$: work is produced and the wage is paid, with no inspection cost.
  • $(D,S)$: the employee is paid despite producing no work.
Boss: rows · Employee: columns · Payoffs: (boss, employee)
$W$$S$
$I$$(v-w-h,\ w-g)$$(-h,\ 0)$
$D$$(v-w,\ w-g)$$(-w,\ w)$
Exercise 2 · Question 2
Find all Nash equilibria. Explain how equilibrium behavior changes with the parameters.
1
Check pure supports.
  • If the employee works, the boss prefers $D$ to save $h$. If the employee shirks, the boss prefers $I$ because $-h>-w$.
  • If the boss inspects, the employee prefers $W$ because $w-g>0$. Without inspection, the employee prefers $S$ because $w>w-g$.

No cell contains mutual best responses, so there is no pure equilibrium. A pure strategy by either player gives the other a unique best response, which in turn makes the first player want to switch. Thus neither player can be pure in an equilibrium where the other mixes: both must use both actions.

2
Make the employee indifferent to find the inspection probability.

Let $p=\Pr(I)$ and $q=\Pr(W)$. Working always earns the wage and incurs the effort cost. Shirking earns the wage only without inspection:

$$u_2(W,p)=w-g,\qquad u_2(S,p)=(1-p)w.$$

Since the employee mixes, these payoffs must be equal:

$$w-g=(1-p)w\iff\boxed{p^*=\frac gw.}$$
3
Make the boss indifferent to find the work probability.
$$\begin{aligned} u_1(I,q)&=q(v-w-h)+(1-q)(-h)=q(v-w)-h,\\ u_1(D,q)&=q(v-w)+(1-q)(-w)=qv-w. \end{aligned}$$

Equating these payoffs gives:

$$q(v-w)-h=qv-w\iff w(1-q)=h\iff\boxed{q^*=1-\frac hw.}$$

Both $p^*$ and $q^*$ lie strictly between $0$ and $1$. Every action is in the support, so there is no unused action to check.

4
State the unique equilibrium and its payoffs.
$$\boxed{\sigma_1^*=\frac gw I+\left(1-\frac gw\right)D,\qquad \sigma_2^*=\left(1-\frac hw\right)W+\frac hw S.}$$

The probabilities describe behavior. The equilibrium payoffs are obtained by substituting them into either supported action’s payoff:

$$\boxed{u_1^*=v\left(1-\frac hw\right)-w,\qquad u_2^*=w-g.}$$
5
Compare changes in the parameters.

Hold the other parameters fixed and keep $0<h<g<w$.

Parameter increasesInspection $p^*=g/w$Work $q^*=1-h/w$
Inspection cost $h$UnchangedDecreases
Effort cost $g$IncreasesUnchanged
Wage $w$DecreasesIncreases
Work value $v$UnchangedUnchanged

The inspection probability keeps the employee indifferent, so it depends on $g/w$. The shirking probability keeps the boss indifferent, so it depends on $h/w$. A larger $v$ raises the boss’s payoff from work equally with or without inspection; it cancels from the comparison between those actions.

Exercise 3

The Volunteer’s Dilemma

The Game

$N\geq2$ witnesses hear a cry for help. Each can answer the call or ignore it. A witness who answers receives $1-c$, with $0<c<1$.

If at least one witness answers, anyone who ignored the call receives $1$. If nobody answers, everyone receives $0$.

Exercise 3 · Question 1
Write the game in normal form.
1
Specify actions and payoffs.

The players are the $N$ witnesses. For each player $i$, let $A$ mean answer and $I$ mean ignore:

$$S_i=\{A,I\},\qquad i=1,\ldots,N.$$ $$u_i(s)=\begin{cases} 1-c,&s_i=A,\\ 1,&s_i=I\text{ and at least one }j\neq i\text{ chooses }A,\\ 0,&s_j=I\text{ for every }j. \end{cases}$$

Answering guarantees that help arrives but costs $c$. Ignoring is better if someone else answers, and worse if nobody else does.

2
Express payoffs against mixed strategies.

Let $p_i$ be the probability that witness $i$ answers. Choices are independent, so the probability that all the other witnesses ignore the call is:

$$Q_{-i}=\prod_{j\neq i}(1-p_j).$$ $$u_i(A,p_{-i})=1-c,\qquad u_i(I,p_{-i})=1-Q_{-i}.$$

The comparison gives the best-response rule:

$$\begin{cases} p_i=1,&Q_{-i}>c,\\ p_i=0,&Q_{-i}<c,\\ p_i\in[0,1],&Q_{-i}=c. \end{cases}$$

Mixing requires $Q_{-i}=c$: the risk that everyone else ignores the call must exactly equal the cost of answering.

Exercise 3 · Question 2
Determine the complete set of Nash equilibria as a function of $c$ and $N$.
1
Find the pure equilibria.
  • Nobody answers: any player can improve from $0$ to $1-c>0$ by answering.
  • At least two answer: an answering player can ignore instead, relying on another witness and improving from $1-c$ to $1$.
  • Exactly one answers: that player would get $0$ by ignoring, while everyone else already gets $1$ and would lose $c$ by answering.

Thus there are $N$ pure equilibria: each has exactly one volunteer.

2
Identify which players can mix.

If some player answers with probability $1$, everyone else strictly prefers to ignore. This gives one of the pure equilibria above.

In any other equilibrium, let $M$ be the set of witnesses who answer with positive probability, and let $m=|M|$. Everyone in $M$ must mix, with $0<p_i<1$; those outside $M$ have $p_i=0$.

We need $m\geq2$. With no active witness, someone would answer; with just one mixing witness, that person faces nobody else answering and would strictly prefer to answer with certainty.

3
Use indifference to solve for the mixing probabilities.

For every $i\in M$, indifference requires:

$$\prod_{\substack{j\in M\\j\neq i}}(1-p_j)=c.$$

Let $Q=\prod_{j\in M}(1-p_j)>0$. The equations become $Q/(1-p_i)=c$, so all players in $M$ have the same probability $p$. Hence:

$$(1-p)^{m-1}=c\iff\boxed{p=1-c^{1/(m-1)}.}$$

This probability lies strictly between $0$ and $1$.

4
Check the witnesses outside the mixing group.

For $i\notin M$, the probability that nobody else answers is:

$$Q_{-i}=(1-p)^m=c^{m/(m-1)}<c.$$

Therefore ignoring gives $1-c^{m/(m-1)}>1-c$, strictly more than answering. These players are indeed best responding with $p_i=0$.

✓
Collect all equilibria.

The complete set consists of:

  • Pure equilibria: one player has $p_i=1$ and all others have $p_j=0$.
  • Mixed equilibria: choose any subset $M$ of $m\in\{2,\ldots,N\}$ witnesses and set
$$\boxed{p_i=\begin{cases} 1-c^{1/(m-1)},&i\in M,\\ 0,&i\notin M. \end{cases}}$$

The completely mixed, symmetric equilibrium uses $M=\{1,\ldots,N\}$:

$$\boxed{p_1=\cdots=p_N=1-c^{1/(N-1)}.}$$

Each mixing witness earns $1-c$. Witnesses outside the mixing group earn $1-c^{m/(m-1)}$. At a pure equilibrium, the volunteer earns $1-c$ and every other witness earns $1$.

Exercise 3 · Question 3
Compute the probability that the victim is saved at equilibrium. Comment on the effects of cost and group size.
1
Compute the probability that at least one witness answers.

The victim is saved unless every witness ignores the call:

$$\Pr(\text{saved})=1-\prod_{i=1}^{N}(1-p_i).$$

At any pure equilibrium, one person answers with certainty, so the victim is saved with probability $1$.

At an equilibrium with $m\geq2$ mixing witnesses:

$$\boxed{\Pr(\text{saved})=1-c^{m/(m-1)}.}$$
2
Compare costs and the number of mixing witnesses.

For a fixed $m$, a higher cost $c$ lowers both the probability that each active witness answers and the probability of rescue.

For a fixed $c$, increasing $m$ lowers each individual’s answering probability. It also lowers the rescue probability: the exponent $m/(m-1)$ decreases, so $c^{m/(m-1)}$ increases because $0<c<1$.

3
Apply this to the symmetric equilibrium.
$$\Pr(\text{saved})_{\mathrm{sym}}=1-c^{N/(N-1)},\qquad \lim_{N\to\infty}\Pr(\text{saved})_{\mathrm{sym}}=1-c.$$

For example, if $c=1/2$:

WitnessesEach answers with probabilityRescue probability
$N=2$$1/2$$1-(1/2)^2=3/4$
$N=3$$1-1/\sqrt2\approx0.293$$1-(1/2)^{3/2}\approx0.646$

When everyone mixes symmetrically, more witnesses can mean a lower chance of rescue: each relies more on someone else to act. This comparison concerns the symmetric mixed equilibrium. Pure equilibria still guarantee rescue, and adding witnesses who always ignore does not change the probability for a fixed mixing group.

Exercise 4

Teamwork

The Game

Two individuals can produce a good that is shared equally. Each simultaneously chooses no effort ($P$) or effort ($E$). Effort costs $c\in(1/2,1)$.

Each person’s payoff is half of the total output, minus $c$ if that person exerted effort. The two questions use different production rules.

Exercise 4 · Question 1
Total output is 2 if both exert effort, 1 if only one does, and 0 otherwise. Give the normal form, identify the type of game, and find all Nash equilibria.
1
Convert total output into individual payoffs.

If both exert effort, each receives $1$ and pays $c$. If only one does, each receives $1/2$, but only the person who worked pays $c$. With no effort, output and payoffs are zero.

Player 1: rows · Player 2: columns
$P$$E$
$P$$(0,0)$$(1/2,\ 1/2-c)$
$E$$(1/2-c,\ 1/2)$$(1-c,\ 1-c)$
2
Compare effort with no effort.
  • Against $P$, choosing $P$ gives $0>1/2-c$, the payoff from $E$.
  • Against $E$, choosing $P$ gives $1/2>1-c$, the payoff from $E$.

Both inequalities follow from $c>1/2$. Thus $P$ strictly dominates $E$ for each player.

For any probability $q$ that the other person exerts effort, the comparison remains strict:

$$\begin{aligned} u_i(P,q)&=\frac q2,\\ u_i(E,q)&=(1-q)\left(\frac12-c\right)+q(1-c) =\frac12+\frac q2-c,\\ u_i(P,q)-u_i(E,q)&=c-\frac12>0. \end{aligned}$$
✓
Identify the game and its equilibrium.

This is a prisoner’s dilemma. Each player strictly prefers no effort, yet both would be better off at $(E,E)$ because $1-c>0$.

$$\boxed{NE=\{(P,P)\},\qquad (u_1,u_2)=(0,0).}$$

Strict dominance also rules out any equilibrium assigning positive probability to effort, so there are no additional mixed equilibria.

Exercise 4 · Question 2
Total output is 2 if both exert effort, and 0 otherwise. Give the normal form, identify the game, find all Nash equilibria, and explain how effort in the completely mixed equilibrium changes with $c$.
1
Write the new payoff matrix.

Someone who works alone now receives no output and pays $c$. A person who does not work receives zero regardless of the other person’s effort.

Player 1: rows · Player 2: columns
$P$$E$
$P$$(0,0)$$(0,-c)$
$E$$(-c,0)$$(1-c,\ 1-c)$

This is a coordination game (stag hunt): effort is worthwhile when the other person also works, but costly when undertaken alone.

2
Check singleton supports.

Against $P$, the unique best response is $P$ because $0>-c$. Against $E$, it is $E$ because $1-c>0$.

Thus the pure equilibria are $(P,P)$ and $(E,E)$. At either off-diagonal outcome, a player can improve by switching. A pure strategy by one player also forces a pure best response from the other, so no equilibrium has exactly one mixing player.

3
Check the full supports and impose indifference.

Let $p=\Pr_1(E)$ and $q=\Pr_2(E)$. If Player 1 mixes:

$$u_1(E,q)=q(1-c)+(1-q)(-c)=q-c,\qquad u_1(P,q)=0.$$ $$q-c=0\iff q^*=c.$$

If Player 2 mixes, the analogous condition is:

$$u_2(p,E)=p-c=0=u_2(p,P)\iff p^*=c.$$

Since $c\in(1/2,1)$, both probabilities are valid and both actions are used. There are no unused actions to check.

4
State every equilibrium and its payoffs.
$$\boxed{NE=\left\{(P,P),(E,E), \left((1-c)P+cE,\ (1-c)P+cE\right)\right\}.}$$

Payoffs are $(0,0)$ at $(P,P)$ and $(1-c,1-c)$ at $(E,E)$. At the mixed equilibrium, each player is indifferent to $P$, which pays zero, so both expected payoffs are $0$.

5
Explain why the equilibrium effort probability increases with cost.
$$\boxed{\Pr_1(E)=\Pr_2(E)=c\quad\text{in the completely mixed equilibrium}.}$$

Holding the other player’s behavior fixed, a higher cost makes effort less attractive. To preserve indifference at a mixed equilibrium, the other player must therefore exert effort more often. The condition $q-c=0$ requires $q$ to rise with $c$, and the same reasoning gives $p=c$.

The pure equilibria remain $(P,P)$ and $(E,E)$ throughout the interval $c\in(1/2,1)$.
