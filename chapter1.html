Chapter 1

Static Games with Perfect Information

Modelling simultaneous strategic interactions — and finding rational predictions for play.

A. Motivation & Core Intuition

We study static games: all players make their choices simultaneously and independently. We assume perfect information: each player knows the complete rules of the game — the players, all possible strategies, and the resulting payoffs.

The central question: If players are rational, what will they play?

In standard microeconomics, a decision-maker solves a single-agent optimisation problem: maximise $U(x)$ subject to a constraint. In a game, player $i$'s payoff depends not only on her own action $s_i$, but on the actions $s_{-i}$ chosen by everyone else. Because those choices are made simultaneously, player $i$ must anticipate what others will do, knowing they are reasoning the same way about her.

Definition & Notation

B. Formal Definition
Game in Normal Form $\Gamma = (N, S, g)$

A game in normal (or strategic) form is a triplet $\Gamma = (N, S, g)$ where:

$N$A finite set of players: $N = \{1, 2, \ldots, N\}$.
$S_i$The strategy set (or action set) of player $i$ — the set of all pure strategies available to player $i$.
$g_i : \prod_{j=1}^{N} S_j \to \mathbb{R}$The payoff function of player $i$ — maps each complete strategy profile to a real utility number.

Timing of play: Each player $i \in N$ independently and simultaneously selects a strategy $s_i \in S_i$. Once all actions are chosen, each player $i$ receives the payoff $g_i(s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_N)$.

Nature of payoffs: Payoffs represent ordinal or cardinal utility preferences. A higher number indicates a strictly preferred outcome. Payoffs do not need to represent monetary amounts.

C. Decoding the Notation

Game theory notation is very compact. Always distinguish sets from selected elements:

🧠 Core Distinction
Capital $S$ = Set of possibilities
$S_i$ = strategy set of player $i$
$S = \prod_j S_j$ = set of all strategy profiles
$S_{-i}$ = set of profiles for all players except $i$
Lowercase $s$ = Selected action
$s_i \in S_i$ = strategy chosen by player $i$
$s = (s_1,\ldots,s_N) \in S$ = chosen strategy profile
$s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$ = strategies chosen by everyone except $i$
$S = \prod_{j=1}^{N} S_j$Cartesian product of all players' action sets: the space of all possible joint outcomes.
$s = (s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_N)$One joint strategy profile (one action per player). Also written $s = (s_i, s_{-i})$.
$s_{-i} = (s_1,\ldots,s_{i-1},s_{i+1},\ldots,s_N)$Profile of all opponents' actions. Isolates player $i$'s strategic environment.
$g_i(s_1, \ldots, s_N) \equiv g_i(s_i, s_{-i})$Payoff received by player $i$ under profile $s$.
$g(s) = (g_1(s), g_2(s), \ldots, g_N(s))$Vector payoff function giving the payoff of every player at profile $s$.
Why $g_i(s_i, s_{-i})$ defines a game: Player $i$ controls only $s_i$. But her objective function $g_i(s_i, s_{-i})$ depends on $s_{-i}$, which is controlled by other autonomous decision-makers. This strategic externality is the defining mark of game theory.

Complement · First-Price Sealed-Bid Auction
A single indivisible object is auctioned among $N \geq 2$ bidders. Each bidder $i$ has a private valuation $v_i \geq 0$ and submits a sealed bid $b_i \geq 0$ simultaneously. The highest bidder wins and pays her own bid. In case of a tie among $M(b)$ highest bidders, each tied bidder receives the object with equal probability $1/M(b)$.

Key difference from second-price: In a first-price auction the winner pays her own bid, not the second-highest bid.

Classification of Normal Form Games
Coordination games: Players' objectives are aligned ($g_i = g_j$ or positively correlated). Examples: Location Game, Stag Hunt.
Conflict games: Players' interests are directly opposed. Constant-sum/zero-sum games ($g_1 + g_2 = \text{cst}$, e.g. Penalty Kick) and anti-coordination games (Hawk-Dove).
Mixed games: Combine coordination and conflict. Examples: Battle of the Sexes, Prisoner's Dilemma, Cournot Duopoly.
Section 2

Dominance in Pure Strategies

A. Opening Intuition

Before asking "What will a rational player play?", game theory asks a simpler and more fundamental question:

What would a rational player NEVER play?

If a player has a strategy that is always strictly worse than another — regardless of what rivals do — then no rational player will ever choose it. Eliminating such strategies reduces the game and simplifies our prediction.

§ 2.1

Strict Dominance

B. Formal Definitions
1. Strictly Dominated Strategy

In words: A strategy is strictly dominated if there exists another strategy that yields a strictly higher payoff against every possible combination of opponents' strategies.

A strategy $s_i \in S_i$ is strictly dominated if $\exists\, t_i \in S_i$ such that:

$\forall\; s_{-i} \in S_{-i}, \quad g_i(t_i,\, s_{-i}) \;>\; g_i(s_i,\, s_{-i})$

We say $s_i$ is strictly dominated by $t_i$, or $t_i$ strictly dominates $s_i$.

Decoding the notation: $s_i$ is the candidate strategy being tested. $t_i$ is the superior alternative. $s_{-i}$ ranges over all elements of $S_{-i}$. The strict inequality $>$ must hold for all $s_{-i}$.

🔍 Practical Matrix Check

Player 1 (rows): Compare rows using the first payoff in each cell. Row $s_1$ is strictly dominated by row $t_1$ if, in every column, the first payoff of $t_1$ is strictly greater than that of $s_1$.

Player 2 (columns): Compare columns using the second payoff in each cell. Column $s_2$ is strictly dominated by column $t_2$ if, in every row, the second payoff of $t_2$ is strictly greater than that of $s_2$.

Rationality axiom: A rational player will never play a strictly dominated strategy, because $t_i$ is guaranteed to yield a strictly higher payoff no matter what happens.
B. Formal Definitions
2. Strictly Dominant Strategy

In words: A strategy is strictly dominant if it is strictly better than every other strategy of player $i$, against all possible actions of the opponents.

A strategy $s_i \in S_i$ is strictly dominant if for every $t_i \in S_i$ with $t_i \neq s_i$:

$\forall\; s_{-i} \in S_{-i}, \quad g_i(s_i,\, s_{-i}) \;>\; g_i(t_i,\, s_{-i})$
Why at most one can exist: Suppose $s_i$ and $t_i$ were both strictly dominant ($s_i \neq t_i$). Then $s_i$ strictly dominates $t_i$, so $g_i(s_i, s_{-i}) > g_i(t_i, s_{-i})$ for all $s_{-i}$. But $t_i$ strictly dominant implies $g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) > g_i(s_i, s_{-i})$. Contradiction. Hence, a strictly dominant strategy is unique if it exists.

Definition — Strictly Dominant Strategy Equilibrium

A strategy profile $s^* = (s_1^*, s_2^*, \ldots, s_N^*)$ is an equilibrium in strictly dominant strategies if for every player $i \in N$, $s_i^*$ is a strictly dominant strategy.

Strategic robustness: When it exists, this equilibrium requires no assumption about what players believe others will do. Each player simply plays their dominant action. May not exist; when it does, it is unique.

§ 2.2

Iterated Elimination of Strictly Dominated Strategies (IESDS)

A. The Chain of Rationality

When no player has a strictly dominant strategy, we can still make progress if common knowledge of rationality holds:

  1. Player $i$ is rational $\implies$ Player $i$ will never play a strictly dominated strategy.
  2. Other players know player $i$ is rational $\implies$ they know $i$ will never play that strategy.
  3. They can eliminate it from consideration and examine the smaller residual game.
  4. In the residual game, a strategy that was previously NOT dominated may now become strictly dominated (because the eliminated strategy is no longer there to justify it).
  5. Repeat the process until no further strictly dominated strategies can be found.
B. Formal Procedure

Let $\Gamma^1 = \Gamma$ denote the initial game. At each step $k \geq 1$:

  • If no player has a strictly dominated strategy in $\Gamma^k$: stop.
  • Otherwise, choose a player $i$ and a strategy $s_i \in S_i^k$ that is strictly dominated in $\Gamma^k$. Define $\Gamma^{k+1}$ by setting $S_i^{k+1} = S_i^k \setminus \{s_i\}$ and keeping $S_j^{k+1} = S_j^k$ for all $j \neq i$.

Solvability by IESDS: If the process terminates with exactly one strategy per player ($|S_i^k| = 1$ for all $i$), the game is said to be solvable by IESDS. The remaining profile is the unique IESDS solution.

Proposition — Order Independence of Strict IESDS

For finite games and strict dominance:

  1. The set of surviving strategies is independent of the order of elimination. Whichever strictly dominated strategy is eliminated first, the procedure reaches the exact same final game.
  2. IESDS never eliminates any Nash equilibrium: $NE(\Gamma^k) = NE(\Gamma)$ for all $k$.
  3. If $\Gamma$ is solvable by IESDS, the surviving profile is the unique Nash equilibrium of $\Gamma$.

§ 2.3

Weak Dominance

A. Motivation

Many economic games have no strictly dominated strategies. Can we relax the strict inequality to a weak inequality ($\geq$), allowing for ties?

B. Formal Definitions
ConceptMathematical ConditionInterpretation
Strict Dominance $g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) > g_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \quad \forall\, s_{-i}$ $t_i$ is strictly better against every opponent profile. Zero doubt.
Weak Dominance $g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) \geq g_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \quad \forall\, s_{-i}$
with strict inequality for at least one $s_{-i}$		 $t_i$ is never worse than $s_i$, and strictly better against at least one profile.

A strategy $s_i$ is weakly dominant if for all $t_i \in S_i$: $g_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \geq g_i(t_i, s_{-i})$ for all $s_{-i}$.

A profile $s^*$ is a weakly dominant strategy equilibrium if every $s_i^*$ is weakly dominant.

⚠️ Critical warning: Iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies (IEWDS) is NOT order-independent!
Unlike strict IESDS, changing the order of elimination of weakly dominated strategies can eliminate valid Nash equilibria and produce different residual games.

1

2
Case 1 ($v_i > m$): Truthful bid wins.
With $b_i = v_i$: bidder $i$ wins (since $b_i > m$) and pays price $m$. Payoff: $g_i(v_i, b_{-i}) = v_i - m > 0$.
Alternative bid $b_i'$:
• If $b_i' > m$: she still wins and still pays $m \implies g_i(b_i', b_{-i}) = v_i - m$ (identical payoff).
• If $b_i' < m$: she loses $\implies g_i(b_i', b_{-i}) = 0 < v_i - m$ (strictly worse).
• If $b_i' = m$: she ties and earns $\frac{v_i - m}{M} \leq v_i - m$ (worse).
Hence: $g_i(v_i, b_{-i}) \geq g_i(b_i', b_{-i})$.
3
Case 2 ($v_i < m$): Truthful bid loses.
With $b_i = v_i$: bidder $i$ loses (since $b_i < m$). Payoff: $g_i(v_i, b_{-i}) = 0$.
Alternative bid $b_i'$:
• If $b_i' < m$: she still loses $\implies g_i(b_i', b_{-i}) = 0$ (identical payoff).
• If $b_i' > m$: she wins, but pays the second-highest bid $m > v_i$. Payoff is $v_i - m < 0$ (strictly worse!).
• If $b_i' = m$: she ties, expected payoff is $\frac{v_i - m}{M} \leq 0$ (worse).
Hence: $g_i(v_i, b_{-i}) \geq g_i(b_i', b_{-i})$.
4
Case 3 ($v_i = m$): Truthful bid ties.
With $b_i = v_i$: $g_i = \frac{v_i - m}{M} = 0$. Any alternative $b_i'$ either gives 0 (if losing) or gives $v_i - m = 0$ (if winning). Payoff is 0 in all subcases.

Furthermore, strict inequality holds whenever $m$ falls between $v_i$ and $b_i'$. Therefore, bidding one's true valuation $b_i = v_i$ is a weakly dominant strategy. $\blacksquare$

Economic Insight — Revenue Equivalence Theorem

Under standard assumptions (risk-neutral bidders, independent private values, same valuation distribution):

  • First-price auction: winner pays their own bid. Bidders bid below their true value.
  • Second-price auction: winner pays the second-highest bid. Bidding your true value is weakly dominant.

The bidding strategies are different, but these effects compensate each other, so:

$$\boxed{\mathbb{E}[\text{seller revenue in first price}] = \mathbb{E}[\text{seller revenue in second price}]}$$
⚠️ Important: It means same expected revenue, not necessarily the same revenue in every individual auction.

Example: With the same bidders, one particular first-price auction could generate €80 and the second-price auction €75, but on average over many auctions, revenues are the same under the theorem's assumptions.
Section 3

Nash Equilibrium in Pure Strategies

§ 3.1

Definitions & Best Response

A. Intuition First

A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile where nobody wants to change their own strategy ALONE.

The word alone is paramount: we evaluate only unilateral deviations. Each player holds the actions of all rivals fixed and asks: "Can I do strictly better by switching to another strategy?" If the answer is NO for every single player, the situation is strategically stable: a Nash equilibrium.

B. Formal Definition — Nash Equilibrium

A strategy profile $s^* = (s_1^*, s_2^*, \ldots, s_N^*) \in S$ is a Nash equilibrium if for every player $i \in N$ and for every alternative strategy $t_i \in S_i$:

$$g_i(s_i^*,\, s_{-i}^*) \;\geq\; g_i(t_i,\, s_{-i}^*)$$

Profitable deviation: A strategy $t_i \in S_i$ is a profitable unilateral deviation for player $i$ from profile $s$ if $g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) > g_i(s_i, s_{-i})$. A strategy profile is a Nash equilibrium if and only if no player has a profitable unilateral deviation.

C. Best Response

In words: A best response of player $i$ is a strategy that maximizes her payoff given the choices of the other players.

$$BR_i(s_{-i}) = \left\{ s_i \in S_i \;\mid\; g_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \geq g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) \quad \forall\, t_i \in S_i \right\}$$

Note: $BR_i(s_{-i})$ is a set. It can contain multiple strategies if there are ties.

$$s^* \text{ is a Nash equilibrium} \iff s_i^* \in BR_i(s_{-i}^*) \quad \forall\, i \in N$$
Mutual Best Response: A Nash equilibrium is a profile where every player is simultaneously best-responding to the strategies of all other players.
🔍 The Practical Matrix Underlining Method

For Player 1 (Row player): Go column by column. In each column, identify the maximum first payoff and underline it.

For Player 2 (Column player): Go row by row. In each row, identify the maximum second payoff and underline it.

Conclusion: Any cell where both payoffs are underlined is a Nash equilibrium.

D. Interpretations of Nash Equilibrium

Why should we expect players to end up at a Nash equilibrium? The lecturer identifies six interpretations:

1
Prescription. An external individual (mediator, social planner) proposes a strategy profile. If the proposed profile is a Nash equilibrium, no player has an incentive to deviate unilaterally — the prescription is self-enforcing.
2
Communication. Players agree in advance on a strategy profile. A Nash equilibrium is a sustainable agreement: once the agreement is reached, nobody wants to deviate alone, so no player has a reason to break the deal.
3
Introspection. Players reason strategically about the game and about each other's reasoning. The argument is particularly compelling when there is a unique Nash equilibrium — strategic logic converges to a single prediction.
4
Convention. A social convention can coordinate players on a Nash equilibrium. Lecturer example: countries coordinate on driving on the right or on the left — both are Nash equilibria of the driving coordination game, and history / convention selects one.
5
Learning. If players repeatedly encounter similar strategic situations, they may learn over time how others play. Adaptive learning processes can converge toward a profile where nobody gains from changing strategy unilaterally.
6
Evolution. If species (or populations) adapt to one another over time through selection pressure, the resulting ecosystem may be interpreted using Nash-equilibrium logic — organisms that are best-responding to their environment survive.

Exercise 3.2.F · Cournot Duopoly Nash Equilibrium
In the Cournot duopoly, inverse demand is $P(Q) = 13 - q_1 - q_2$ (for $q_1 + q_2 \leq 13$), and marginal cost is $c = 0$. Firm $i$'s profit is $g_i(q_i, q_j) = q_i(13 - q_i - q_j)$.
Task: Derive the reaction (best-response) function $BR_i(q_j)$ for each firm. Solve for the Nash equilibrium $(q_1^*, q_2^*)$, the equilibrium price $P^*$, and the profits $g_1^*, g_2^*$.
1
Firm 1's Optimisation problem:
Firm 1 chooses $q_1 \geq 0$ to maximise $g_1(q_1, q_2) = 13q_1 - q_1^2 - q_1 q_2$, taking $q_2$ as given.
First-Order Condition (FOC): $$\frac{\partial g_1}{\partial q_1} = 13 - 2q_1 - q_2 = 0 \implies 2q_1 = 13 - q_2 \implies BR_1(q_2) = \frac{13 - q_2}{2}$$ Second-Order Condition: $\frac{\partial^2 g_1}{\partial q_1^2} = -2 < 0$ (strict concavity, global maximum).
2
Firm 2's Optimisation problem:
By exact symmetry: $$BR_2(q_1) = \frac{13 - q_1}{2}$$
3
Equilibrium System:
At Nash equilibrium, each firm must be best-responding to the other: $$q_1^* = \frac{13 - q_2^*}{2} \quad \text{and} \quad q_2^* = \frac{13 - q_1^*}{2}$$ Substituting $q_2^*$ into $q_1^*$: $$q_1^* = \frac{13 - \frac{13 - q_1^*}{2}}{2} = \frac{26 - 13 + q_1^*}{4} = \frac{13 + q_1^*}{4} \implies 4q_1^* - q_1^* = 13 \implies 3q_1^* = 13 \implies q_1^* = \frac{13}{3}$$ By symmetry: $q_2^* = \frac{13}{3}$.
✓
Equilibrium Values:
• Total output: $Q^* = q_1^* + q_2^* = \frac{26}{3}$.
• Market price: $P^* = 13 - \frac{26}{3} = \frac{13}{3}$.
• Equilibrium profits: $g_1^* = g_2^* = P^* \cdot q_i^* = \left(\frac{13}{3}\right)\left(\frac{13}{3}\right) = \frac{169}{9} \approx 18.78$.
Graphic interpretation: The Nash equilibrium is the intersection point of the two downward-sloping reaction curves in the $(q_1, q_2)$ plane.

Proposition 1 — Strict Dominance & Nash Equilibrium

(1) If $s^*$ is an equilibrium in strictly dominant strategies, then $s^*$ is the unique Nash equilibrium of $\Gamma$.

(2) For any IESDS sequence $(\Gamma^k)$: $NE(\Gamma^k) = NE(\Gamma)$ for all $k$. IESDS never eliminates any Nash equilibrium.

(3) If $\Gamma$ is solvable by IESDS with surviving profile $\bar{s}$, then $\bar{s}$ is the unique Nash equilibrium of $\Gamma$.

1
Proof of (1) — Existence: Since $s_i^*$ is strictly dominant for all $i$, $g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}) > g_i(t_i, s_{-i})$ for all $t_i \neq s_i^*$ and all $s_{-i}$. In particular, at $s_{-i} = s_{-i}^*$, $g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) > g_i(t_i, s_{-i}^*)$ for all $t_i \neq s_i^*$. Thus $s^*$ satisfies the Nash equilibrium condition.
2
Proof of (1) — Uniqueness: Suppose $\hat{s} \neq s^*$ is also a Nash equilibrium. Then there exists at least one player $j$ such that $\hat{s}_j \neq s_j^*$. Since $s_j^*$ strictly dominates $\hat{s}_j$, we have $g_j(s_j^*, \hat{s}_{-j}) > g_j(\hat{s}_j, \hat{s}_{-j})$. But this means player $j$ can strictly improve her payoff by deviating to $s_j^*$ against $\hat{s}_{-j}$, contradicting the assumption that $\hat{s}$ is a Nash equilibrium. Thus, no other Nash equilibrium can exist. $\blacksquare$
3
Proof of (3): By (2), $NE(\Gamma) = NE(\Gamma^k)$. When the terminal game $\Gamma^k$ has only one strategy per player $\bar{s}$, the only possible profile in $\Gamma^k$ is $\bar{s}$, so $NE(\Gamma^k) = \{\bar{s}\}$. Hence $NE(\Gamma) = \{\bar{s}\}$. $\blacksquare$
Proposition 2 — Weak Dominance & Nash Equilibrium

(1) If $s^*$ is an equilibrium in weakly dominant strategies, then $s^*$ is a Nash equilibrium of $\Gamma$ (though not necessarily unique).

(2) Under iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies, $NE(\Gamma^{k+1}) \subseteq NE(\Gamma^k)$. The inclusion may be strict: weak elimination can remove valid Nash equilibria.

1
Let $s^*$ be a weakly dominant strategy equilibrium. Then for all $i \in N$, for all $t_i \in S_i$, and for all $s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$, $g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}) \geq g_i(t_i, s_{-i})$. Evaluating at $s_{-i} = s_{-i}^*$ gives $g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \geq g_i(t_i, s_{-i}^*)$ for all $t_i$, which is precisely the definition of a Nash equilibrium. $\blacksquare$
1
Setup: Let $\Gamma^{k+1}$ be obtained from $\Gamma^k$ by deleting a weakly dominated strategy $s_i$ of player $i$. By definition, there exists another strategy $t_i$ that remains available in $\Gamma^{k+1}$ such that: $$g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) \;\geq\; g_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \qquad \forall\, s_{-i} \in S_{-i}^k$$
2
Take any Nash equilibrium $s^*$ of the reduced game $\Gamma^{k+1}$.

For every player $j \neq i$: the strategy set of $j$ has not changed between $\Gamma^k$ and $\Gamma^{k+1}$, so player $j$ has no profitable deviation in $\Gamma^k$ either.

For player $i$: $s_i^*$ is a best response among all strategies remaining in $\Gamma^{k+1}$. In particular: $$g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \;\geq\; g_i(t_i, s_{-i}^*)$$
3
Show the deleted strategy cannot be a profitable deviation in $\Gamma^k$:

Since $t_i$ weakly dominates the deleted strategy $s_i$ in $\Gamma^k$: $$g_i(t_i, s_{-i}^*) \;\geq\; g_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*)$$ Combining both inequalities: $$g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \;\geq\; g_i(t_i, s_{-i}^*) \;\geq\; g_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*)$$ Therefore $s_i$ (the deleted strategy) is not a profitable deviation from $s^*$ in $\Gamma^k$.
4
Conclusion:
Every player has no profitable deviation from $s^*$ in $\Gamma^k$. Thus $s^*$ is a Nash equilibrium of $\Gamma^k$, proving: $$NE(\Gamma^{k+1}) \;\subseteq\; NE(\Gamma^k)$$ The inclusion may be strict: eliminating a weakly dominated strategy can delete a Nash equilibrium in which that weakly dominated strategy was played. $\blacksquare$

The 3-Step Exam Algorithm
1
Step 1: Check for Strictly Dominant Strategies

Does any player have a strategy that beats all their other strategies against every opponent profile? If every player has one $\implies$ that profile is the unique strictly dominant strategy equilibrium and the unique Nash equilibrium. Stop.

On the remaining matrix: underline Player 1's maximal first payoff(s) in each column, and Player 2's maximal second payoff(s) in each row. Any cell where both payoffs are underlined is a pure Nash equilibrium. If no cell has both underlined $\implies$ no pure strategy Nash equilibrium exists.
