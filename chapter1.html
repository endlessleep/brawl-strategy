Chapter 1

Static Games with Perfect Information

Modelling simultaneous strategic interactions — and finding rational predictions for play.

01 — Normal Form Games & Dominance
A. Motivation & Core Intuition

We study static games: all players make their choices simultaneously and independently. We assume complete information about the game: each player knows the rules of the game — the players, all possible strategies, and the resulting payoffs.

The central question: If players are rational, what will they play?

In standard microeconomics, a decision-maker solves a single-agent optimisation problem: maximise $U(x)$ subject to a constraint. In a game, player $i$'s payoff depends not only on her own action $s_i$, but on the actions $s_{-i}$ chosen by everyone else. Because those choices are made simultaneously, player $i$ must anticipate what others will do, knowing they are reasoning the same way about her.

Normal Form Games

§ 1.1

Definition & Notation

B. Formal Definition
Game in Normal Form $\Gamma = (N, S, g)$

A game in normal (or strategic) form is a triplet $\Gamma = (N, S, g)$ where:

$N$A finite set of players: $N = \{1, 2, \ldots, N\}$.
$S_i$The strategy set (or action set) of player $i$ — the set of all pure strategies available to player $i$.
$g_i : \prod_{j=1}^{N} S_j \to \mathbb{R}$The payoff function of player $i$ — maps each complete strategy profile to a real utility number.

Timing of play: Each player $i \in N$ independently and simultaneously selects a strategy $s_i \in S_i$. Once all actions are chosen, each player $i$ receives the payoff $g_i(s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_N)$.

Nature of payoffs: Payoffs represent ordinal or cardinal utility preferences. A higher number indicates a strictly preferred outcome. Payoffs do not need to represent monetary amounts.

C. Decoding the Notation

Game theory notation is very compact. Always distinguish sets from selected elements:

🧠 Core Distinction
Capital $S$ = Set of possibilities
$S_i$ = strategy set of player $i$
$S = \prod_j S_j$ = set of all strategy profiles
$S_{-i}$ = set of profiles for all players except $i$
Lowercase $s$ = Selected action
$s_i \in S_i$ = strategy chosen by player $i$
$s = (s_1,\ldots,s_N) \in S$ = chosen strategy profile
$s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$ = strategies chosen by everyone except $i$
$S = \prod_{j=1}^{N} S_j$Cartesian product of all players' action sets: the space of all possible joint outcomes.
$s = (s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_N)$One joint strategy profile (one action per player). Also written $s = (s_i, s_{-i})$.
$s_{-i} = (s_1,\ldots,s_{i-1},s_{i+1},\ldots,s_N)$Profile of all opponents' actions. Isolates player $i$'s strategic environment.
$g_i(s_1, \ldots, s_N) \equiv g_i(s_i, s_{-i})$Payoff received by player $i$ under profile $s$.
$g(s) = (g_1(s), g_2(s), \ldots, g_N(s))$Vector payoff function giving the payoff of every player at profile $s$.
Why $g_i(s_i, s_{-i})$ defines a game: Player $i$ controls only $s_i$. But her objective function $g_i(s_i, s_{-i})$ depends on $s_{-i}$, which is controlled by other autonomous decision-makers. This strategic externality is the defining mark of game theory.

Example 7 · Cournot Duopoly
Two firms (1 and 2) produce a homogeneous good with quantities $q_1 \geq 0$ and $q_2 \geq 0$. Total market output is $Q = q_1 + q_2$. Inverse market demand is given by: $$P(Q) = \max(13 - Q,\; 0) = \max(13 - q_1 - q_2,\; 0)$$ Both firms face the same constant marginal cost $c \geq 0$, with no fixed cost.
Task: Formalize this economic interaction as a normal-form game $\Gamma = (N, S, g)$. Specify players, strategy sets, derive each firm's payoff function $g_i(q_i, q_j)$, and explain why the other firm's quantity enters firm $i$'s payoff.
1
Players and strategies. $$N=\{1,2\},\qquad S_1=S_2=\mathbb R_+.$$

Each firm chooses a nonnegative quantity $q_i$. The strategy sets are continuous, so a finite payoff matrix cannot represent the whole game.

2
Profit = revenue − cost.

Write $q_j$ for the other firm’s quantity. Total output is $Q=q_i+q_j$:

$$\begin{aligned}g_i(q_i,q_j)&=P(q_i+q_j)q_i-cq_i,\\[3pt]&=q_i\left[\max\{13-q_i-q_j,0\}-c\right].\end{aligned}$$
3
Why the rival’s quantity matters.

Hold $q_i$ fixed. While the price is positive, an increase in $q_j$ lowers the price and therefore firm $i$’s revenue. Once the price reaches zero, further output cannot lower it. This is why $g_i$ depends on both quantities.

Complement · First-Price Sealed-Bid Auction
A single indivisible object is auctioned among $N \geq 2$ bidders. Each bidder $i$ has a private valuation $v_i \geq 0$ and submits a sealed bid $b_i \geq 0$ simultaneously. The highest bidder wins and pays her own bid. In case of a tie among $M(b)$ highest bidders, each tied bidder receives the object with equal probability $1/M(b)$.

Key difference from second-price: In a first-price auction the winner pays her own bid, not the second-highest bid.
Task: Formalize as a normal-form game $\Gamma = (N, S, g)$. Define the piecewise payoff function $g_i(b)$.
1
Players: $N = \{1, 2, \ldots, N\}$.
2
Strategy sets: $S_i = \mathbb{R}_+$ for every $i \in N$ (the chosen bid is $b_i\geq0$).
3
Notation:
  • $v_i$: private valuation of bidder $i$.
  • $b_i$: bid submitted by bidder $i$.
  • $m_i(b) = \max_{j \neq i} b_j$: highest competing bid.
  • $M(b) = |\{j \in N : b_j = \max_k b_k\}|$: number of bidders tied for the highest bid.
4
Payoff cases:
  • Case 1 ($b_i > m_i(b)$): Bidder $i$ is the sole highest bidder. She wins and pays her own bid $b_i$. Payoff: $g_i = v_i - b_i$.
  • Case 2 ($b_i < m_i(b)$): Bidder $i$ loses. She receives nothing and pays nothing: $g_i = 0$.
  • Case 3 ($b_i = m_i(b)$): Tie at the top. Bidder $i$ wins with probability $\frac{1}{M(b)}$ and pays $b_i$. Expected payoff: $\frac{v_i - b_i}{M(b)}$.
5
Piecewise payoff function:
$$g_i(b) = \begin{cases} v_i - b_i & \text{if } b_i > m_i(b) \\[8pt] \dfrac{v_i - b_i}{M(b)} & \text{if } b_i = m_i(b) \\[8pt] 0 & \text{if } b_i < m_i(b) \end{cases}$$

Classification of Normal Form Games
Coordination games: Players' objectives are aligned ($g_i = g_j$ or positively correlated). Examples: Location Game, Stag Hunt.
Conflict games: Players' interests are directly opposed. Constant-sum/zero-sum games ($g_1 + g_2 = \text{cst}$, e.g. Penalty Kick) and anti-coordination games (Hawk-Dove).
Mixed games: Combine coordination and conflict. Examples: Battle of the Sexes, Prisoner's Dilemma, Cournot Duopoly.
Section 2

Dominance in Pure Strategies

A. Opening Intuition

Before asking "What will a rational player play?", game theory asks a simpler and more fundamental question:

What would a rational player NEVER play?

If a player has a strategy that is always strictly worse than another — regardless of what rivals do — then no rational player will ever choose it. Eliminating such strategies reduces the game and simplifies our prediction.

§ 2.1

Strict Dominance

B. Formal Definitions
1. Strictly Dominated Strategy

In words: A strategy is strictly dominated if there exists another strategy that yields a strictly higher payoff against every possible combination of opponents' strategies.

A strategy $s_i \in S_i$ is strictly dominated if $\exists\, t_i \in S_i$ such that:

$\forall\; s_{-i} \in S_{-i}, \quad g_i(t_i,\, s_{-i}) \;>\; g_i(s_i,\, s_{-i})$

We say $s_i$ is strictly dominated by $t_i$, or $t_i$ strictly dominates $s_i$.

Decoding the notation: $s_i$ is the candidate strategy being tested. $t_i$ is the superior alternative. $s_{-i}$ ranges over all elements of $S_{-i}$. The strict inequality $>$ must hold for all $s_{-i}$.

🔍 Practical Matrix Check

Player 1 (rows): Compare rows using the first payoff in each cell. Row $s_1$ is strictly dominated by row $t_1$ if, in every column, the first payoff of $t_1$ is strictly greater than that of $s_1$.

Player 2 (columns): Compare columns using the second payoff in each cell. Column $s_2$ is strictly dominated by column $t_2$ if, in every row, the second payoff of $t_2$ is strictly greater than that of $s_2$.

Rationality axiom: A rational player will never play a strictly dominated strategy, because $t_i$ is guaranteed to yield a strictly higher payoff no matter what happens.
B. Formal Definitions
2. Strictly Dominant Strategy

In words: A strategy is strictly dominant if it is strictly better than every other strategy of player $i$, against all possible actions of the opponents.

A strategy $s_i \in S_i$ is strictly dominant if for every $t_i \in S_i$ with $t_i \neq s_i$:

$\forall\; s_{-i} \in S_{-i}, \quad g_i(s_i,\, s_{-i}) \;>\; g_i(t_i,\, s_{-i})$
Why at most one can exist: Suppose $s_i$ and $t_i$ were both strictly dominant ($s_i \neq t_i$). Then $s_i$ strictly dominates $t_i$, so $g_i(s_i, s_{-i}) > g_i(t_i, s_{-i})$ for all $s_{-i}$. But $t_i$ strictly dominant implies $g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) > g_i(s_i, s_{-i})$. Contradiction. Hence, a strictly dominant strategy is unique if it exists.

Definition — Strictly Dominant Strategy Equilibrium

A strategy profile $s^* = (s_1^*, s_2^*, \ldots, s_N^*)$ is an equilibrium in strictly dominant strategies if for every player $i \in N$, $s_i^*$ is a strictly dominant strategy.

Strategic robustness: When it exists, this equilibrium requires no assumption about what players believe others will do. Each player simply plays their dominant action. May not exist; when it does, it is unique.

§ 2.2

Iterated Elimination of Strictly Dominated Strategies (IESDS)

A. The Chain of Rationality

When no player has a strictly dominant strategy, we can still make progress if common knowledge of rationality holds:

  1. Player $i$ is rational $\implies$ Player $i$ will never play a strictly dominated strategy.
  2. Other players know player $i$ is rational $\implies$ they know $i$ will never play that strategy.
  3. They can eliminate it from consideration and examine the smaller residual game.
  4. In the residual game, a strategy that was previously NOT dominated may now become strictly dominated (because the eliminated strategy is no longer there to justify it).
  5. Repeat the process until no further strictly dominated strategies can be found.
B. Formal Procedure

Let $\Gamma^1 = \Gamma$ denote the initial game. At each step $k \geq 1$:

  • If no player has a strictly dominated strategy in $\Gamma^k$: stop.
  • Otherwise, choose a player $i$ and a strategy $s_i \in S_i^k$ that is strictly dominated in $\Gamma^k$. Define $\Gamma^{k+1}$ by setting $S_i^{k+1} = S_i^k \setminus \{s_i\}$ and keeping $S_j^{k+1} = S_j^k$ for all $j \neq i$.

Solvability by IESDS: If the process terminates with exactly one strategy per player ($|S_i^k| = 1$ for all $i$), the game is said to be solvable by IESDS. The remaining profile is the unique IESDS solution.

Proposition — Order Independence of Strict IESDS

For finite games and strict dominance:

  1. The set of surviving strategies is independent of the order of elimination. Whichever strictly dominated strategy is eliminated first, the procedure reaches the exact same final game.
  2. IESDS never eliminates any Nash equilibrium: $NE(\Gamma^k) = NE(\Gamma)$ for all $k$.
  3. If $\Gamma$ is solvable by IESDS, the surviving profile is the unique Nash equilibrium of $\Gamma$.
Exercise 2.2.A · Chain of Rationality
Consider the following game:
Player 2
$S$$T$
P1$S$$(0,-2)$$(-10,-1)$

§ 2.3

Weak Dominance

A. Motivation

Many economic games have no strictly dominated strategies. Can we relax the strict inequality to a weak inequality ($\geq$), allowing for ties?

B. Formal Definitions
ConceptMathematical ConditionInterpretation
Strict Dominance $g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) > g_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \quad \forall\, s_{-i}$ $t_i$ is strictly better against every opponent profile. Zero doubt.
Weak Dominance $g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) \geq g_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \quad \forall\, s_{-i}$
with strict inequality for at least one $s_{-i}$		 $t_i$ is never worse than $s_i$, and strictly better against at least one profile.

A strategy $s_i$ is weakly dominant if for all $t_i \in S_i$: $g_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \geq g_i(t_i, s_{-i})$ for all $s_{-i}$.

A profile $s^*$ is a weakly dominant strategy equilibrium if every $s_i^*$ is weakly dominant.

⚠️ Critical warning: Iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies (IEWDS) is NOT order-independent!
Unlike strict IESDS, changing the order of elimination of weakly dominated strategies can eliminate valid Nash equilibria and produce different residual games.
Exercise 2.3.A · Weak vs. Strict Dominance
Consider the following game:
Player 2
$L$$R$
P1$U$$(2,2)$$(4,4)$
$D$$(3,1)$$(4,1)$
Task: In the following game, are there strictly dominant strategies? In your opinion, what would be a smart choice?
1
Player 1: compare $D$ with $U$. $$\begin{aligned}g_1(D,L)=3&>2=g_1(U,L),\\[3pt]g_1(D,R)=4&=4=g_1(U,R).\end{aligned}$$

$D$ weakly dominates $U$, but not strictly: the payoffs are equal against $R$. Thus Player 1 has no strictly dominant strategy.

2
Player 2: compare $R$ with $L$. $$\begin{aligned}g_2(U,R)=4&>2=g_2(U,L),\\[3pt]g_2(D,R)=1&=1=g_2(D,L).\end{aligned}$$

$R$ weakly dominates $L$, but not strictly. Player 2 also has no strictly dominant strategy.

✓
Choice justified by dominance.

$D$ for Player 1 and $R$ for Player 2 are never worse than their alternatives and are sometimes better. These are weakly dominant choices. The equalities matter: $U$ is still a best response to $R$, and $L$ is still a best response to $D$.

Exercise 2.3.B · Order-Dependence in IEWDS
Consider the following game:
Player 2
$L$$R$
P1$U$$(1,1)$$(0,0)$
$M$$(1,1)$$(2,1)$
$D$$(0,0)$$(2,1)$
Task: Apply a procedure of iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies to the following game.
1
First order: delete $U$, then $L$.

For Player 1:

$$\begin{aligned}g_1(M,L)=1&=1=g_1(U,L),\\[3pt]g_1(M,R)=2&>0=g_1(U,R).\end{aligned}$$

Delete $U$, weakly dominated by $M$. In $\{M,D\}\times\{L,R\}$, compare Player 2’s columns:

$$\begin{aligned}g_2(M,R)=1&=1=g_2(M,L),\\[3pt]g_2(D,R)=1&>0=g_2(D,L).\end{aligned}$$

Delete $L$, weakly dominated by $R$. In the remaining column, $g_1(M,R)=g_1(D,R)=2$, so neither row weakly dominates the other with a strict improvement.

$$\boxed{\text{Surviving profiles: }\{(M,R),(D,R)\}.}$$
2
Second order: delete $D$, then $R$.

For Player 1:

$$\begin{aligned}g_1(M,L)=1&>0=g_1(D,L),\\[3pt]g_1(M,R)=2&=2=g_1(D,R).\end{aligned}$$

Delete $D$, weakly dominated by $M$. In $\{U,M\}\times\{L,R\}$:

$$\begin{aligned}g_2(U,L)=1&>0=g_2(U,R),\\[3pt]g_2(M,L)=1&=1=g_2(M,R).\end{aligned}$$

Delete $R$, weakly dominated by $L$. The two remaining rows both give Player 1 payoff $1$, so the procedure stops.

$$\boxed{\text{Surviving profiles: }\{(U,L),(M,L)\}.}$$
✓
Compare the results.

The surviving profiles differ, so IEWDS depends on the elimination order. All four profiles above are Nash equilibria of the original game; each order removes some of them.

Exercise 2.3.C · Second-Price Auction Proof
In a second-price sealed-bid auction with $N \geq 2$ bidders, bidder $i$ has private valuation $v_i \geq 0$ and submits bid $b_i \geq 0$.
Task: Prove that in a second price auction, playing one's true value is a weakly dominant strategy.
1
Fix the other bids.

Fix bidder $i$ and the rival bids $b_{-i}$. Write $m=\max_{j\neq i}b_j$. Compare the truthful bid $v_i$ with any other bid $b_i\neq v_i$. If $b_i=m$, let $M\geq2$ be the number of tied highest bidders.

2
Case $v_i>m$.

Truthful bidding wins and gives $g_i(v_i,b_{-i})=v_i-m>0$. For another bid:

$$g_i(b_i,b_{-i})=\begin{cases}v_i-m,&b_i>m,\\(v_i-m)/M,&b_i=m,\\0,&b_i<m.\end{cases}$$

Each value is at most $v_i-m$. Truthful bidding is at least as good.

3
Case $v_i<m$.

Truthful bidding loses and gives $g_i(v_i,b_{-i})=0$. A bid below $m$ also gives zero. A bid above $m$ gives $v_i-m<0$; a tie gives $(v_i-m)/M<0$. No alternative gives more than zero.

4
Case $v_i=m$.

Winning, losing or tying all give payoff zero. Thus every bid gives the same payoff as truthful bidding.

✓
Conclude weak dominance. $$g_i(v_i,b_{-i})\geq g_i(b_i,b_{-i})\qquad\forall b_i,\ \forall b_{-i}.$$

For each $b_i\neq v_i$, choose the highest rival bid $m$ strictly between $b_i$ and $v_i$. The truthful bid then gives strictly more: it either wins with positive surplus when the other bid loses, or loses when the other bid wins with negative surplus. Therefore $b_i=v_i$ is weakly dominant.

§ 2.3 — Result at Profile Level · Second-Price Auction
The proof above establishes truthful bidding as a weakly dominant strategy for each individual bidder. We now state the result at the profile level.
1
Individual level:
For every bidder $i$, the strategy $b_i = v_i$ is a dominant strategy in the terminology of §2.3 (i.e. weakly dominant): $$g_i(v_i, b_{-i}) \;\geq\; g_i(b_i', b_{-i}) \qquad \forall\, b_i' \in S_i,\; \forall\, b_{-i} \in S_{-i}$$
2
Profile level:
Since every bidder $i$ has a dominant strategy $b_i^* = v_i$, the strategy profile $$b^* = (v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_N)$$ is a dominant-strategy equilibrium.
3
Conceptual distinction:
LevelStatement
Individual$b_i = v_i$ is a weakly dominant strategy for bidder $i$, regardless of what others bid.
Profile$b^* = (v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_N)$ is a dominant-strategy equilibrium: every player simultaneously uses her dominant strategy.
✓
Key takeaway: The dominant-strategy equilibrium exists and is truthful bidding by all players. By Proposition 2(1) in §3.3, every dominant-strategy equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium — so $b^* = (v_1, \ldots, v_N)$ is also a Nash equilibrium of the auction game. $\blacksquare$
Economic Insight — Revenue Equivalence Theorem

Under standard assumptions (risk-neutral bidders, independent private values, same valuation distribution):

  • First-price auction: the winner pays their own bid. Bid shading may arise in equilibrium; it is not a dominant bidding rule.
  • Second-price auction: winner pays the second-highest bid. Bidding your true value is weakly dominant.

The bidding strategies are different, but these effects compensate each other, so:

$$\boxed{\mathbb{E}[\text{seller revenue in first price}] = \mathbb{E}[\text{seller revenue in second price}]}$$
⚠️ Important: It means same expected revenue, not necessarily the same revenue in every individual auction.

Example: With the same bidders, one particular first-price auction could generate €80 and the second-price auction €75, but on average over many auctions, revenues are the same under the theorem's assumptions.
