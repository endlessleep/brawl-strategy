Section 6

Nash Equilibrium in Mixed Strategies

A. When Is Randomizing Optimal?

Suppose that, against the opponent’s strategy, $L$ gives an expected payoff of $6$ and $R$ gives $4$. Choosing $R$ some of the time lowers the average payoff: playing $L$ with certainty is better.

To use both actions at equilibrium, the player must therefore get the same expected payoff from them. This is the indifference condition used to find mixing probabilities.

§ 6.1

Definitions and the Indifference Principle

Definition — No Profitable Unilateral Deviation

A mixed profile $\sigma\in\Sigma$ is a Nash equilibrium if, for every player $i$, her chosen distribution performs at least as well as every alternative distribution against $\sigma_{-i}$:

$$\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,N\},\quad\forall\tau_i\in\Sigma_i,\qquad g_i(\sigma_i,\sigma_{-i})\geq g_i(\tau_i,\sigma_{-i})$$

Compare the current strategy $\sigma_i$ with any alternative $\tau_i$, keeping the opponents’ strategies $\sigma_{-i}$ fixed. The alternative may use different probabilities, including probability $1$ on a single action.

The comparison is ex ante: it uses expected payoffs before actions are drawn. Losing a particular play does not imply that another strategy would have given a better expected payoff.

Proposition — Checking Pure Deviations Is Sufficient

It is enough to check deviations to pure actions:

$$\forall i,\quad\forall t_i\in S_i,\qquad g_i(\sigma_i,\sigma_{-i})\geq g_i(t_i,\sigma_{-i})$$

A mixed deviation gives a weighted average of pure-action payoffs. That average cannot exceed the largest of those payoffs. If no pure action improves on the proposed strategy, no mixture can improve on it either.

Best Responses — The Same Equilibrium Condition

The best-response set $BR_i(\sigma_{-i})$ contains all mixed strategies that maximize player $i$’s expected payoff against the opponents’ given profile.

$$BR_i(\sigma_{-i})=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\tau_i\in\Sigma_i}g_i(\tau_i,\sigma_{-i})$$
$$\sigma\text{ is a Nash equilibrium}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sigma_i\in BR_i(\sigma_{-i})\ \text{for every }i$$

As in pure strategies, an equilibrium is a profile of mutual best responses. Here a best response may itself be a distribution.

Proposition — The Strong Indifference Principle

A profile $\sigma$ is a Nash equilibrium if and only if both conditions below hold for every player.

i
All actions used with positive probability give the same payoff.
$$\forall s_i,t_i\in\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_i),\qquad g_i(s_i,\sigma_{-i})=g_i(t_i,\sigma_{-i})$$
Evaluate these payoffs against the same opponents’ distributions. If one used action paid less, moving its probability to a better action would raise the player’s expected payoff.
ii
An unused action cannot give a higher payoff.
$$\begin{gathered}\forall s_i\in\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_i),\quad\forall t_i\notin\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_i),\\g_i(t_i,\sigma_{-i})\leq g_i(s_i,\sigma_{-i}).\end{gathered}$$
For example, if the used actions $L$ and $M$ each pay $2$, but unused $R$ pays $3$, the player would switch to $R$. Equal payoffs within the support are not enough. An unused action may tie with them, but cannot pay more.
Proof Exercise · Strong Indifference Principle
Task: Prove both directions: an equilibrium satisfies conditions (i)–(ii), and those conditions rule out every profitable deviation.
1
Necessity: a used action cannot be worse than another action.
Assume that $\sigma$ is an equilibrium. Suppose a player uses $s_i$ with $\sigma_i(s_i)>0$, but some action $t_i$ gives a strictly higher payoff against $\sigma_{-i}$. Move the probability on $s_i$ to $t_i$, leaving all other probabilities unchanged. The expected-payoff increase is $$\sigma_i(s_i)\bigl[g_i(t_i,\sigma_{-i})-g_i(s_i,\sigma_{-i})\bigr]>0.$$ This would be a profitable unilateral deviation, contradicting equilibrium. Hence each used action must give a maximal payoff. All used actions therefore tie, and no unused action can give more. These are conditions (i) and (ii).
2
Sufficiency: any deviation is an average of payoffs no larger than the common payoff.
Now assume (i) and (ii). For player $i$, denote the common payoff of the supported actions by $v_i$. Because all probability is assigned to these actions, $$g_i(\sigma_i,\sigma_{-i})=\sum_{s_i\in\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_i)}\sigma_i(s_i)v_i=v_i.$$ Every pure action gives at most $v_i$: the supported ones give exactly $v_i$, and (ii) bounds the others. For any mixed deviation $\tau_i$, $$g_i(\tau_i,\sigma_{-i})=\sum_{t_i\in S_i}\tau_i(t_i)g_i(t_i,\sigma_{-i})\leq\sum_{t_i\in S_i}\tau_i(t_i)v_i=v_i.$$ Thus no player can profitably deviate. Since this argument holds for every player, $\sigma$ is a Nash equilibrium.
Method — Search over Supports
1
Choose which actions each player uses.
These actions form the proposed support. For example, $\{L,R\}$ means positive probabilities on $L$ and $R$, and zero on every other action. Include supports with just one action: these represent pure strategies.
2
Find probabilities that make the used actions equally good.
For each player who mixes, equate the expected payoffs of the actions in her support. Keep only solutions with positive probabilities on that support and total probability $1$.
3
Test whether an unused action is better.
Using the probabilities just found, calculate the payoff of every action outside the support. If any pays more than the used actions, reject the candidate.

Repeat for every possible combination of supports.

Why the equations solve for the other player’s probability: in a $2\times2$ game, Player 1’s payoff from a fixed row depends on Player 2’s column probability $q$. Equating row payoffs therefore determines $q$. Equating Player 2’s column payoffs determines Player 1’s row probability $p$.
Examples

Equilibria, Payoffs and Best-Response Graphs

In each example, $p$ is Player 1’s probability of the first row and $q$ is Player 2’s probability of the first column. The graphs place $p$ on the horizontal axis and $q$ on the vertical axis. Intersections are Nash equilibria: both players are best responding there. When two actions tie, every mixture of them is a best response, producing a line segment on the graph.

Example 6.1.A · Coordination
Player 1: rows · Player 2: columns · Payoffs: (player 1, player 2)
$L$$C$
$L$$(1,1)$$(0,0)$
$C$$(0,0)$$(1,1)$
Task: Find all Nash equilibria, calculate their payoffs, and plot both best-response correspondences.
1
Define the probabilities and test pure supports of Player 2.
$\sigma_1=pL+(1-p)C$; $\sigma_2=qL+(1-q)C$. Here $p=P_1(L)$, $q=P_2(L)$.

$\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{L\}$: $BR_1(L)=\{L\}$ and $BR_2(L)=\{L\}$, giving $(L,L)$ or $(p,q)=(1,1)$.
$\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{C\}$: $BR_1(C)=\{C\}$ and $BR_2(C)=\{C\}$, giving $(C,C)$ or $(p,q)=(0,0)$.
2
Mixed support: $\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{L,C\}$.
Since $0<q<1$, Player 2 must be indifferent between L and C. Using the second payoffs: $$g_2(\sigma_1,L)=g_2(\sigma_1,C)\iff p(1)+(1-p)(0)=p(0)+(1-p)(1)$$ $$p=1-p\iff\boxed{p=\tfrac12\in(0,1).}$$ Because Player 1 then plays both actions, we also need Player 1 indifferent. Using the first payoffs: $$g_1(L,\sigma_2)=g_1(C,\sigma_2)\iff q(1)+(1-q)(0)=q(0)+(1-q)(1)$$ $$q=1-q\iff\boxed{q=\tfrac12\in(0,1).}$$ Both proposed supports contain both available actions. There are no unused actions to check for this candidate.
3
All Nash equilibria (probabilities).
There are two pure equilibria and one completely mixed equilibrium. At the pure profiles, deviating gives $0$ instead of $1$. At the completely mixed profile, all actions are in the support. Thus no unused action yields a profitable deviation: $$\boxed{NE=\{(1,1),(0,0),(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\}.}$$ The first two pairs correspond to $(L,L)$ and $(C,C)$, while $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ represents $\sigma_1=\sigma_2=\tfrac12L+\tfrac12C$.
4
NE payoffs — separate from the probabilities.
For pure equilibria, both payoff pairs are $(1,1)$. At the mixed NE, either supported pure action gives the same expected payoff: $$g_1(L,\sigma_2)=\tfrac12(1)+\tfrac12(0)=\tfrac12,$$ $$g_2(\sigma_1,L)=\tfrac12(1)+\tfrac12(0)=\tfrac12.$$ $$\boxed{\text{NE payoffs: }(1,1),\ (1,1),\ (\tfrac12,\tfrac12).}$$
5
Best-response functions and plot.
$$BR_1(q)=\begin{cases}\{0\}&q<\tfrac12,\\\left[0,1\right]&q=\tfrac12,\\\{1\}&q>\tfrac12,\end{cases}\qquad BR_2(p)=\begin{cases}\{0\}&p<\tfrac12,\\\left[0,1\right]&p=\tfrac12,\\\{1\}&p>\tfrac12.\end{cases}$$ The graph's intersections are precisely the three NE probability pairs.

Pure supports of Player 2.
Set $\sigma_1=pF+(1-p)O$ and $\sigma_2=qF+(1-q)O$. If Player 2 chooses only F, both choose F: $(F,F)$, so $(p,q)=(1,1)$. If Player 2 chooses only O, both choose O: $(O,O)$, so $(p,q)=(0,0)$.
2
Mixed support: $\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{F,O\}$.
Since $0<q<1$, make Player 2 indifferent using the second payoffs: $$g_2(\sigma_1,F)=g_2(\sigma_1,O)\iff p\cdot1+(1-p)\cdot0=p\cdot0+(1-p)\cdot2$$ $$p=2(1-p)\iff\boxed{p=\tfrac23\in(0,1).}$$ Now Player 1 must also be indifferent. At each pure equilibrium, changing action gives $0$ instead of a strictly positive payoff, so the unused actions are not profitable. Example 6.1.C · A Game with No Pure Equilibrium
Player 1: rows · Player 2: columns · Payoffs: (player 1, player 2)
$T$$C$
$W$$(1,3)$$(1,2)$
$S$$(2,-2)$$(0,-1)$
Task: Find all Nash equilibria, their payoffs, and the best-response graphs.
1
Check pure supports: none gives a Nash equilibrium.
Player 1: $BR_1(T)=\{S\}$ and $BR_1(C)=\{W\}$. Player 2: $BR_2(W)=\{T\}$ and $BR_2(S)=\{C\}$. No profile is a pair of mutual best responses, so $NE_{\mathrm{pure}}=\varnothing$.
2
Mixed support: $\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{T,C\}$.
Let $\sigma_1=pW+(1-p)S$ and $\sigma_2=qT+(1-q)C$, with $0<q<1$. First make Player 2 indifferent using her second payoffs: $$g_2(\sigma_1,T)=g_2(\sigma_1,C)$$ $$3p-2(1-p)=2p-(1-p)$$ $$5p-2=3p-1\iff2p=1\iff\boxed{p=\tfrac12\in(0,1).}$$ Because Player 1 also mixes, she must be indifferent using the first payoffs: $$g_1(W,\sigma_2)=g_1(S,\sigma_2)$$ $$q\cdot1+(1-q)\cdot1=2q+0(1-q)$$ $$1=2q\iff\boxed{q=\tfrac12\in(0,1).}$$ Both probabilities are admissible and both supports are complete. There are no unused actions to check.
3
Write the unique equilibrium and its payoffs.
$$\boxed{NE=\{(p,q)=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\}.}$$ The equilibrium strategies are $\sigma_1=\tfrac12W+\tfrac12S$, $\sigma_2=\tfrac12T+\tfrac12C$. For Player 1, $g_1(W,\sigma_2)=1$; for Player 2, $g_2(\sigma_1,T)=3/2-1=1/2$. Therefore: $$\boxed{\text{NE payoffs: }(1,\tfrac12).}$$
4
Best-response functions and plot.
$$BR_1(q)=\begin{cases}\{1\}&q<\tfrac12,\\\left[0,1\right]&q=\tfrac12,\\\{0\}&q>\tfrac12,\end{cases}\qquad BR_2(p)=\begin{cases}\{0\}&p<\tfrac12,\\\left[0,1\right]&p=\tfrac12,\\\{1\}&p>\tfrac12.\end{cases}$$ The sole intersection is at $(p,q)=(1/2,1/2)$.
§ 6.2

Existence

Theorem — Nash (1950)

Every normal-form game with a finite number of players and a finite, nonempty action set for each player has at least one Nash equilibrium in its mixed extension.

$$NE_{\mathrm{mix}}(\Gamma)\neq\varnothing$$

The equilibrium may be pure or involve randomization. The theorem guarantees existence, not uniqueness.

Section 7

Nash Equilibrium and Dominance

B. Motivation — Can We Simplify Before Solving?

A strictly dominated action cannot be used at equilibrium: moving its probability to the dominating strategy would increase the player’s expected payoff. We can therefore delete such actions before searching for supports. With weak dominance, some payoffs tie, so this argument no longer rules out every equilibrium using the action.

Proposition — Strict Dominance Preserves Every Equilibrium
1
A strictly dominant-strategy equilibrium is the unique Nash equilibrium.
If every player has a strictly dominant strategy, the profile formed by these strategies is the game’s only Nash equilibrium, even when all mixed strategies are allowed.
2
IESDS preserves the entire equilibrium set.
Let $(\Gamma^k)_{k\geq1}$ be a sequence obtained by deleting strictly dominated pure actions, possibly dominated by mixtures. Then $$NE_{\mathrm{mix}}(\Gamma^k)=NE_{\mathrm{mix}}(\Gamma^1)\qquad\text{for every }k\geq1.$$ An equilibrium of the reduced game is viewed as a profile in the original game by assigning probability zero to deleted actions. With this identification, the equilibrium sets are equal: no pure or mixed equilibrium is lost.
3
If strict elimination leaves a single profile, it solves the game.
When the game is dominance solvable by IESDS, the surviving profile is its unique Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies. No further support search is needed.
Proposition — Weak Dominance May Remove Equilibria
1
A dominant-strategy equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium.
If every player chooses a weakly dominant strategy, each is choosing a best response to the others. The resulting profile is a Nash equilibrium; the statement does not assert uniqueness.
2
Weak elimination gives inclusion, not equality.
For a sequence of games obtained by deleting weakly dominated pure actions, possibly dominated by mixtures, $$NE_{\mathrm{mix}}(\Gamma^k)\subseteq NE_{\mathrm{mix}}(\Gamma^1).$$ Again, extend the surviving strategies by zero on deleted actions. Every equilibrium found in the reduced game is an original equilibrium, but the elimination may have removed other original equilibria.
3
A unique surviving profile is an equilibrium of the original game.
If IEWDS leaves only one profile, that profile is a Nash equilibrium. The original game may still have other equilibria that were deleted along the way.
Method — Dominance First, Indifference Second
1
Look for strictly dominant strategies.
If every player has one, stop: their profile is the unique Nash equilibrium.
2
Otherwise, run IESDS.
Search for pure actions strictly dominated by either pure strategies or mixtures. Continue in each reduced game. If one profile remains, stop: the game is dominance solvable.
3
If several actions survive, search their possible supports.
Apply the strong indifference principle in the reduced game: impose equality within each support, check the probability restrictions, and verify that unused actions do not give higher payoffs. Include singleton supports.
4
Return to the original game.
Assign zero probability to the deleted actions. Because the eliminations were strict, the equilibria of the reduced game give exactly the original game’s full equilibrium set.
Example · The Full Step-by-Step Method
Player 1: rows · Player 2: columns · Payoffs: (player 1, player 2)
$A$$B$$C$
$A$$(1,1)$$(0,0)$$(5,-1)$
$B$$(-1,4)$$(0,4)$$(4,5)$
$C$$(0,0)$$(1,1)$$(5,0)$
Task: Find every Nash equilibrium using dominance first and the strong indifference principle in the reduced game.
1
Delete player 1’s strictly dominated row B.
Compare the first payoffs of rows $C$ and $B$ across columns $(A,B,C)$: $$C:(0,1,5),\qquad B:(-1,0,4).$$ Every comparison is strict: $0>-1$, $1>0$, and $5>4$. Thus $C$ strictly dominates $B$ for player 1. Delete row $B$.
Player 1: rows · Player 2: columns · Payoffs: (player 1, player 2)
$A$$B$$C$
$A$$(1,1)$$(0,0)$$(5,-1)$
$C$$(0,0)$$(1,1)$$(5,0)$
2
Delete player 2’s strictly dominated column C in the reduced game.
Using the second payoffs and row order $(A,C)$: $$B:(0,1),\qquad C:(-1,0).$$ Column $B$ strictly dominates column $C$, since $0>-1$ and $1>0$. This comparison uses the reduced game; in the original game, row $B$ gave player 2 a payoff of $5$ in column $C$ and $4$ in column $B$. After the deletion we obtain:
Player 1: rows · Player 2: columns · Payoffs: (player 1, player 2)
$A$$B$
$A$$(1,1)$$(0,0)$
$C$$(0,0)$$(1,1)$
3
Find the pure equilibria of the surviving game.
At $(A,A)$, each player obtains $1$ and would obtain $0$ by switching. The same is true at $(C,B)$. The two off-diagonal profiles give $(0,0)$, so they are not equilibria.

No further strict deletion is possible: each remaining action is a strict best response to one of the opponent’s actions.
4
Find the completely mixed equilibrium.
Let $p$ be player 1’s probability of $A$ (with $1-p$ on $C$), and let $q$ be player 2’s probability of $A$ (with $1-q$ on $B$). Player 1 is indifferent when $$g_1(A,q)=q=1-q=g_1(C,q)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad q=\frac12.$$ Player 2 is indifferent when $$g_2(p,A)=p=1-p=g_2(p,B)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p=\frac12.$$ Against a pure opponent, the best response is unique, so no equilibrium has exactly one mixing player. These three profiles exhaust the reduced game’s equilibria.
5
Restore the original action sets and check the unused actions.
At the mixed candidate, the supported actions give $1/2$ to each player. The deleted row $B$ gives player 1 $$g_1\left(B,\tfrac12A+\tfrac12B\right)=\tfrac12(-1)+\tfrac12(0)=-\frac12<\frac12.$$ The deleted column $C$ gives player 2 $$g_2\left(\tfrac12A+\tfrac12C,C\right)=\tfrac12(-1)+\tfrac12(0)=-\frac12<\frac12.$$ Thus the outside-support condition is satisfied. Strict elimination also ensures that both pure equilibria of the reduced game remain equilibria of the original game.
✓
State the complete answer.
The original game’s equilibria are $$NE=\left\{(A,A),(C,B),\left(\tfrac12A+\tfrac12C,\tfrac12A+\tfrac12B\right)\right\}.$$ The pure equilibria yield $(1,1)$ and the mixed equilibrium yields $(1/2,1/2)$. In vector form, using the original order $(A,B,C)$ for both players, the mixed equilibrium is $$\sigma_1=\left(\frac12,0,\frac12\right),\qquad\sigma_2=\left(\frac12,\frac12,0\right).$$
