Section 4

Mixed Extension

A. Choosing an Action at Random

If you always play rock, your opponent can win by playing paper. Always playing paper has the same problem: it can be beaten by scissors. Any fixed choice can be exploited.

A mixed strategy assigns a probability to each action. For example, choose rock, paper or scissors at random, each with probability $1/3$. Your opponent may know these probabilities without knowing which action you will play.

You still play one action. The strategy specifies the probabilities used to choose it.
Example · Why introduce mixed strategies?

Game 1 — Rock–paper–scissors. $R$: rock, $P$: paper, $S$: scissors.

Player 1: rows · Player 2: columns · Payoffs: (player 1, player 2)
$R$$P$$S$
$R$$(0,0)$$(-1,1)$$(1,-1)$
$P$$(1,-1)$$(0,0)$$(-1,1)$
$S$$(-1,1)$$(1,-1)$$(0,0)$

Game 2 — Different actions are rewarded.

Player 1: rows · Player 2: columns · Payoffs: (player 1, player 2)
$F$$O$
$F$$(0,0)$$(1,1)$
$O$$(1,1)$$(0,0)$
Task: Find the pure-strategy Nash equilibria of both games. Intuitively, how would you expect players to play?
1
Rock–paper–scissors: read each player’s own payoff.
For player 1, compare the first payoffs down each column: $$BR_1(R)=\{P\},\qquad BR_1(P)=\{S\},\qquad BR_1(S)=\{R\}.$$ For player 2, compare the second payoffs across each row: $$BR_2(R)=\{P\},\qquad BR_2(P)=\{S\},\qquad BR_2(S)=\{R\}.$$ For instance, when player 1 chooses $R$, player 2 obtains $0$ from $R$, $1$ from $P$, and $-1$ from $S$. Her best response is therefore $P$.
2
No cell is a pair of mutual best responses.
On the diagonal, both players receive $0$ and can obtain $1$ by switching to the action that wins. Off the diagonal, the losing player receives $-1$ and can switch to an action that wins. Thus every pure profile admits a profitable unilateral deviation: $$NE_{\mathrm{pure}}=\varnothing.$$ Choosing each action with probability $1/3$ removes this advantage: against that mixture, rock, paper and scissors each win with probability $1/3$, lose with probability $1/3$, and tie with probability $1/3$. Each therefore gives expected payoff $0$.
3
The second game rewards different actions.
For either player, the best response to $F$ is $O$, and the best response to $O$ is $F$. At $(F,F)$ or $(O,O)$, changing action raises the deviating player’s payoff from $0$ to $1$. At $(F,O)$ or $(O,F)$, changing action lowers it from $1$ to $0$. Therefore: $$NE_{\mathrm{pure}}=\{(F,O),(O,F)\}.$$ Both equilibria give payoffs $(1,1)$, but they require different choices: one player chooses $F$, the other $O$. Nothing in this symmetric game tells the players who should choose which action.

Both players could instead use the same rule: choose $F$ or $O$ with probability $1/2$. Their independent draws may produce different actions. Against an opponent using this rule, either action gives expected payoff $1/2$, so neither player can improve by changing her probabilities. This is a symmetric mixed equilibrium: the distributions are identical, although the realized actions need not be.
§ 4.1

Mixed Strategies and Expected Payoff

B. Definition — The Mixed Extension

Let $\Gamma=(N,S,g)$ be a normal-form game with finitely many players and a finite action set $S_i$ for each player. In the mixed extension, each player chooses probabilities over these actions. The payoff matrix stays the same.

$$\Sigma_i=\Delta(S_i)=\left\{p\in\mathbb R^{|S_i|}:p_k\geq0\ \text{for every }k,\quad \sum_{k=1}^{|S_i|}p_k=1\right\}$$

$\Delta(S_i)$ is the set of all probability distributions over $S_i$. Each entry $p_k$ is the probability of one action: it must be non-negative, and all entries must sum to $1$.

$$\Sigma=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\Sigma_i,\qquad\bar\Gamma=(N,\Sigma,\bar g)$$

A profile $\sigma\in\Sigma$ specifies one distribution for every player. Payoffs in this extended game are expected payoffs:

$$\bar g_i:\Sigma\longrightarrow\mathbb R,\qquad\bar g_i(\sigma)=\sum_{s\in S}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_j(s_j)\right]g_i(s)$$

A pure profile $s$ is one possible combination of actions, such as $(R,P)$. For each combination, multiply its probability × its payoff, then add the results. This expected payoff is an average; it need not equal the payoff obtained in a particular play.

C. Notation and Support

The notation distinguishes an action, the probability assigned to that action, and the entire probability distribution.

$S_i$ and $s_i\in S_i$$S_i$ is player $i$’s set of actions; $s_i$ is one action in that set. For example, $S_i=\{R,P,S\}$ and $s_i=R$.
$\sigma_i\in\Sigma_i$Player $i$’s mixed strategy: a complete distribution over $S_i$.
$\sigma_i(s_i)$The probability that player $i$ chooses the particular pure action $s_i$.
$\sigma_i=\sum_{s_i\in S_i}\sigma_i(s_i)\,s_i$A weighted-action notation. It lists the actions together with their probabilities.
$\sigma_i=(\sigma_i(s_i^1),\ldots,\sigma_i(s_i^{|S_i|}))$A vector of probabilities, in a specified action order. The last entry can be omitted when it is understood to equal one minus the sum of the other entries.
$\sigma\in\Sigma$The profile containing every player’s mixed strategy.
$\sigma_{-i}\in\Sigma_{-i}$The strategies of everyone except player $i$, and the set of such profiles.
$\bar g=(\bar g_j)_{j=1}^{N}$The profile of expected-payoff functions. We also write $g_i$ for $\bar g_i$ when a mixed strategy appears as an argument.
$$\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_i)=\{s_i\in S_i:\sigma_i(s_i)>0\}$$

The support is the set of actions that receive strictly positive probability. An action with probability zero is available in the game, but is not used by this particular mixed strategy.

Reading a Strategy and Its Support

Player 2 has three available actions, but chooses only $L$ and $M$:

$$S_2=\{L,M,R\},\qquad \sigma_2=qL+(1-q)M+0R,\qquad \operatorname{supp}(\sigma_2)=\{L,M\}\quad(0<q<1).$$

$L$ receives probability $q$, $M$ receives $1-q$, and $R$ receives zero. Thus $R$ belongs to the available action set $S_2$, but not to the support. If $q=1$, only $L$ remains in the support; if $q=0$, only $M$ remains.

A probability tells us how often an action is chosen. Its expected payoff depends on the payoff matrix and the opponents’ probabilities.
Remark — Independent Choices

Players randomize independently. To obtain the probability of a pure profile $s=(s_1,\ldots,s_N)$, we therefore multiply the probability of each player’s action:

$$\Pr(s)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_j(s_j)$$

For example, if each player independently chooses $F$ with probability $1/2$, the probability of $(F,F)$ is $1/2\times1/2=1/4$. Using the same probabilities does not mean drawing the same action.

Remark — Pure Strategies Are Included

A pure strategy is represented by a distribution that puts probability $1$ on a single action. The notation $\delta_{s_i}$ denotes this distribution:

$$\delta_{s_i}(s_i)=1,\qquad\delta_{s_i}(t_i)=0\quad(t_i\neq s_i),\qquad\operatorname{supp}(\delta_{s_i})=\{s_i\}$$

Thus we can identify each pure action with its corresponding distribution and write $S_i\subseteq\Delta(S_i)$. “Mixed strategy” is the general term and includes these pure strategies.

Proposition — Multi-linearity of Expected Payoffs

The expected payoff is linear in each player’s distribution when the other players’ distributions are held fixed. This is what multi-linearity means here.

$$g_i(\sigma_i,\sigma_{-i})=\sum_{s_i\in S_i}\sigma_i(s_i)\,g_i(s_i,\sigma_{-i})$$

First hold the opponents’ profile $\sigma_{-i}$ fixed. Compute what player $i$ would obtain from each pure action, then take the weighted average using her own probabilities $\sigma_i(s_i)$.

$$g_i(s_i,\sigma_{-i})=\sum_{s_{-i}\in S_{-i}}\left[\prod_{j\neq i}\sigma_j(s_j)\right]g_i(s_i,s_{-i})$$

In this second formula, player $i$’s action $s_i$ is fixed. Only the opponents’ actions are random, so we weight the payoffs using their probabilities.

Task: Write the set of mixed strategies $\Sigma_1, \Sigma_2$ and compute the expected payoffs $\bar{g}_1$ and $\bar{g}_2$ for the strategy profile $\sigma = \left(\frac{1}{4}L + \frac{3}{4}R,\; \frac{1}{3}L + \frac{2}{3}R\right)$.
1
Sets of mixed strategies:
$S_1 = \{R, L\}$ and $S_2 = \{R, L\}$, so: $$\Sigma_1 = \Delta(S_1) = \left\{(p, 1-p) : p \in [0,1]\right\}$$ $$\Sigma_2 = \Delta(S_2) = \left\{(q, 1-q) : q \in [0,1]\right\}$$ where $p = \sigma_1(R)$ is the probability P1 plays $R$, and $q = \sigma_2(R)$ is the probability P2 plays $R$.
2
Identify the probabilities from the given profile:
Player 1: $\sigma_1 = \frac{1}{4}L + \frac{3}{4}R$, so $\sigma_1(R) = \frac{3}{4}$, $\sigma_1(L) = \frac{1}{4}$.
Player 2: $\sigma_2 = \frac{1}{3}L + \frac{2}{3}R$, so $\sigma_2(R) = \frac{2}{3}$, $\sigma_2(L) = \frac{1}{3}$.
3
Probability of each pure profile (by independence):
$$P(R,R) = \sigma_1(R)\cdot\sigma_2(R) = \frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{2}{3} = \frac{1}{2}$$ $$P(R,L) = \sigma_1(R)\cdot\sigma_2(L) = \frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{4}$$ $$P(L,R) = \sigma_1(L)\cdot\sigma_2(R) = \frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{2}{3} = \frac{1}{6}$$ $$P(L,L) = \sigma_1(L)\cdot\sigma_2(L) = \frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{12}$$ Check: $\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{6}+\frac{1}{12} = \frac{6+3+2+1}{12} = 1$ ✓
4
Expected payoff for Player 1: $$\bar{g}_1(\sigma) = P(R,R)\cdot g_1(R,R) + P(R,L)\cdot g_1(R,L) + P(L,R)\cdot g_1(L,R) + P(L,L)\cdot g_1(L,L)$$ $$= \frac{1}{2}\cdot(-1) + \frac{1}{4}\cdot(1) + \frac{1}{6}\cdot(1) + \frac{1}{12}\cdot(-1)$$ $$= -\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{6} - \frac{1}{12}$$ $$= \frac{-6 + 3 + 2 - 1}{12} = \frac{-2}{12} = -\frac{1}{6}$$
5

✓

§ 4.2

Interpretation

D. Four Interpretations of Mixed Strategies

A probability of $30\%$ on $L$ can have several meanings:

1
Randomization.
The player uses a random device that selects $L$ with probability $30\%$. Each player makes an independent draw.
2
Random beliefs.
The player has chosen an action, but the opponents do not know it. They assign a $30\%$ probability to $L$.
3
Population.
Everyone plays a pure action, and $30\%$ of the population chooses $L$.
4
Unknown preferences.
Small private preferences influence which pure action a player chooses. Others cannot observe those preferences; their uncertainty about them gives a $30\%$ chance of $L$.
Section 5

Dominance in Mixed Strategies

A. Motivation

Two actions may have complementary advantages: one performs well against some opposing choices, the other against the rest. A suitable mixture can then outperform a third action against every opposing choice, even though neither action does so alone.

Definition

Dominance and the Pure-Profile Test

Definition — Strict and Weak Dominance

A strategy $\sigma_i\in\Sigma_i$ is strictly dominated if another strategy $\tau_i\in\Sigma_i$ gives a strictly higher expected payoff against every possible mixed profile of the opponents:

$$\exists\tau_i\in\Sigma_i,\quad\forall\sigma_{-i}\in\Sigma_{-i},\qquad g_i(\tau_i,\sigma_{-i})>g_i(\sigma_i,\sigma_{-i})$$

The same alternative $\tau_i$ must work against all opponents’ profiles. We cannot choose a different dominating strategy after seeing what the opponents do.

For weak dominance, replace $>$ by $\geq$ for every profile, with a strict improvement for at least one profile. The alternative must never be worse and must sometimes be better.

Proposition — Only Pure Opponent Profiles Need to Be Checked

To verify that $\tau_i$ strictly dominates $\sigma_i$, it is enough to check every pure profile of the opponents:

$$\forall s_{-i}\in S_{-i},\qquad g_i(\tau_i,s_{-i})>g_i(\sigma_i,s_{-i})$$

The analogous test for weak dominance uses $\geq$ everywhere and $>$ for at least one pure opponent profile. The compared strategies $\sigma_i$ and $\tau_i$ may still be mixed: only the opponents’ profiles are restricted to pure profiles for this test.

This follows from multi-linearity: payoffs against mixed opponents are weighted averages of payoffs against pure profiles. A strict improvement at every pure profile remains strict in the average.

Practical Remarks — What Do We Eliminate?

We eliminate pure actions dominated by a pure or mixed strategy. Deleting an action also removes every mixture that assigns it positive probability, so there is no need to eliminate distributions one by one.

A strictly dominant strategy must be pure. With payoff ties, a mixture can also be a best response against every profile of the opponents.
Procedure

Iterated Elimination with Mixed Strategies

Definition — IESDS and IEWDS

Start from $\Gamma^1=\Gamma$. At each stage, the current game contains only the actions not yet deleted. Both the comparisons against the opponents and the possible dominating mixtures must use this current game.

1
Search in the current game.
At stage $k$, search for a pure action $s_i\in S_i^k$ dominated by a mixed strategy $\tau_i\in\Delta(S_i^k)$. Check all remaining pure profiles $s_{-i}\in S_{-i}^k$. Use strict dominance for IESDS and weak dominance for IEWDS.
2
Delete the dominated action.
Remove that action from player $i$’s set and leave the opponents’ sets unchanged: $$S_i^{k+1}=S_i^k\setminus\{s_i\},\qquad S_{-i}^{k+1}=S_{-i}^{k}.$$ Keep the same payoff numbers on the remaining profiles. Then repeat the comparison in this smaller game.
3
Stop when no further deletion is possible.
The procedure ends when the reduced game has no remaining pure action dominated in the relevant sense by a pure or mixed strategy.
Remarks — More Deletions May Become Possible

A procedure restricted to domination by pure strategies may stop even though domination by mixtures still allows another deletion. Allowing mixed dominating strategies therefore enlarges the comparisons available at each stage.

The order properties stated earlier remain unchanged: IESDS is order-independent, while IEWDS can depend on the order. For IESDS, this concerns the final reduced game after the elimination procedure has been continued until it stops.
