Section 4

Mixed Extension

A. Motivation

The Nash equilibrium concept suffers from two caveats:

1
Some games do not have any NE in pure strategies. In this case, we miss a prediction on players' behaviors. For example, this happens in games with much conflict of interest (such as zero-sum games). In zero-sum games, the best response defines a circular path between all the strategy profiles. Each player would like to conceal her strategy and become unpredictable. Requiring each of the two players to do their best given the other player's strategy is too strong a condition.
2
Some games have multiple Nash equilibria. Players therefore need to coordinate on a specific equilibrium. In particular, in symmetric games, there may be no symmetric Nash equilibrium, but several asymmetric Nash equilibria. In asymmetric NE, players must share roles between the various equilibrium actions. This may be unfeasible given that players cannot communicate. Implicitly, in symmetric games, players come from the same population, and cannot be identified to one specific role.

§ 4.1

Mixed Strategies and Expected Payoff

B. Setting Up the Mixed Extension

Let $\Gamma=(N,S,g)$ be a game in normal form. Throughout the rest of this chapter, we assume that for every $i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}$, $S_i$ is a finite set.

Mixed, or randomized, strategies make it possible to represent opportunities to bluff or to play randomly. We enrich the set of strategies and the set of payoffs of a normal form game to allow for this.

Definition — Mixed Extension
The Mixed Extension $(N, \Sigma, \bar{g})$

The mixed extension of the game in normal form $(N,S,g)$ is the game in normal form $(N,\Sigma,\bar g)$, where:

Pure-strategy space:
$$S = \prod_{i=1}^{n} S_i$$
Mixed-strategy set for player $i$:
$$\Sigma_i = \Delta(S_i) := \left\{ p \in \mathbb{R}^{|S_i|} : \forall k,\; p_k \geq 0,\; \sum_{k=1}^{|S_i|} p_k = 1 \right\}$$
Expected payoff function for player $i$:
$$\bar{g}_i : \prod_{j=1}^{N} \Delta(S_j) \to \mathbb{R}$$
$$\sigma \mapsto \sum_{s \in S} \left[ \prod_{j=1}^{N} \sigma_j(s_j) \right] g_i(s)$$
A mixed strategy is a probability distribution over the set of pure strategies.
🎲 Core Intuition
Pure strategy
Choose one action with probability 1.
Example: always play $L$.
Mixed strategy
Choose a probability distribution over pure actions.
Example: if $S_i = \{L, R\}$, then $\sigma_i = \left(\tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{3}{4}\right)$ means $P(L)=\tfrac{1}{4}$, $P(R)=\tfrac{3}{4}$.
Remark — Independence and Profile Probability

Players choose their mixed strategies independently of each other. Hence the product

$$\prod_{j=1}^{N} \sigma_j(s_j)$$

is the probability that players play the pure strategy profile $s = (s_1, \ldots, s_N)$.

The payoff associated to a mixed strategy profile is the expected payoff. One immediate consequence is multi-linearity.

How to Compute the Expected Payoff

For every possible pure strategy profile $s \in S$:

  1. Compute the probability that this profile occurs: $\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{N} \sigma_j(s_j)$.
  2. Multiply this probability by the payoff generated by the profile: $g_i(s)$.
  3. Sum over all possible profiles $s \in S$.
$$\bar{g}_i(\sigma) = \sum_{s \in S} \left[ \prod_{j=1}^{N} \sigma_j(s_j) \right] g_i(s)$$
Proposition — Multi-linearity of the Expected Payoff

The expected payoff function is multi-linear. In particular:

$$\bar{g}_i(\sigma_i, \sigma_{-i}) = \sum_{s_i \in S_i} \sigma_i(s_i)\, g_i(s_i, \sigma_{-i})$$

This says: the expected payoff to player $i$ equals the weighted average of her payoffs across pure strategies, where the weights are the probabilities $\sigma_i(s_i)$ she assigns to each pure strategy $s_i$.

C. Notation

We use the following notations:

$\sigma_j(s_j)$Probability that player $j$ plays pure strategy $s_j$.
$\sigma_i \in \Sigma_i$Strategy of player $i$ (a mixed strategy).
$\sigma_i = \sigma_i(s_i)\cdot s_i + \ldots + \sigma_i(s_i') \cdot s_i'$Explicit representation of $\sigma_i$ as a weighted combination of pure strategies.
$\sigma_i = \bigl(\sigma_i(s_i), \ldots, \sigma_i(s_i')\bigr)$Vector representation of $\sigma_i$. When unambiguous, the last weight $\sigma_i(s_i')$ can be omitted (all weights sum to 1).
$\sigma \in \Sigma$Mixed strategy profile — actions (distributions) for all players.
$\Sigma = \displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{N} \Sigma_j$Set of all mixed strategy profiles.
$\sigma_{-i} \in \Sigma_{-i}$Mixed strategy profile and set of strategy profiles for all players except $i$.
$\bar{g} = (\bar{g}_j)_{j \in \{1,\ldots,n\}}$Payoff function profile. We often write $g$ for $\bar{g}$.
🔤 Notation Decoder — Pure vs. Mixed
$S_i$
Pure-strategy set of player $i$.
$\Sigma_i = \Delta(S_i)$
Set of all probability distributions over $S_i$ — mixed strategies available to player $i$.
$s_i \in S_i$
One pure strategy.
$\sigma_i \in \Sigma_i$
One mixed strategy (a probability distribution).
$\sigma_i(s_i)$
Probability assigned by player $i$ to pure strategy $s_i$.
$g_i$ vs $\bar{g}_i$
$g_i$ = payoff on pure profiles. $\bar{g}_i$ = expected payoff on mixed profiles.
Remark — Pure Strategy as a Special Case of Mixed

A pure strategy is a specific case of a mixed strategy. The pure strategy $s_i$ corresponds to the degenerate distribution:

$$\delta_{s_i} \qquad (S_i \subset \Delta(S_i))$$

i.e., all probability is placed on the single pure strategy $s_i$ and zero elsewhere. The set of pure strategies $S_i$ is therefore a subset of the set of mixed strategies $\Delta(S_i)$.

Example · Mixed Strategy — Expected Payoff Computation
Consider the following game:
Player 2
$R$$L$
Player 1$R$$(-1,1)$$(1,-1)$
$L$$(1,-1)$$(-1,1)$
Consider the mixed strategy profile:
$$\left(\frac{1}{4}L + \frac{3}{4}R,\quad \frac{1}{3}L + \frac{2}{3}R\right)$$
i.e., Player 1 plays $L$ with probability $\frac{1}{4}$ and $R$ with probability $\frac{3}{4}$; Player 2 plays $L$ with probability $\frac{1}{3}$ and $R$ with probability $\frac{2}{3}$.
Task: Write the set of mixed strategies $\Sigma_1, \Sigma_2$ and compute the expected payoffs $\bar{g}_1$ and $\bar{g}_2$ for the strategy profile $\sigma = \left(\frac{1}{4}L + \frac{3}{4}R,\; \frac{1}{3}L + \frac{2}{3}R\right)$.
1
Sets of mixed strategies:
$S_1 = \{R, L\}$ and $S_2 = \{R, L\}$, so: $$\Sigma_1 = \Delta(S_1) = \left\{(p, 1-p) : p \in [0,1]\right\}$$ $$\Sigma_2 = \Delta(S_2) = \left\{(q, 1-q) : q \in [0,1]\right\}$$ where $p = \sigma_1(R)$ is the probability P1 plays $R$, and $q = \sigma_2(R)$ is the probability P2 plays $R$.
2
Identify the probabilities from the given profile:
Player 1: $\sigma_1 = \frac{1}{4}L + \frac{3}{4}R$, so $\sigma_1(R) = \frac{3}{4}$, $\sigma_1(L) = \frac{1}{4}$.
Player 2: $\sigma_2 = \frac{1}{3}L + \frac{2}{3}R$, so $\sigma_2(R) = \frac{2}{3}$, $\sigma_2(L) = \frac{1}{3}$.
3
Probability of each pure profile (by independence):
$$P(R,R) = \sigma_1(R)\cdot\sigma_2(R) = \frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{2}{3} = \frac{1}{2}$$ $$P(R,L) = \sigma_1(R)\cdot\sigma_2(L) = \frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{4}$$ $$P(L,R) = \sigma_1(L)\cdot\sigma_2(R) = \frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{2}{3} = \frac{1}{6}$$ $$P(L,L) = \sigma_1(L)\cdot\sigma_2(L) = \frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{12}$$ Check: $\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{6}+\frac{1}{12} = \frac{6+3+2+1}{12} = 1$ ✓
4
Expected payoff for Player 1: $$\bar{g}_1(\sigma) = P(R,R)\cdot g_1(R,R) + P(R,L)\cdot g_1(R,L) + P(L,R)\cdot g_1(L,R) + P(L,L)\cdot g_1(L,L)$$ $$= \frac{1}{2}\cdot(-1) + \frac{1}{4}\cdot(1) + \frac{1}{6}\cdot(1) + \frac{1}{12}\cdot(-1)$$ $$= -\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{6} - \frac{1}{12}$$ $$= \frac{-6 + 3 + 2 - 1}{12} = \frac{-2}{12} = -\frac{1}{6}$$
5
Expected payoff for Player 2: $$\bar{g}_2(\sigma) = P(R,R)\cdot g_2(R,R) + P(R,L)\cdot g_2(R,L) + P(L,R)\cdot g_2(L,R) + P(L,L)\cdot g_2(L,L)$$ $$= \frac{1}{2}\cdot(1) + \frac{1}{4}\cdot(-1) + \frac{1}{6}\cdot(-1) + \frac{1}{12}\cdot(1)$$ $$= \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{12}$$ $$= \frac{6 - 3 - 2 + 1}{12} = \frac{2}{12} = \frac{1}{6}$$
✓
Conclusion: $$\bar{g}_1\!\left(\tfrac{1}{4}L+\tfrac{3}{4}R,\; \tfrac{1}{3}L+\tfrac{2}{3}R\right) = -\frac{1}{6}$$ $$\bar{g}_2\!\left(\tfrac{1}{4}L+\tfrac{3}{4}R,\; \tfrac{1}{3}L+\tfrac{2}{3}R\right) = +\frac{1}{6}$$ Note: $\bar{g}_1 + \bar{g}_2 = 0$ — consistent with the fact that this is a zero-sum game.
§ 4.2

Interpretation

D. Four Interpretations of Mixed Strategies

The most common interpretations of mixed strategies are:

1
Randomization. Each player independently chooses their pure strategy at random because they want to create uncertainty for the other players.
2
Random beliefs. Players do not actually randomize their play, but the other players believe that they do.
3
Population. Each player individually plays a pure strategy; the mixed strategy represents the proportions of the population playing each pure strategy.
4
Unknown preferences. Each player chooses their pure strategy based on the known payoffs, plus a small preference that is unknown to the other players.
