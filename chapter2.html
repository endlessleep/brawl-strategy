Extensive-Form Games

A. Motivation & Core Intuition

In a dynamic game, players move in sequence. A later player can react to earlier actions. With perfect information, the player moving knows the full history: they know exactly which node they are at.

An extensive-form game represents this timing as a tree. Start at the root, follow the chosen branches, and stop at a terminal node, where payoffs are received.

ObjectWhat to identify
Tree $\mathcal T$Decision nodes, branches and terminal nodes; no cycles.
Players $N$ and control $\mathcal P$Who moves at each decision node. $Z_i$ is the set of nodes controlled by player $i$.
Actions $A(z)$The choices available at decision node $z$.
Payoffs $g_i(t)$Player $i$'s payoff at terminal node $t$.
$\Gamma=(\mathcal T,N,\mathcal P,A,g)$
Key Vocabulary
History: actions already taken before a node. Play / path: the sequence actually followed from the root to an endpoint. On path: visited under the chosen strategies. Off path: not visited under those strategies.
Formal course

Why timing changes the model

In a simultaneous normal-form game, players cannot react to the actions currently chosen by others. In many situations, investment precedes production, entry precedes the incumbent's response, or an offer precedes acceptance. Whether a player moves, the available actions and the resulting payoffs can depend on past play. A tree records those dependencies explicitly.

Perfect information concerns observed past actions. Knowledge of the game's rules and payoff functions is also maintained here.

Formal course

Tree, nodes and predecessor

A rooted tree $\mathcal T=(Z,\phi)$ consists of nodes $Z=H\sqcup Q$: $H$ contains the nonterminal (decision) nodes and $Q$ the terminal nodes. The root $r$ has no predecessor; every other node $z$ has exactly one immediate predecessor $\phi(z)$. There are no cycles and each node is connected to the root by a unique finite path.

The terminal set is also denoted $T$ in expressions such as $g_i(t)$, $t\in T$. Thus $T=Q$ here; the tree itself is $\mathcal T$.

An extensive-form game is $\Gamma=(\mathcal T,N,\mathcal P,A,g)$:

  1. $\mathcal T$ specifies the tree.
  2. $N\in\mathbb N$ is the finite number of players, indexed by $i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}$.
  3. $\mathcal P=(Z_i)_{i=1}^N$ partitions $H$: $H=\bigcup_i Z_i$, with disjoint sets. Player $i$ controls the nodes in $Z_i$.
  4. $A=(A(h))_{h\in H}$ gives the available actions; each action selects a successor node.
  5. $g=(g_i(t))_{t\in T,\,i=1,\ldots,N}$ assigns each player a payoff at each terminal node.

At the root, its controlling player chooses an action. Play continues at the successor, under the player controlling that node, until a terminal node $t$ is reached; player $i$ then receives $g_i(t)$.

A play is the sequence of visited nodes from root to terminal. A history before a node records the preceding visited nodes (equivalently, the past actions). Perfect information means that the player moving observes this history and knows the current node.

Pure, Mixed & Behavioral Strategies

A. Pure strategies

A strategy is a complete contingent plan

An action is one choice at one node. A pure strategy specifies an action at every node controlled by the player, including nodes that will not be reached.

$$S_i=\prod_{z\in Z_i}A(z)$$ $$|S_i|=\prod_{z\in Z_i}|A(z)|$$

The product means “choose one action for each node.” With $m$ actions at each of $k$ nodes, there are $m^k$ pure strategies. Two nodes with two actions each give $2\times2=4$ strategies.

▶ Show Proof ▼

A strategy is a tuple with one component per decision node. The first component has $|A(z_1)|$ choices, the second has $|A(z_2)|$, and so on. Every combination defines a plan, so the multiplication rule gives the formula. This counting formula applies to finitely many nodes with finite action sets.

Additional explanation · proof

More formally, the map $s_i\mapsto(s_i(z))_{z\in Z_i}$ is a bijection between complete plans and $\prod_{z\in Z_i}A(z)$: it is injective because equal instructions at every node mean the same plan, and surjective because any tuple of available actions defines a plan. For finitely many nodes, count the independent choices successively. If $Z_i=\varnothing$, the empty plan is the single strategy and the empty product equals $1$.

Exam trap: never remove a component just because its node is off path. The plan must say what the player would do there.
Formal course

From a plan to a payoff

A pure strategy $s_i\in S_i$ is a function assigning $s_i(z)\in A(z)$ to every $z\in Z_i$. A profile $s=(s_1,\ldots,s_N)\in S=\prod_i S_i$ induces a unique play and terminal node $t(s)$ in a terminating game. The same payoff notation is therefore used for $g_i(s)=g_i(t(s))$.

Listing complete strategies is a critical modelling step: an action at one node and a vector of actions across all controlled nodes are different objects.

$\Delta(X)$ means the set of probability distributions over $X$. We write $\mu_i$ for a mixed strategy and $\beta_i$ for a behavioral strategy to keep the distinction visible.

$$\mathbb E[g_i]=\sum_{t\text{ terminal}}\Pr(t)\,g_i(t)$$

Under behavioral play, multiply action probabilities along a path to obtain its terminal probability, then multiply by the payoff and sum over endpoints.

Equivalence: in the finite perfect-information games studied here, every mixed strategy has an outcome-equivalent behavioral strategy and conversely. “Equivalent” means the same terminal probabilities against any fixed strategies of the other players; the two objects need not be identical.

The general result requires perfect recall, which these games have.

Formal course

Random strategies and outcome equivalence

A mixed strategy is $\mu_i\in\Delta(S_i)$: one complete plan is drawn ex ante. A behavioral strategy is $\sigma_i=(\sigma_i(z))_{z\in Z_i}$, with $\sigma_i(z)\in\Delta(A(z))$: actions are drawn independently at the decision nodes. For terminal node $t$, write $\sigma(t)$ for the probability of its path. Then

$$g_i(\sigma)=\sum_{t\in T}\sigma(t)g_i(t).$$

Proposition. In a finite extensive-form game with perfect information, every mixed strategy has an outcome-equivalent behavioral strategy, and conversely. Equivalence holds against every fixed strategy profile of the other players. Both representations can subsequently be denoted $\sigma_i\in\Sigma_i$; $\mu_i$ and $\beta_i$ distinguish them when needed.

ProofShow why mixed and behavioral strategies are equivalent▼
Additional explanation · proof

Behavioral → mixed. Before play, independently draw the action prescribed at every node using the behavioral probabilities. The resulting complete plan has probability $\mu_i(s_i)=\prod_{z\in Z_i}\sigma_i(z)(s_i(z))$. Summing over unused instructions leaves exactly the products of probabilities along each terminal path.

Mixed → behavioral. At node $z$, condition the distribution of complete plans on the player's own past instructions along the unique path to $z$. Use the conditional probability of each current instruction as $\sigma_i(z)$. At a zero-probability history any distribution can be assigned. Conditional probabilities multiply and telescope along each path, reproducing its probability under the mixed plan.

Perfect recall ensures that the conditioning history is well defined for the player. Perfect-information games have this property. Using unconditional marginals at successive nodes controlled by the same player would generally lose the correlation encoded by a mixed plan. In the kidnapping game the two nodes are mutually exclusive, so the simpler marginal formulas suffice.

Normal Form & Nash Equilibrium

The method
  1. List complete pure strategies for each player.
  2. Build the normal form: for each strategy profile, follow the implied path and copy the endpoint payoffs.
  3. Find mutual best responses as in Chapter 1. A Nash equilibrium leaves no player a profitable unilateral change of their complete plan.
$\Gamma^{NF}=(N,S,g),\qquad g_i(s)=g_i(t(s))$

$t(s)$ is the endpoint reached under profile $s$. Different profiles can lead to the same endpoint because their plans differ only off path.

The limitation of Nash: an off-path threat can help deter an earlier move even when carrying it out would hurt the threatening player. Nash alone need not rule this out.
Formal course

Normal-form reduction

The normal-form reduction of $\Gamma$ is $\Gamma'=(N,S,g)$, where $S=\prod_i S_i$ is the set of complete pure-strategy profiles and $g_i(s)=g_i(t(s))$. An extensive-form game can always be represented this way; finite strategy sets give a payoff matrix for two players.

Dominance and Nash equilibrium therefore apply to dynamic games. But the normal form evaluates a whole plan ex ante: it need not constrain actions at unreached nodes. A threat is credible only when carrying it out is optimal for the player making it, given the subsequent behavior.

Solve a Game

Subgame Perfection & Backward Induction

A. Definitions

A subgame starts at a decision node and includes everything that follows it. With perfect information, each decision node starts a subgame. The root gives the whole game; other decision nodes give proper subgames.

A subgame-perfect equilibrium (SPE) is a strategy profile that is a Nash equilibrium in every subgame, including those off path.

$$SPE\subseteq NE$$
Credibility test: if this node were reached, would the player still want to take the prescribed action, given the continuation strategies?
Formal course

Subgames and sequential optimality

For $z_0\in Z\setminus T$, the subgame starting at $z_0$ is the subtree containing $z_0$ and all its successors, with the same players, actions and terminal payoffs. A profile $\sigma$ is a subgame-perfect (Nash) equilibrium if its restriction is a Nash equilibrium in every such subgame.

This strengthens Nash equilibrium: actions must remain optimal after every history, on path and off path. Since the root also defines a subgame, every SPE is a Nash equilibrium. Nash profiles supported by noncredible threats fail this stronger test.

B. Backward Induction — Step-by-Step Method
  1. Start at the last decision nodes.
  2. At each node, maximize the payoff of the player moving there.
  3. Replace the solved continuation by its payoff pair and move one step backward.
  4. Continue to the root. Report the complete strategy profile, then its path and payoffs.
If a player is indifferent, keep all maximizing choices. Different continuation choices can produce multiple SPE; in mixed strategies, mixtures over tied optimal actions may also be possible.
Formal course

Backward induction characterizes SPE

Algorithm. Identify a minimal subgame, compute one of its Nash equilibria, replace that subgame by a terminal node carrying its equilibrium payoff vector, and repeat. In a finite perfect-information tree, a minimal subgame is a last decision node.

Proposition. The solutions obtained by backward induction are exactly the SPE. To characterize the whole set, retain every maximizing continuation, including mixtures over tied optimal actions.

ProofShow one-shot deviations, backward induction and existence▼
Additional explanation · proof

One-shot deviation principle (finite horizon). An SPE cannot admit a profitable change at one node of a subgame, since that change is an admissible unilateral strategy deviation. Conversely, suppose no one-node change is profitable, conditional on reaching that node. At the last nodes, prescribed actions are optimal. Moving backward, optimal future behavior is fixed, so no earlier change followed by any continuation can improve on the prescribed action. Induction over the remaining depth rules out every profitable multi-node deviation.

Backward induction. Solving last subgames imposes exactly their Nash conditions. Replacing each with its equilibrium payoff leaves the preceding choices and incentives unchanged. Induction to the root therefore produces an SPE. Conversely, an SPE must use an equilibrium in each minimal subgame, and its earlier choices must be optimal for those continuations. It is thus obtained by some sequence of backward-induction choices.

Existence theorem. A finite tree with finite action sets and perfect information has at least one pure SPE. At each last decision node a maximum over a finite nonempty action set exists. Choose a maximizer, collapse the continuation, and repeat. The finite number of nodes ensures termination. The preceding argument establishes subgame perfection:

$$\varnothing\ne SPE\subseteq NE.$$

Nonemptiness does not imply uniqueness. Continuous-action games need a separate existence argument, as in the Cournot optimization.

C. One-shot deviations & existence

For the finite-horizon perfect-information games considered here, a profile is SPE iff there is no profitable one-shot deviation at any decision node: change only the action there and keep subsequent behavior fixed.

Evaluate the gain starting from that node, even if it is off path. Checking only deviations that alter the original equilibrium path is insufficient.

Existence: every finite game tree with finite action sets and perfect information has at least one pure-strategy SPE. Backward induction constructs one. Existence does not mean uniqueness.
▶ Show Corollary ▼

Under these finite-game assumptions, if there is exactly one pure Nash equilibrium, it must be the unique pure SPE. If several pure NE exist, test their continuations in every proper subgame. The continuous-action Cournot example is solved directly; the finite-action theorem does not by itself prove its existence.

Formal course

Sequential games with simultaneous stages

Some strategic situations combine sequential choices with simultaneous decisions. Extend the extensive form by allowing a decision node to be replaced by a normal-form game. Any subgame starting there or above must include that whole simultaneous game: neither player observes the other's current action before choosing.

SPE, the one-shot deviation principle and backward induction still apply under the corresponding finite-horizon assumptions. At a simultaneous stage, solve a Nash equilibrium of the matrix, rather than optimizing one player's move and letting the other observe it. Replace the stage by its equilibrium payoff pair and continue backward.

If that stage has several Nash equilibria, each continuation must be examined; different continuation equilibria can change earlier incentives.

03

Time & Repetition

Formal course

Payoffs at different dates

Over a long or infinite horizon, payoffs may arrive before terminal nodes. The intertemporal payoff function $v_i(x_1,\ldots,x_t,\ldots)$ evaluates the stream, where $x_t$ is received at date $t\ge1$. Summing the payments assumes that one unit has the same value at every date. Preferences for earlier utility can change the ranking of streams even when their undiscounted totals coincide.

Worked tree: move dated payments to the endpoints →

Formal course

Time consistency and exponential discounting

Definition. Time preferences are consistent if, in any game, the player has the same ranking of feasible future terminal outcomes ex ante and interim after any given history. Comparisons after a history concern continuations sharing that history.

One-shot deviation principle. Under time-consistent preferences, a profile is SPE iff no player has a profitable one-shot deviation, within the finite-horizon setting. For infinite horizons, time consistency alone is not enough: the usual extension also needs continuity at infinity, satisfied by bounded stage payoffs with discount factor $0<\delta<1$.

With an infinite horizon there is no last period from which to begin backward induction. Use the definition of SPE and, under these conditions, the one-shot deviation principle.

Exponential discounting:

$$v_i(x_1,x_2,\ldots)=(1-\delta)\sum_{t\ge1}\delta^{t-1}x_t,\qquad\delta\in(0,1).$$

A low $\delta$ discounts the future more strongly; a high $\delta$ gives more weight to later payoffs. The factor $1-\delta$ normalizes the infinite weights to one and does not change preferences. This specification is tractable and requires only one discount parameter.

Formal course

Geometric sums and useful identities

$$\sum_{t=1}^{T}\delta^{t-1}=\frac{1-\delta^T}{1-\delta},\qquad \sum_{t\ge1}\delta^{t-1}=\frac1{1-\delta}.$$ $$v(x,x,\ldots)=x,\qquad v(x,y,y,\ldots)=(1-\delta)x+\delta y.$$

Proposition. Exponential discounting preferences are time consistent.

ProofShow geometric sums, the two identities and time consistency▼
Additional explanation · proof

Write $S_T=1+\delta+\cdots+\delta^{T-1}$. Subtract $\delta S_T$ to obtain $(1-\delta)S_T=1-\delta^T$. As $T\to\infty$, $\delta^T\to0$ because $0<\delta<1$.

$$v(x,x,\ldots)=(1-\delta)x\frac1{1-\delta}=x,$$ $$\begin{aligned}v(x,y,y,\ldots)&=(1-\delta)\left[x+y\sum_{t=2}^{\infty}\delta^{t-1}\right]\\&=(1-\delta)x+(1-\delta)y\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\\&=(1-\delta)x+\delta y.\end{aligned}$$

For time consistency, fix a history through date $\tau-1$. Its realized contribution is the same for every feasible continuation. At the initial date, continuation $x$ contributes

$$(1-\delta)\sum_{t=\tau}^{\infty}\delta^{t-1}x_t=\delta^{\tau-1}(1-\delta)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta^k x_{\tau+k}.$$

The remaining sum is exactly the evaluation at date $\tau$. Multiplication by the positive constant $\delta^{\tau-1}$ and addition of the common past cannot change rankings. Hence a preferred continuation remains preferred after the shared history.

Formal course

Repeated games: history-dependent plans

Repeated interaction lets players condition current behavior on earlier play. Reputation, promises and punishments can create incentives for coordination and efficiency.

Fix a normal-form stage game $G$. The $T$-repeated game $G_T$ plays $G$ in each of $T$ periods; $G_\infty$ repeats it infinitely. At the end of each stage, players observe the actions taken in that and all previous stages and receive the stage payoffs.

The players remain those of $G$. A pure strategy specifies an action in every period after every possible history, not just a sequence along the intended path. Payoffs are evaluated by an intertemporal function, such as exponential discounting. Simultaneous actions within a stage are not observed before that stage's choice.

Formal course

Finite repetition: what backward induction implies

Proposition. If the stage game $G$ has a unique Nash equilibrium $\sigma$, then the finite repeated game $G_T$ has a unique SPE: play $\sigma$ in every period, after every history. Payoffs are separable over dates with positive weights, as with a common discount factor $\delta\in(0,1]$ in a finite horizon.

Dominance, NE and SPE are defined as in other dynamic games. With several stage-game equilibria, continuation play can depend on history: a less attractive equilibrium can serve as a credible punishment, potentially sustaining an earlier action profile that is not itself a stage-game equilibrium.

ProofShow the unique-stage-equilibrium result▼
Additional explanation · proof

At period $T$, after every history, there is no future reward or punishment. The continuation must therefore use the unique stage equilibrium $\sigma$. Its payoff is independent of how that history was reached.

At $T-1$, changing the current action cannot change the already-determined continuation payoff at $T$. Best responses are thus exactly those of the stage game, forcing $\sigma$ again. Repeat the argument through periods $T-2,\ldots,1$. This proves both existence of the repeated-$\sigma$ SPE and uniqueness at every history.

Formal course

Equilibrium payoff sets

Let $E_T$ denote the set of Nash-equilibrium payoffs of $G_T$. Under a common normalized payoff convention across horizons,

$$\varnothing\ne E_1\subseteq E_T.$$

The nonemptiness statement assumes a finite stage game (mixed strategies allowed), or another hypothesis ensuring a stage-game Nash equilibrium. For finite $T$, compare weighted averages: $v_T=\sum_{t=1}^T w_tg^t$ with $w_t>0$ and $\sum_t w_t=1$. With discounting, $w_t=\delta^{t-1}/\sum_{k=1}^T\delta^{k-1}$.

The normalization matters: an unnormalized sum of $T$ identical stage payoffs is $T$ times that payoff when $\delta=1$, so the literal inclusion of payoff vectors would not follow under that convention.

ProofShow why every stage-equilibrium payoff remains available▼
Additional explanation · proof

Take any stage-game equilibrium $\sigma$. Prescribe $\sigma$ every period after every history, independently of past actions. No unilateral deviation can affect future opponents' behavior. Each period's equilibrium action is a best response, so adding the corresponding inequalities with positive weights rules out any profitable deviation over the repeated game. The profile is an SPE, hence a NE.

If its stage payoff is $u$, its normalized repeated payoff is $\sum_tw_tu=u$. Thus every $u\in E_1$ belongs to $E_T$. At least one such $u$ exists under the stated existence assumptions.

Formal course

Infinite horizons and discussion

An infinite repetition has no final period. The finite backward-induction argument therefore cannot simply be started “at infinity.” Discounted continuation values and credible responses after every history are needed.
