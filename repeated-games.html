Repeated Games

Formal course

Repeated games: history-dependent plans

Repeated interaction lets players condition current behavior on earlier play. Reputation, promises and punishments can create incentives for coordination and efficiency.

Fix a normal-form stage game $G$. The $T$-repeated game $G_T$ plays $G$ in each of $T$ periods; $G_\infty$ repeats it infinitely. At the end of each stage, players observe the actions taken in that and all previous stages and receive the stage payoffs.

The players remain those of $G$. A pure strategy specifies an action in every period after every possible history, not just a sequence along the intended path. Payoffs are evaluated by an intertemporal function, such as exponential discounting. Simultaneous actions within a stage are not observed before that stage's choice.

Formal course

Finite repetition: what backward induction implies

Proposition. If the stage game $G$ has a unique Nash equilibrium $\sigma$, then the finite repeated game $G_T$ has a unique SPE: play $\sigma$ in every period, after every history. Payoffs are separable over dates with positive weights, as with a common discount factor $\delta\in(0,1]$ in a finite horizon.

Dominance, NE and SPE are defined as in other dynamic games. With several stage-game equilibria, continuation play can depend on history: a less attractive equilibrium can serve as a credible punishment, potentially sustaining an earlier action profile that is not itself a stage-game equilibrium.

ProofShow the unique-stage-equilibrium result▼
Additional explanation · proof

At period $T$, after every history, there is no future reward or punishment. The continuation must therefore use the unique stage equilibrium $\sigma$. Its payoff is independent of how that history was reached.

At $T-1$, changing the current action cannot change the already-determined continuation payoff at $T$. Best responses are thus exactly those of the stage game, forcing $\sigma$ again. Repeat the argument through periods $T-2,\ldots,1$. This proves both existence of the repeated-$\sigma$ SPE and uniqueness at every history.

Formal course

Equilibrium payoff sets

Let $E_T$ denote the set of Nash-equilibrium payoffs of $G_T$. Under a common normalized payoff convention across horizons,

$$\varnothing\ne E_1\subseteq E_T.$$

The nonemptiness statement assumes a finite stage game (mixed strategies allowed), or another hypothesis ensuring a stage-game Nash equilibrium. For finite $T$, compare weighted averages: $v_T=\sum_{t=1}^T w_tg^t$ with $w_t>0$ and $\sum_t w_t=1$. With discounting, $w_t=\delta^{t-1}/\sum_{k=1}^T\delta^{k-1}$.

The normalization matters: an unnormalized sum of $T$ identical stage payoffs is $T$ times that payoff when $\delta=1$, so the literal inclusion of payoff vectors would not follow under that convention.

ProofShow why every stage-equilibrium payoff remains available▼
Additional explanation · proof

Take any stage-game equilibrium $\sigma$. Prescribe $\sigma$ every period after every history, independently of past actions. No unilateral deviation can affect future opponents' behavior. Each period's equilibrium action is a best response, so adding the corresponding inequalities with positive weights rules out any profitable deviation over the repeated game. The profile is an SPE, hence a NE.

If its stage payoff is $u$, its normalized repeated payoff is $\sum_tw_tu=u$. Thus every $u\in E_1$ belongs to $E_T$. At least one such $u$ exists under the stated existence assumptions.

Formal course

Infinite horizons and discussion

An infinite repetition has no final period. The finite backward-induction argument therefore cannot simply be started “at infinity.” Discounted continuation values and credible responses after every history are needed.
