Exercise 1

Nash Equilibria and Dominance

The Game

Player 1 chooses a row and Player 2 chooses a column. In each cell, the first number is Player 1’s payoff and the second is Player 2’s.

Player 1: rows · Player 2: columns
$A_2$$B_2$$C_2$
$A_1$$(a,b)$$(c,d)$$(e,f)$
$B_1$$(g,h)$$(i,j)$$(k,l)$

Determine the conditions on the parameters in each question.

Exercise 1 · Question 1
When is $(A_1,C_2)$ a Nash equilibrium?
1
Check Player 1’s possible deviation.

Keep Player 2’s column fixed at $C_2$. Player 1 receives $e$ from $A_1$ and would receive $k$ by switching to $B_1$. Staying at $A_1$ must be at least as good:

$$e\geq k.$$
2
Check Player 2’s possible deviations.

Keep Player 1’s row fixed at $A_1$. Player 2 receives $f$ from $C_2$, compared with $b$ from $A_2$ and $d$ from $B_2$:

$$f\geq b,\qquad f\geq d.$$
✓
Combine the conditions.
$$\boxed{(A_1,C_2)\in NE_{\mathrm{pure}}\iff e\geq k,\quad f\geq b,\quad f\geq d.}$$

Equality is allowed: an indifferent player has no profitable deviation.

Exercise 1 · Question 2
When is $A_1$ strictly dominant for Player 1?
1
Compare the two rows in every column.

Read only the first payoff in each cell. The comparison must favor $A_1$ whatever Player 2 chooses.

Player 2 choosesPayoff from $A_1$Payoff from $B_1$Required
$A_2$$a$$g$$a>g$
$B_2$$c$$i$$c>i$
$C_2$$e$$k$$e>k$
✓
Require all three strict inequalities.
$$\boxed{A_1\text{ is strictly dominant}\iff a>g,\quad c>i,\quad e>k.}$$

Unlike the Nash check in Question 1, dominance compares the strategies against every opposing action.

Exercise 1 · Question 3
When is $B_2$ dominant for Player 2?
1
Compare columns using Player 2’s payoffs.

“Dominant” here means at least as good as every alternative, for every opposing action.

  • If Player 1 chooses $A_1$, column $B_2$ gives $d$, compared with $b$ and $f$. We need $d\geq b$ and $d\geq f$.
  • If Player 1 chooses $B_1$, column $B_2$ gives $j$, compared with $h$ and $l$. We need $j\geq h$ and $j\geq l$.
✓
State the four conditions.
$$\boxed{B_2\text{ is dominant}\iff d\geq b,\quad d\geq f,\quad j\geq h,\quad j\geq l.}$$

These conditions allow ties. Strict dominance would require all four inequalities to be strict.

Exercise 2

Bertrand Competition

The Game

Two firms sell the same good to one consumer. Each firm simultaneously sets a price. The consumer buys from the cheaper firm; if prices are equal, each firm is chosen with probability $1/2$.

One unit is sold, production costs are zero, and any non-negative price is allowed. Thus a sale at price $s_i$ gives firm $i$ a profit of $s_i$.

Exercise 2 · Question 1
Formulate this situation as a strategic game.
1
Specify the players and their strategies.

The players are firms $1$ and $2$. Firm $i$’s strategy $s_i$ is its price:

$$S_1=S_2=\mathbb R_+=[0,\infty).$$

Prices are continuous: a firm can undercut a positive rival price by an arbitrarily small amount.

2
Write profit for each possible price comparison.

Let $j\neq i$. Firm $i$ sells with certainty if it is cheaper, sells nothing if it is more expensive, and sells with probability $1/2$ in a tie:

$$g_i(s_i,s_j)= \begin{cases} s_i,& s_i<s_j,\\ 0,& s_i>s_j,\\ \dfrac{s_i}{2},& s_i=s_j. \end{cases}$$

The normal-form game is $\Gamma=(\{1,2\},S_1\times S_2,(g_1,g_2))$.

Exercise 2 · Question 2
Find all Nash equilibria in pure strategies.
1
Best responses to a zero price.

If the rival sets $s_j=0$, charging zero gives profit $0$, and any higher price also gives $0$ because the rival gets the sale. Every non-negative price is a best response:

$$BR_i(0)=\mathbb R_+.$$
2
Show why no best response exists to a positive price.

Fix a rival price $s_j=q>0$. Every own price $r\geq0$ can be improved:

  • If $r<q$: firm $i$ wins the sale, but can charge $r'=(r+q)/2$. Since $r<r'<q$, it still wins and earns more.
  • If $r=q$: the tie gives $q/2$. Charging $r'=3q/4$ wins for sure and gives $3q/4>q/2$.
  • If $r>q$: the firm earns $0$. Charging $r'=q/2$ wins and gives a positive profit.

Profits can approach $q$ from below, but no price attains that profit. Hence:

$$BR_i(s_j)=\begin{cases} \mathbb R_+,&s_j=0,\\ \varnothing,&s_j>0. \end{cases}$$
3
Find mutual best responses.

If either firm charges a positive price, the other firm has no best response, so the profile cannot be a Nash equilibrium. At $(0,0)$, neither firm can obtain a positive profit by changing its price.

$$\boxed{NE_{\mathrm{pure}}=\{(0,0)\},\qquad (g_1,g_2)=(0,0).}$$

A firm can be indifferent among many responses to zero, yet only the profile with both prices zero is a pair of mutual best responses.

Exercise 3

Free Riding

The Game

There are $N$ players. Each simultaneously chooses an integer contribution $s_i$ from the 51 integers $\{0,1,\ldots,50\}$. Player $i$’s payoff is:

$$g_i(s_1,\ldots,s_N)=\frac{13}{10}(50-s_i)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}s_j.$$
Exercise 3 · Question 1
Interpret the payoff function.
1
Separate private resources from the public benefit.

Player $i$ starts with $50$ units. She contributes $s_i$ to a public good and keeps $50-s_i$. Each unit kept yields a private payoff of $13/10$. Every unit contributed, by anyone, yields a benefit of $1$ to each player.

2
Isolate the effect of the player’s own contribution.
$$\begin{aligned} g_i(s_i,s_{-i}) &=\frac{13}{10}(50-s_i)+s_i+\sum_{j\neq i}s_j\\ &=65-\frac{3}{10}s_i+\sum_{j\neq i}s_j. \end{aligned}$$

Contributing one extra unit costs the player $13/10$ in private payoff and returns only $1$ to her, so her net payoff falls by $3/10$. An extra unit contributed by someone else raises her payoff by $1$.

This creates the incentive to free ride: benefit from others’ contributions while keeping one’s own resources.

Exercise 3 · Question 2
For $N=2$, restrict each player’s actions to $\{0,50\}$. Write the payoff matrix and identify the type of game.
1
Calculate all four outcomes.
  • $(0,0)$: each player keeps all $50$ units and gets $(13/10)\times50=65$.
  • $(0,50)$: Player 1 gets $65+50=115$; Player 2 gets $0+50=50$.
  • $(50,0)$: the payoffs are reversed, $(50,115)$.
  • $(50,50)$: the total contribution is $100$ and neither player keeps anything, so each gets $100$.
Player 1: rows · Player 2: columns · Payoffs: (Player 1, Player 2)
$0$$50$
$0$$(65,65)$$(115,50)$
$50$$(50,115)$$(100,100)$
2
Identify the prisoner’s dilemma.

For either player, choosing $0$ is strictly better whatever the other does: $65>50$ against $0$, and $115>100$ against $50$. Thus $0$ is strictly dominant and $(0,0)$ is the unique Nash equilibrium.

Yet both prefer $(50,50)$, where each gets $100$ instead of $65$. Individual incentives lead to an outcome that both would prefer to replace with mutual contribution: this is a prisoner’s dilemma.

Exercise 3 · Question 3
In the general game, determine all Nash equilibria in pure strategies. Comment on the outcome.
1
Show that zero is strictly dominant.

Fix any contributions $s_{-i}$ by the other players. For every $s_i>0$:

$$g_i(0,s_{-i})-g_i(s_i,s_{-i})=\frac{3}{10}s_i>0.$$

Therefore every player strictly prefers $0$ to any positive contribution, regardless of the others’ choices.

$$\boxed{NE_{\mathrm{pure}}=\{(0,\ldots,0)\},\qquad g_i=65\text{ for every }i.}$$
2
Compare the equilibrium with total welfare.

Sum the players’ payoffs. Each unit contributed benefits all $N$ players:

$$\begin{aligned} W(s)&=\sum_{i=1}^{N}g_i(s)\\ &=65N-\frac{13}{10}\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_i+N\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_i\\ &=65N+\left(N-\frac{13}{10}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_i. \end{aligned}$$

For $N\geq2$, the coefficient is positive. Total welfare is therefore maximized when everyone contributes the maximum, $50$:

$$\boxed{s^{\mathrm{opt}}=(50,\ldots,50),\qquad g_i(s^{\mathrm{opt}})=50N.}$$

Since $50N>65$ for $N\geq2$, everyone would be better off at full contribution. A player still wants to reduce her own contribution while keeping the benefits from others, so that profile is not an equilibrium.

For $N=1$, there is no benefit to other players: zero contribution is both individually and socially optimal.

Exercise 4

Majority Judgment

The Game

$N$ judges simultaneously assign integer scores $s_i\in\{0,1,\ldots,10\}$ to a figure skater. The final score is $x(s_1,\ldots,s_N)$. Judge $i$’s preferred score is $a_i\in\{0,1,\ldots,10\}$, and her payoff is:

$$g_i(s_1,\ldots,s_N)=-\left|a_i-x(s_1,\ldots,s_N)\right|.$$

A judge wants the final score as close as possible to her own opinion. The highest possible payoff is $0$, attained when $x=a_i$.

Exercise 4 · Question 1
Suppose $x(s)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_i$. Give values of $N$ and $(a_1,\ldots,a_N)$ for which no player has a dominant strategy.
1
Choose two judges who both want a final score of five.
$$N=2,\qquad a_1=a_2=5,\qquad x(s_1,s_2)=\frac{s_1+s_2}{2}.$$

For either judge, a best response makes the average equal to $5$. Given the other score $s_j$, this requires:

$$\frac{s_i+s_j}{2}=5\iff s_i=10-s_j.$$

This is always an allowed integer score. It uniquely gives payoff $0$; every other score gives a negative payoff.

2
Compare best responses to two opposing scores.
$$BR_i(0)=\{10\},\qquad BR_i(10)=\{0\}.$$

A dominant strategy would have to be a best response to both $s_j=0$ and $s_j=10$. These best-response sets are disjoint, so no such strategy exists. The same argument applies to each judge.

Conclusion: neither player has a dominant strategy, even though their preferred scores are identical.

Exercise 4 · Question 2
Now let $N$ be odd and let $x(s)$ be the median of the reported scores. Show that an equilibrium in dominant strategies exists.
1
Fix the other reports and consider truthful reporting.

Fix a judge $i$ and any reports $s_{-i}$ by the other judges. Write

$$M(r)=x(s_1,\ldots,r,\ldots,s_N),\qquad m=M(a_i).$$

Thus $m$ is the median when judge $i$ reports her actual opinion. We show that no alternative report $r$ brings the final score closer to $a_i$.

Use two properties of the median:

  • Increasing one reported score cannot decrease the median; decreasing it cannot increase the median.
  • A report strictly above the median can be raised further without changing the median. Similarly, a report strictly below it can be lowered further without changing it. In either case the middle score is determined by the other reports.
2
If the truthful median equals the preferred score.

If $m=a_i$, truthful reporting gives payoff $0$, the largest possible payoff. No deviation can improve it:

$$g_i(a_i,s_{-i})=0\geq -|a_i-M(r)|=g_i(r,s_{-i}).$$
3
If the truthful median lies below the preferred score.

Suppose $m<a_i$.

  • If $r\leq a_i$, monotonicity gives $M(r)\leq M(a_i)=m$.
  • If $r>a_i$, the truthful report was already strictly above the median. Raising it further leaves the median unchanged: $M(r)=m$.

Thus every deviation yields $M(r)\leq m<a_i$, moving the final score no closer to $a_i$:

$$\begin{aligned} g_i(a_i,s_{-i}) &=-|a_i-m|=-a_i+m\\ &\geq -a_i+M(r)\\ &=-|a_i-M(r)|=g_i(r,s_{-i}). \end{aligned}$$
4
If the truthful median lies above the preferred score.

Suppose $m>a_i$.

  • If $r\geq a_i$, monotonicity gives $M(r)\geq m$.
  • If $r<a_i$, lowering a report already strictly below the median leaves it unchanged: $M(r)=m$.

Therefore every deviation yields $M(r)\geq m>a_i$, and:

$$\begin{aligned} g_i(a_i,s_{-i}) &=-|a_i-m|=a_i-m\\ &\geq a_i-M(r)\\ &=-|a_i-M(r)|=g_i(r,s_{-i}). \end{aligned}$$
✓
Conclude for every judge.

In all three cases, truthful reporting is at least as good as any deviation, for any reports by the others. Thus $s_i=a_i$ is a weakly dominant strategy for every judge.

$$\boxed{s^*=(a_1,\ldots,a_N)\text{ is an equilibrium in dominant strategies.}}$$

It is therefore a Nash equilibrium, with final score $\operatorname{med}(a_1,\ldots,a_N)$. Dominance is weak: some other reports can leave the median, and hence the payoff, unchanged.

Exercise 5

War of Attrition

The Game

Two players dispute an object. Player $i$ values it at $v_i>0$. Time is continuous, starting at $0$, and each player chooses a concession time $t_i\geq0$.

The first player to concede loses the object; the other obtains it immediately. If both concede at the same time, each receives half of the object’s value. Until the first concession, each player pays a waiting cost of $c>0$ per unit of time.

Exercise 5 · Question 1
Formulate this situation as a strategic game.
1
Specify the choices and the end of the dispute.

The two players simultaneously choose finite non-negative concession times:

$$S_1=S_2=[0,\infty).$$

The dispute ends at $\min\{t_i,t_j\}$. Both players’ waiting costs stop at that time, including the winner’s.

2
Write the payoff in each case.
$$g_i(t_i,t_j)= \begin{cases} v_i-ct_j,&t_i>t_j\quad\text{(wins)},\\ -ct_i,&t_i<t_j\quad\text{(concedes first)},\\ \dfrac{v_i}{2}-ct_i,&t_i=t_j\quad\text{(tie)}. \end{cases}$$

If Player $i$ plans to wait longer than the other player, she wins at time $t_j$ and pays $ct_j$, regardless of how much larger $t_i$ is.

Exercise 5 · Question 2
Show that in every pure-strategy Nash equilibrium, one player concedes immediately.
1
Rule out different, strictly positive concession times.

If $0<t_1<t_2$, Player 1 concedes first and gets $-ct_1<0$. Switching to $t_1'=0$ gives $0$, a strict improvement.

If $0<t_2<t_1$, the same argument applies to Player 2. Thus no unequal pair of positive times is an equilibrium.

2
Rule out a tie at a positive time.

If $t_1=t_2=t>0$, Player $i$ gets $v_i/2-ct$. By choosing $t_i'=t+\varepsilon$, for any $\varepsilon>0$, she lets the other player concede first at time $t$ and gets:

$$g_i(t+\varepsilon,t)=v_i-ct>\frac{v_i}{2}-ct=g_i(t,t).$$

The gain is $v_i/2$. There is no extra waiting cost: the dispute still ends at time $t$.

3
Check the zero-time tie.

At $(0,0)$, each player gets $v_i/2$. Choosing any positive time instead wins the whole object immediately when the other player concedes at $0$, giving $v_i>v_i/2$.

Conclusion: every pure Nash equilibrium has exactly one zero concession time:

$$\boxed{(t_1,t_2)=(0,t_2),\ t_2>0,\quad\text{or}\quad(t_1,0),\ t_1>0.}$$
Exercise 5 · Question 3
Find all Nash equilibria in pure strategies.
1
Find the necessary condition for a profile of the form $(0,t_2)$.

Let $t_2>0$. Player 1 concedes immediately and earns $0$; Player 2 gets the object and earns $v_2$.

If $t_2<v_1/c$, Player 1 can instead choose any $t_1'>t_2$, win the object, and earn $v_1-ct_2>0$. Therefore a necessary condition is:

$$t_2\geq\frac{v_1}{c}.$$
2
Check every deviation by Player 1 when this condition holds.

Assume $t_2\geq v_1/c$. Player 1’s current payoff is $0$.

$t_1'=0$$0$Unchanged

No deviation is profitable. The boundary $t_2=v_1/c$ is included: winning after waiting until $t_2$ would give exactly $0$, so Player 1 is indifferent.

3
Check Player 2 and exchange the players’ roles.

Against $t_1=0$, any positive $t_2'$ gives Player 2 the full value $v_2$ with no waiting cost. Choosing $t_2'=0$ would give only $v_2/2$. Player 2 therefore has no profitable deviation.

Thus $(0,t_2)$ is an equilibrium exactly when $t_2\geq v_1/c$. Exchanging the players gives equilibria $(t_1,0)$ exactly when $t_1\geq v_2/c$.

✓
State all pure equilibria and their payoffs.
$$\boxed{\begin{aligned} NE_{\mathrm{pure}}={}&\left\{(0,t_2):t_2\geq\frac{v_1}{c}\right\}\\ &\cup\left\{(t_1,0):t_1\geq\frac{v_2}{c}\right\}. \end{aligned}}$$ $$\begin{aligned} (0,t_2)&\longmapsto(0,v_2),\\ (t_1,0)&\longmapsto(v_1,0). \end{aligned}$$

The winner’s announced waiting time must be long enough to make waiting for the object unprofitable for the loser. In equilibrium, the loser concedes at once, so neither player actually incurs a waiting cost.
