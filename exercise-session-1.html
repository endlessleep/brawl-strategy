Exercise 1

Nash Equilibria and Dominance

The Game

Player 1 chooses a row and Player 2 chooses a column. In each cell, the first number is Player 1’s payoff and the second is Player 2’s.

Player 1: rows · Player 2: columns
$A_2$$B_2$$C_2$
$A_1$$(a,b)$$(c,d)$$(e,f)$
$B_1$$(g,h)$$(i,j)$$(k,l)$

Determine the conditions on the parameters in each question.

Exercise 1 · Question 1
When is $(A_1,C_2)$ a Nash equilibrium?
1
Can Player 1 gain by switching?

Player 2 stays at $C_2$. Player 1 gets $e$ by staying at $A_1$, or $k$ by switching to $B_1$. Staying must give at least as much:

$$e\geq k.$$
2
Can Player 2 gain by switching?

Player 1 stays at $A_1$. Player 2 gets $f$ by staying at $C_2$, or $b$ or $d$ by switching columns. We need:

$$f\geq b,\qquad f\geq d.$$
✓
All conditions.
$$\boxed{(A_1,C_2)\in NE_{\mathrm{pure}}\iff e\geq k,\quad f\geq b,\quad f\geq d.}$$

Equality is allowed: the same payoff is no improvement.

Exercise 1 · Question 2
When is $A_1$ strictly dominant for Player 1?
1
Compare the two rows in every column.

Read the first payoff in each cell. $A_1$ must give more than $B_1$ in every column.

Player 2 choosesPayoff from $A_1$Payoff from $B_1$Required
$A_2$$a$$g$$a>g$
$B_2$$c$$i$$c>i$
$C_2$$e$$k$$e>k$
✓
All three comparisons must hold.
$$\boxed{A_1\text{ is strictly dominant}\iff a>g,\quad c>i,\quad e>k.}$$

Here we check every column. In Question 1, Player 2’s column stayed fixed at $C_2$.

Exercise 1 · Question 3
When is $B_2$ dominant for Player 2?
1
Read the second payoff in each cell.

$B_2$ must give at least as much as both other columns, in each row.

  • In row $A_1$: compare $d$ with $b$ and $f$. We need $d\geq b$ and $d\geq f$.
  • In row $B_1$: compare $j$ with $h$ and $l$. We need $j\geq h$ and $j\geq l$.
✓
State the four conditions.
$$\boxed{B_2\text{ is dominant}\iff d\geq b,\quad d\geq f,\quad j\geq h,\quad j\geq l.}$$

Ties are allowed. For strict dominance, replace every $\geq$ with $>$.

Exercise 2

Bertrand Competition

The Game

Two firms sell the same good to one consumer. Each firm simultaneously sets a price. The consumer buys from the cheaper firm; if prices are equal, each firm is chosen with probability $1/2$.

One unit is sold, production costs are zero, and any non-negative price is allowed. Thus a sale at price $s_i$ gives firm $i$ a profit of $s_i$.

Exercise 2 · Question 1
Formulate this situation as a strategic game.
1
Who plays, and what do they choose?

Players: firms $1$ and $2$. Each chooses a price $s_i$:

$$S_1=S_2=\mathbb R_+=[0,\infty).$$

Any non-negative price is allowed; there is no smallest positive price cut.

2
Who makes the sale?

Firm $j$ is the rival. Firm $i$ sells if cheaper, sells nothing if dearer, and has a $1/2$ chance of selling at equal prices:

$$g_i(s_i,s_j)= \begin{cases} s_i,& s_i<s_j,\\ 0,& s_i>s_j,\\ \dfrac{s_i}{2},& s_i=s_j. \end{cases}$$

The normal-form game is $\Gamma=(\{1,2\},S_1\times S_2,(g_1,g_2))$.

Exercise 2 · Question 2
Find all Nash equilibria in pure strategies.
1
If the rival charges zero.

If $s_j=0$, charging zero earns $0$. Charging more loses the sale and also earns $0$. Every price therefore gives the same payoff:

$$BR_i(0)=\mathbb R_+.$$
2
If the rival charges a positive price.

Let the rival charge $q>0$. Whatever price $r\geq0$ we choose, we can do better:

  • If $r<q$: we sell, but can raise our price to $r'=(r+q)/2$. Since $r<r'<q$, we still sell and earn more.
  • If $r=q$: we earn $q/2$ on average. Charging $r'=3q/4$ wins for sure and gives $3q/4>q/2$.
  • If $r>q$: we earn $0$. Charging $r'=q/2$ wins and gives a positive profit.

We can earn almost $q$, but never $q$. No price is best: there is always a better one.

$$BR_i(s_j)=\begin{cases} \mathbb R_+,&s_j=0,\\ \varnothing,&s_j>0. \end{cases}$$
3
Can either firm gain by changing its price?

If either price is positive, the other firm can improve its choice, as shown above. At $(0,0)$, neither firm can earn more by changing its price.

$$\boxed{NE_{\mathrm{pure}}=\{(0,0)\},\qquad (g_1,g_2)=(0,0).}$$

Both firms must be best responding at the same time. This happens only when both prices are zero.

Exercise 3

Free Riding

The Game

There are $N$ players. Each simultaneously chooses an integer contribution $s_i$ from the 51 integers $\{0,1,\ldots,50\}$. Player $i$’s payoff is:

$$g_i(s_1,\ldots,s_N)=\frac{13}{10}(50-s_i)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}s_j.$$
Exercise 3 · Question 1
Interpret the payoff function.
1
What does each term mean?

Player $i$ has $50$ units: she contributes $s_i$ and keeps $50-s_i$. Each unit kept gives her $13/10$. Each unit contributed gives every player a benefit of $1$.

2
Does contributing more help the contributor?
$$\begin{aligned} g_i(s_i,s_{-i}) &=\frac{13}{10}(50-s_i)+s_i+\sum_{j\neq i}s_j\\ &=65-\frac{3}{10}s_i+\sum_{j\neq i}s_j. \end{aligned}$$

Giving one extra unit means losing $13/10$ and gaining $1$: a net loss of $3/10$. If someone else gives that unit, she gains $1$ without paying the cost.

Free riding means keeping your own units while benefiting from what others give.

Exercise 3 · Question 2
For $N=2$, restrict each player’s actions to $\{0,50\}$. Write the payoff matrix and identify the type of game.
1
Calculate the payoff in each cell.
  • $(0,0)$: each player keeps all $50$ units and gets $(13/10)\times50=65$.
  • $(0,50)$: Player 1 gets $65+50=115$; Player 2 gets $0+50=50$.
  • $(50,0)$: the payoffs are reversed, $(50,115)$.
  • $(50,50)$: both give all their units; each receives the total contribution, $100$.
Player 1: rows · Player 2: columns · Payoffs: (Player 1, Player 2)
$0$$50$
$0$$(65,65)$$(115,50)$
$50$$(50,115)$$(100,100)$
2
Why is this a prisoner’s dilemma?

Each player gains by choosing $0$: $65>50$ if the other gives $0$, and $115>100$ if the other gives $50$. So $0$ is strictly dominant and $(0,0)$ is the unique NE.

At $(50,50)$, both would get $100$ instead of $65$. Each wants to give nothing, although both would gain from contributing together: a prisoner’s dilemma.

Exercise 3 · Question 3
In the general game, determine all Nash equilibria in pure strategies. Comment on the outcome.
1
Why does everyone choose zero?

Keep everyone else’s contributions fixed. Compare giving nothing with giving any $s_i>0$:

$$g_i(0,s_{-i})-g_i(s_i,s_{-i})=\frac{3}{10}s_i>0.$$

The difference is positive: giving nothing always pays more.

$$\boxed{NE_{\mathrm{pure}}=\{(0,\ldots,0)\},\qquad g_i=65\text{ for every }i.}$$
2
What would be best for the group?

$W$ is the sum of everyone’s payoffs. Each unit given benefits all $N$ players:

$$\begin{aligned} W(s)&=\sum_{i=1}^{N}g_i(s)\\ &=65N-\frac{13}{10}\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_i+N\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_i\\ &=65N+\left(N-\frac{13}{10}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_i. \end{aligned}$$

For $N\geq2$, $N-13/10>0$: more contributions raise the total payoff. The maximum occurs when everyone gives $50$:

$$\boxed{s^{\mathrm{opt}}=(50,\ldots,50),\qquad g_i(s^{\mathrm{opt}})=50N.}$$

For $N\geq2$, $50N>65$: everyone would gain. But each person would still gain more by reducing her own contribution. So full contribution is not an NE.

For $N=1$, nobody else benefits; giving zero also maximizes the total payoff.

Exercise 4

Majority Judgment

The Game

$N$ judges simultaneously assign integer scores $s_i\in\{0,1,\ldots,10\}$ to a figure skater. The final score is $x(s_1,\ldots,s_N)$. Judge $i$’s preferred score is $a_i\in\{0,1,\ldots,10\}$, and her payoff is:

$$g_i(s_1,\ldots,s_N)=-\left|a_i-x(s_1,\ldots,s_N)\right|.$$

A judge wants the final score as close as possible to her own opinion. The highest possible payoff is $0$, attained when $x=a_i$.

Exercise 4 · Question 1
Suppose $x(s)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_i$. Give values of $N$ and $(a_1,\ldots,a_N)$ for which no player has a dominant strategy.
1
Take two judges who both want a final score of 5.
$$N=2,\qquad a_1=a_2=5,\qquad x(s_1,s_2)=\frac{s_1+s_2}{2}.$$

Each judge wants the average to equal $5$. If the other reports $s_j$, she must report:

$$\frac{s_i+s_j}{2}=5\iff s_i=10-s_j.$$

This is an allowed integer and gives payoff $0$. Every other report puts the average away from $5$.

2
Does one report work against every choice?
$$BR_i(0)=\{10\},\qquad BR_i(10)=\{0\}.$$

Against $0$, the judge must report $10$; against $10$, she must report $0$. No single report is best in both cases. Neither judge has a dominant strategy.

Conclusion: neither player has a dominant strategy, even though their preferred scores are identical.

Exercise 4 · Question 2
Now let $N$ be odd and let $x(s)$ be the median of the reported scores. Show that an equilibrium in dominant strategies exists.
1
Keep the other scores fixed.

Judge $i$ prefers $a_i$ but may report $r$. Write

$$M(r)=x(s_1,\ldots,r,\ldots,s_N),\qquad m=M(a_i).$$

$M(r)$ is the final median after reporting $r$; $m$ is the median after reporting $a_i$. Can another report bring it closer to $a_i$?

The median is the middle score in the sorted list:

  • Raising one score cannot lower the middle score; lowering one cannot raise it.
  • Raising a score already above the middle leaves the middle unchanged. So does lowering a score already below it.
2
Case 1: the median already equals $a_i$.

If $m=a_i$, the judge gets her preferred score and payoff $0$. She cannot do better:

$$g_i(a_i,s_{-i})=0\geq -|a_i-M(r)|=g_i(r,s_{-i}).$$
3
Case 2: the median is below $a_i$.

Suppose $m<a_i$.

  • If $r\leq a_i$, lowering the report cannot raise the median: $M(r)\leq m$.
  • If $r>a_i$, we raise a score already above the median. The median stays at $m$.

The median stays the same or moves further below $a_i$: $M(r)\leq m<a_i$. So:

$$\begin{aligned} g_i(a_i,s_{-i}) &=-|a_i-m|=-a_i+m\\ &\geq -a_i+M(r)\\ &=-|a_i-M(r)|=g_i(r,s_{-i}). \end{aligned}$$
4
Case 3: the median is above $a_i$.

Suppose $m>a_i$.

  • If $r\geq a_i$, raising the report cannot lower the median: $M(r)\geq m$.
  • If $r<a_i$, we lower a score already below the median. The median stays at $m$.

The median stays the same or moves further above $a_i$: $M(r)\geq m>a_i$. So:

$$\begin{aligned} g_i(a_i,s_{-i}) &=-|a_i-m|=a_i-m\\ &\geq a_i-M(r)\\ &=-|a_i-M(r)|=g_i(r,s_{-i}). \end{aligned}$$
✓
Reporting your own opinion is always best.

In every case, reporting $a_i$ gives at least as much as any other report, whatever the others choose. Thus $s_i=a_i$ is weakly dominant.

$$\boxed{s^*=(a_1,\ldots,a_N)\text{ is an equilibrium in dominant strategies.}}$$

This is an NE with final score $\operatorname{med}(a_1,\ldots,a_N)$. “Weakly” means other reports may tie: changing a report need not change the median.

Exercise 5

War of Attrition

The Game

Two players dispute an object. Player $i$ values it at $v_i>0$. Time is continuous, starting at $0$, and each player chooses a concession time $t_i\geq0$.

The first player to concede loses the object; the other obtains it immediately. If both concede at the same time, each receives half of the object’s value. Until the first concession, each player pays a waiting cost of $c>0$ per unit of time.

Exercise 5 · Question 1
Formulate this situation as a strategic game.
1
Choose when to give up.

Each player chooses a finite time $t_i\geq0$ to give up:

$$S_1=S_2=[0,\infty).$$

The first concession ends the dispute at $\min\{t_i,t_j\}$. From then on, neither player pays any waiting cost.

2
Win, lose or tie?
$$g_i(t_i,t_j)= \begin{cases} v_i-ct_j,&t_i>t_j\quad\text{(wins)},\\ -ct_i,&t_i<t_j\quad\text{(concedes first)},\\ \dfrac{v_i}{2}-ct_i,&t_i=t_j\quad\text{(tie)}. \end{cases}$$

If $t_i>t_j$, Player $i$ wins when the other gives up at $t_j$. She pays $ct_j$, not $ct_i$.

Exercise 5 · Question 2
Show that in every pure-strategy Nash equilibrium, one player concedes immediately.
1
If one gives up first, why wait to lose?

If $0<t_1<t_2$, Player 1 loses and pays $ct_1$. Giving up at $t_1'=0$ instead gives $0>-ct_1$.

If $0<t_2<t_1$, Player 2 can do the same. Neither case is an NE.

2
If both wait until the same time.

If $t_1=t_2=t>0$, each gets half the object. Choosing $t_i^{\prime}=t+\varepsilon$, with $\varepsilon>0$, lets the other give up first at $t$:

$$g_i(t+\varepsilon,t)=v_i-ct>\frac{v_i}{2}-ct=g_i(t,t).$$

Player $i$ gets the whole object for the same waiting cost. The dispute still ends at $t$.

3
If both give up at time zero.

At $(0,0)$, each gets $v_i/2$. Choosing any positive time instead wins immediately: the other still gives up at $0$. This gives $v_i>v_i/2$.

So at a pure NE, exactly one player gives up immediately:

$$\boxed{(t_1,t_2)=(0,t_2),\ t_2>0,\quad\text{or}\quad(t_1,0),\ t_1>0.}$$
Exercise 5 · Question 3
Find all Nash equilibria in pure strategies.
1
When will Player 1 accept giving up at once?

Consider $(0,t_2)$ with $t_2>0$. Player 1 gets $0$; Player 2 wins and gets $v_2$.

Player 1 could choose $t_1'>t_2$ and win for $v_1-ct_2$. To prevent this gain, we need $v_1-ct_2\leq0$, or:

$$t_2\geq\frac{v_1}{c}.$$
2
Check all Player 1’s other choices.

Assume $t_2\geq v_1/c$. Player 1’s current payoff is $0$.

New concession timePayoffComparison with $0$
$t_1'=0$$0$Unchanged
$0<t_1'<t_2$$-ct_1'$Strictly negative
$t_1'=t_2$$v_1/2-ct_2$$\leq-v_1/2<0$
$t_1'>t_2$$v_1-ct_2$$\leq0$

None gives more than $0$. Equality $t_2=v_1/c$ is allowed: winning would give $0$ too.

3
Can the winner gain by switching?

Player 2 already gets $v_2$, the whole value, without waiting. Any positive $t_2'$ gives the same; $t_2'=0$ gives only $v_2/2$. She cannot gain.

Thus $(0,t_2)$ is an NE exactly when $t_2\geq v_1/c$. Switching the players’ roles gives $(t_1,0)$ with $t_1\geq v_2/c$.

✓
All equilibria and their payoffs.
$$\boxed{\begin{aligned} NE_{\mathrm{pure}}={}&\left\{(0,t_2):t_2\geq\frac{v_1}{c}\right\}\\ &\cup\left\{(t_1,0):t_1\geq\frac{v_2}{c}\right\}. \end{aligned}}$$ $$\begin{aligned} (0,t_2)&\longmapsto(0,v_2),\\ (t_1,0)&\longmapsto(v_1,0). \end{aligned}$$

The winner must be willing to wait long enough that winning would cost the loser too much. But the loser gives up at once, so neither actually pays a waiting cost.
