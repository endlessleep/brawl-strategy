Dynamic Games with Perfect Information

Modelling sequential strategic interactions and finding subgame-perfect equilibria.

03 — Worked Examples
Example 1

Entry Game

Payoffs (entrant, incumbent)
An entrant chooses $E$ — Enter or $S$ — Stay out. After entry, the incumbent chooses $F$ — Fight or $A$ — Accommodate. Staying out ends the game immediately.
Task: Write the strategy sets and normal form. Find dominance, Nash equilibria, subgames and SPE.
▶ Show Solution ▼
1

Strategies and payoff matrix

Each player controls one node, so $S_1=\{E,S\}$ and $S_2=\{F,A\}$. The incumbent must specify a response even if the entrant stays out.

Incumbent (P2)
$F$$A$
Entrant (P1)$E$$(-1,1)$$(\underline{1},\underline{2})$
$S$$(\underline{0},\underline{3})$$(0,\underline{3})$

Underlined payoffs = best responses. Shaded cells = Nash equilibria.

The two $S$ cells are identical: the incumbent's action is off path.

2

Dominance

For the incumbent, $A$ weakly dominates $F$: after $E$, $2>1$; after $S$, $3=3$. It is not strict dominance. The entrant has no dominant action.

3

Best responses

For player 1: $BR_1(F)=\{S\}$ because $0>-1$;
$BR_1(A)=\{E\}$ because $1>0$.

For player 2: $BR_2(E)=\{A\}$ because $2>1$;
$BR_2(S)=\{F,A\}$ because both give $3$.

$$NE_{\rm pure}=\{(E,A),(S,F)\}$$

At $(S,F)$, entering would give the entrant $-1$ instead of $0$. The incumbent cannot improve the realized payoff by changing the off-path response. So this is Nash, although the threat to fight is not credible.

4

Subgames

There are two: the whole game and the proper subgame starting at the incumbent's node after $E$.

5

Solve from the end

Incumbent: after entry, compare its own payoffs: $A$ gives $2$, $F$ gives $1$. Choose $A$.

Entrant: anticipate accommodation. Entry gives $1$; staying out gives $0$. Choose $E$.

$$SPE=\{(E,A)\},\qquad g(E,A)=(1,2)$$

Why reject $(S,F)$? In the off-path subgame after $E$, the incumbent has a profitable one-shot deviation $F\to A$ because $2>1$.

✓
Conclusion: Two pure Nash equilibria, $(E,A)$ and $(S,F)$; only $(E,A)$ is subgame-perfect. Equilibrium payoffs: $(1,2)$.
Takeaway: a threat can support a Nash equilibrium while failing the credibility test required by SPE.
▶ Show Mixed-Strategy Equilibria ▼
1

Expected payoff from entry

Let $p=\Pr(E)$ and $q=\Pr(A)$. Entry gives $q(1)+(1-q)(-1)=2q-1$; staying out gives $0$. If $p>0$, the incumbent strictly prefers $A$, so $q=1$ and then $p=1$.

2

Staying out in equilibrium

If $p=0$, the incumbent is indifferent. Staying out remains optimal iff $2q-1\le0$.

3

Complete equilibrium set

$$NE=\{(1,1)\}\;\cup\;\{(0,q):0\le q\le\tfrac12\}$$

The only SPE is still $(E,A)$.

Example 2

Kidnapping Game

Payoffs (State, kidnapper)
The State chooses $P$ — Pay or $A$ — Assault. The kidnapper observes this and chooses $R$ — Release or $K$ — Kill. The payoff after $(P,K)$ is $(-2,-1)$.
Task: Write complete plans, construct the normal form, and find dominance, pure Nash equilibria and SPE.
▶ Show Solution ▼
1

Strategy sets

The State controls one node: $S_{\rm State}=\{P,A\}$. The kidnapper controls two nodes, so $|S_K|=2^2=4$:

$$S_K=\{RR,RK,KR,KK\}$$
PlanAfter PAfter A
RRReleaseRelease
RKReleaseKill
KRKillRelease
KKKillKill

Read the letters in the order $(\text{after }P,\text{ after }A)$. Writing just “$R$ or $K$” forgets one decision node.

2

Fill the payoff matrix by following the path

If the State chooses $P$, read the first letter. If it chooses $A$, read the second letter. For example, $(P,RK)$ reaches $(P,R)$ and pays $(0,1)$; $(A,RK)$ reaches $(A,K)$ and pays $(-1,-2)$.

Kidnapper
$RR$$RK$$KR$$KK$
State$P$$(0,\underline{1})$$(\underline{0},\underline{1})$$(-2,-1)$$(-2,-1)$
$A$$(\underline{1},\underline{0})$$(-1,-2)$$(\underline{1},\underline{0})$$(\underline{-1},-2)$

Underlined payoffs = best responses. Shaded cells = Nash equilibria.

3

Dominance

Compare the kidnapper's payoffs: $RR$ gives $(1,0)$ across rows $P,A$; $RK$ gives $(1,-2)$; $KR$ gives $(-1,0)$; $KK$ gives $(-1,-2)$.

  • $RR$ strictly dominates $KK$.
  • $RR$ weakly dominates both $RK$ and $KR$.
  • $RK$ and $KR$ each weakly dominate $KK$.

$RR$ is weakly dominant, but not strictly dominant. The State has no dominant strategy.

4

Best responses

State:
Against $RR$: choose $A$ because $1>0$.
Against $RK$: choose $P$ because $0>-1$.
Against $KR$: choose $A$ because $1>-2$.
Against $KK$: choose $A$ because $-1>-2$.

Kidnapper:
Against $P$: $BR_K(P)=\{RR,RK\}$, both yielding $1$.
Against $A$: $BR_K(A)=\{RR,KR\}$, both yielding $0$.

$$NE_{\rm pure}=\{(A,RR),(P,RK),(A,KR)\}$$

At $(P,RK)$, the off-path threat to kill after assault keeps the State paying. At $(A,KR)$, the instruction to kill after payment is off path, so changing that instruction alone does not improve the realized payoff.

Eliminating weakly dominated strategies can remove Nash equilibria. To find all pure NE, retain $RK$ and $KR$ while checking best responses.
5

Identify the subgames

Three subgames: the whole game, the kidnapper's decision after $P$, and the kidnapper's decision after $A$.

6

Work backward at both kidnapper nodes

After $P$: releasing gives the kidnapper $1$, killing gives $-1$. Choose $R$.

After $A$: releasing gives the kidnapper $0$, killing gives $-2$. Choose $R$.

The kidnapper's full sequentially optimal plan is therefore $RR$.

7

Return to the State's choice

Given $RR$, paying gives the State $0$ and assault gives $1$. Choose $A$.

$$SPE=\{(A,RR)\},\quad \text{path }A\to R,\quad g=(1,0)$$

$(P,RK)$ fails the subgame after $A$: switching $K\to R$ raises the kidnapper's payoff from $-2$ to $0$. $(A,KR)$ fails the subgame after $P$: switching $K\to R$ raises it from $-1$ to $1$.

✓
Conclusion: Three pure Nash equilibria; the unique SPE is $(A,RR)$. The realized path is $A\to R$, with payoffs $(1,0)$.
Exercise · Mixed & Behavioral Strategies
In the kidnapping game, the kidnapper has four pure strategies: $RR$, $RK$, $KR$ and $KK$.
Task: Write the mixed and behavioral strategy sets, represent uniform randomization, and compute expected payoffs.
▶ Show Solution ▼
1

Mixed strategy: randomize over four complete plans

$$\mu_K=(x_{RR},x_{RK},x_{KR},x_{KK})$$ $$x_j\ge0,\qquad \sum_jx_j=1$$

The set is $\Delta(\{RR,RK,KR,KK\})$. Uniform mixing assigns $1/4$ to each plan.

2

Behavioral strategy: one probability at each node

Let $p=\Pr(R\mid P)$ and $q=\Pr(R\mid A)$. Then $\beta_K=((p,1-p),(q,1-q))$, with $(p,q)\in[0,1]^2$.

Uniform behavior uses $p=q=1/2$: release or kill with equal probability at either node. The mapping from a mixed plan to behavior is:

$$p=x_{RR}+x_{RK}$$ $$q=x_{RR}+x_{KR}$$

Conversely, an equivalent mixed strategy for behavior $(p,q)$ is:

$$\begin{aligned}x_{RR}&=pq\\x_{RK}&=p(1-q)\\x_{KR}&=(1-p)q\\x_{KK}&=(1-p)(1-q)\end{aligned}$$

Only one of the two kidnapper nodes can be reached. These formulas therefore preserve terminal probabilities against any State strategy. In particular, uniform behavior corresponds to $(1/4,1/4,1/4,1/4)$.

3

Expected payoff check

Let the State pay with probability $r$. The four terminal probabilities, in the tree's left-to-right order, are $rp$, $r(1-p)$, $(1-r)q$, $(1-r)(1-q)$.

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbb E[g_S]&=-2r(1-p)\\&\quad +(1-r)(2q-1)\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}\mathbb E[g_K]&=r(2p-1)\\&\quad -2(1-r)(1-q)\end{aligned}$$

With $p=q=1/2$, these simplify to $\mathbb E[g_S]=-r$ and $\mathbb E[g_K]=r-1$. If the State also randomizes uniformly ($r=1/2$), expected payoffs are $(-1/2,-1/2)$.

Uniform does not mean equilibrium. Here the kidnapper strictly prefers release at both nodes, so uniform behavior is not SPE.
Example 3

Dynamic Cournot Duopoly

Sequential quantities · Stackelberg model
Firm 1 chooses $q_1\ge0$. Firm 2 observes it, then chooses $q_2\ge0$. Both receive their profits after production. The follower can condition its quantity on the leader's choice.
Model assumptions. Both firms have the same constant marginal cost $c$, so $C_i(q_i)=cq_i$, with $a>c>0$.
$$P(Q)=\max\{a-Q,0\}$$ $$g_i(q_i,q_j)=q_i\big[P(q_i+q_j)-c\big]$$
1Leader chooses $q_1$
→
2Follower observes $q_1$, chooses $q_2$
→
✓Profits $(g_1,g_2)$

Each possible $q_1$ starts a distinct follower decision node; the tree has infinitely many branches.

Task: Write the normal-form strategy sets, find the SPE by backward induction, and explain why other Nash equilibria can exist.
▶ Show Solution ▼
1

A number for the leader; a function for the follower

$$S_1=\mathbb R_+,\qquad S_2=\{f:\mathbb R_+\to\mathbb R_+\}$$

A follower strategy gives a response $f(x)$ for every possible leader quantity $x$, including quantities not chosen in equilibrium. A single realized number $q_2$ is not a full strategy.

2

Normal-form payoffs

$$G_1(q_1,f)=q_1\big[\max\{a-q_1-f(q_1),0\}-c\big]$$ $$G_2(q_1,f)=f(q_1)\big[\max\{a-q_1-f(q_1),0\}-c\big]$$

The normal form exists, but cannot be displayed as a finite matrix. There is a proper subgame after each possible $q_1$, in addition to the whole game.

3

Solve firm 2's problem for any observed $q_1$

Where production is profitable, the follower maximizes:

$$g_2=q_2(a-c-q_1-q_2)$$ $$\frac{\partial g_2}{\partial q_2}=a-c-q_1-2q_2=0$$

The second derivative is $-2<0$. Include the non-negativity constraint:

$$f^*(q_1)=BR_2(q_1)=\max\left\{\frac{a-c-q_1}{2},0\right\}$$

When $q_1\ge a-c$, the best response is zero. Producing where the market price is zero loses $cq_2$, so that region cannot improve on zero output.

4

The leader anticipates this whole response rule

Set $d=a-c>0$. For $0\le q_1\le d$, substitute $q_2=(d-q_1)/2$ into firm 1's profit:

$$\begin{aligned}g_1&=q_1\left(d-q_1-\frac{d-q_1}{2}\right)\\&=\frac{q_1(d-q_1)}2\end{aligned}$$ $$\frac{d g_1}{d q_1}=\frac d2-q_1=0$$ $$\Longrightarrow\quad q_1^*=\frac d2$$

The second derivative is $-1<0$. For $q_1\ge d$, the follower produces zero and the leader's profit is nonpositive, whereas $q_1=d/2$ gives positive profit. Thus this is the global optimum.

5

Find the follower's realized quantity

$$q_2^*=f^*(q_1^*)=\frac{d-d/2}{2}=\frac d4$$
6

Report the complete SPE, then its outcome

$$q_1^*=\frac{a-c}{2}$$ $$f^*(x)=\max\left\{\frac{a-c-x}{2},0\right\},\quad x\ge0$$
$$Q^*=\frac{3(a-c)}4,\quad P^*=\frac{a+3c}{4}$$ $$g_1^*=\frac{(a-c)^2}{8}$$ $$g_2^*=\frac{(a-c)^2}{16}$$

The quantity pair $(q_1^*,q_2^*)$ describes the outcome. The response function specifies behavior in all off-path follower subgames as well. Under $a>c>0$, the follower's optimum at every node and the leader's optimum are unique, so this pure SPE is unique.

Numerical check: with $a=13$ and $c=1$, $q_1^*=6$, $q_2^*=3$, $P^*=4$, $g_1^*=18$ and $g_2^*=9$.
7

The follower has a weakly dominant response plan

For every leader quantity $q_1$, $f^*(q_1)$ maximizes the follower's payoff. Thus the complete plan $f^*$ does at least as well as any alternative plan against every $q_1$. It is weakly dominant. It is not strictly dominant: another plan can agree at one quantity and differ elsewhere.

There is no dominant quantity for the leader across all possible follower response functions.

8

Construct another pure Nash equilibrium

Let $d=a-c$ and consider:

$$q_1^C=\frac d3,\qquad f^C(x)=\frac d3\quad\text{for every }x\ge0$$

Leader's best response: the follower's announced plan always produces $d/3$. Maximizing $q_1(d-q_1-d/3)$ gives $q_1=d/3$.

Follower's best response at the realized node: against $q_1=d/3$, its optimal output is $(d-d/3)/2=d/3$. Changing the response elsewhere does not affect the realized payoff.

Both players are best-responding, so $(q_1^C,f^C)$ is a Nash equilibrium of the dynamic game's normal form.

9

Why it is not subgame-perfect

At the off-path node $q_1=0$, the plan prescribes $d/3$ but the follower's optimum is $d/2$. The follower would deviate there. The constant plan fails the subgame test.

Two NE exhibited: the Stackelberg SPE and the constant-response Cournot profile above. This is not an enumeration of every NE.
10

Is moving first always better?

In this particular quantity model, the leader earns $d^2/8$, compared with $d^2/9$ per firm in simultaneous Cournot; the follower earns $d^2/16$. Here there is a first-mover advantage. It is not a general theorem about dynamic games: the benefit depends on the payoffs and available responses.

✓
Conclusion: The unique pure SPE is the leader quantity $(a-c)/2$ together with the follower’s full best-response function. The realized follower quantity is $(a-c)/4$.
Revision

Master Revision Map — Dynamic Games

The 5-Step Method
  1. Read: players, timing, actions and the order of payoffs.
  2. Plan: one action for every node a player controls.
  3. Translate: build the normal form and find best responses if Nash is requested.
  4. Solve backward: optimize at every node, including off-path nodes.
  5. Report: complete SPE strategy profile → realized path → payoffs.

Four mistakes to avoid
  • Confusing one action with a complete strategy.
  • Using the wrong player's payoff when solving a node.
  • Checking only the path that actually occurs.
  • Giving Cournot's follower only a number instead of a response function.
Exercise · Revision
  1. A player controls three nodes, with two actions at each node. How many pure strategies are there?
  2. Is every Nash equilibrium subgame-perfect?
  3. In the kidnapping SPE, why specify release after payment if the State chooses assault?
Task: Answer each question and justify the answer briefly.
▶ Show Solution ▼
1

Count complete plans

There are $2^3=8$ pure strategies: one binary choice at each of three nodes.

2

Compare NE and SPE

No. Every SPE is Nash, but a Nash equilibrium may fail in an off-path subgame. The entry profile $(S,F)$ is an example.

3

Specify off-path behavior

Subgame perfection requires optimal behavior after payment too. Release must therefore be part of the kidnapper’s complete plan $RR$, even though the State chooses assault.
