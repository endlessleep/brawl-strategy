Mixed Nash equilibria

Find the Nash equilibria in which at least one player mixes, and calculate their payoffs.

Find the Nash equilibria with mixing and their payoffs.▼
1
Player 1: first try indifference.

Let $p=\Pr_1(E)$ and $q=\Pr_2(F)$:

$$\sigma_1=pE+(1-p)S,\qquad\sigma_2=qF+(1-q)A,\qquad p,q\in[0,1].$$

We seek equilibria in which at least one player mixes. First try the case where both mix, so $p,q\in(0,1)$. Player 1 must then be indifferent:

$$p\in(0,1)\Rightarrow g_1(E,\sigma_2)=g_1(S,\sigma_2).$$ $$g_1(E,\sigma_2)=q(-1)+(1-q)(1)=1-2q,$$ $$g_1(S,\sigma_2)=q(0)+(1-q)(0)=0.$$ $$1-2q=0\iff\boxed{q=\frac12.}$$

This value uses the assumption that Player 1 mixes. We still have to calculate $p$ and check that assumption.

2
Player 2: indifference gives $p=0$.
$$q\in(0,1)\Rightarrow g_2(\sigma_1,A)=g_2(\sigma_1,F).$$ $$g_2(\sigma_1,A)=p(2)+(1-p)(3)=3-p,$$ $$g_2(\sigma_1,F)=p(1)+(1-p)(3)=3-2p.$$ $$\begin{aligned}g_2(\sigma_1,A)=g_2(\sigma_1,F)&\iff3-p=3-2p\\&\iff\boxed{p=0.}\end{aligned}$$

But $p=0\notin(0,1)$: Player 1 plays $S$ with certainty. Thus there is no equilibrium where both players mix. This does not rule out an equilibrium with only Player 2 mixing.

3
Return to Player 1: check the unused action.

Since $p=0$, Player 1 no longer needs to be indifferent. We must check that playing $S$ gives at least as much as switching to $E$:

$$\begin{aligned}g_1(S,\sigma_2)\ge g_1(E,\sigma_2)&\iff0\ge1-2q\\&\iff\boxed{q\ge\frac12.}\end{aligned}$$

For $q>1/2$, Player 1 strictly prefers $S$. At $q=1/2$, Player 1 is indifferent and can still choose $S$. Both cases satisfy the best-response condition.

The first calculation found the boundary $q=1/2$. Once $p=0$ is known, replacing Player 1's provisional indifference equality with the unused-action inequality also finds every $q>1/2$ that works.

Player 2 must actually mix, so $q\in(0,1)$. Combining both restrictions:

$$\boxed{p=0,\qquad\frac12\le q<1.}$$

Candidate:

$$\sigma=(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)=\bigl(S,\ qF+(1-q)A\bigr),\qquad\tfrac12\le q<1.$$

The probabilities are valid. Player 2 is indifferent because $g_2(S,A)=g_2(S,F)=3$. Player 1's unused action $E$ gives no higher payoff than $S$. ✓ Every candidate in this range is an NE.

Completeness: the remaining possibility would be only Player 1 mixing while Player 2 is pure. Against either $A$ or $F$, Player 1 has a unique best response, so this cannot occur. Therefore the family above contains every equilibrium with mixing.

4
Equilibria with mixing and their payoffs.
$$g_1(\sigma)=g_1(S,\sigma_2)=q(0)+(1-q)(0)=0,$$ $$g_2(\sigma)=g_2(S,\sigma_2)=q(3)+(1-q)(3)=3.$$
$$\begin{array}{rcl}\text{Equilibrium }(p,q):&(0,q),\quad\tfrac12\le q<1\\&\downarrow\\\text{Payoffs:}&(0,3)\end{array}$$

Corresponding strategies:

$$\boxed{(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)=\bigl(S,\ qF+(1-q)A\bigr),\quad\tfrac12\le q<1.}$$

Only Player 2 mixes. Player 1 stays out, so the terminal payoff is always $(0,3)$. The value $q=1$ is excluded here because it gives a pure profile, treated in the pure-NE question.

Check the boundary: at $q=1/2$, Player 1's indifference does not allow $p>0$ at equilibrium:

$$g_2(\sigma_1,A)-g_2(\sigma_1,F)=p.$$

If $p>0$, Player 2 strictly prefers $A$ and must choose $q=0$, contradicting mixing. Hence $p=0$ remains necessary even at the boundary.

Task: Write the mixed and behavioral strategy sets, represent uniform randomization, and compute expected payoffs.
Describe mixed and behavioral strategies, and compute the expected payoffs.▼
1. The mixed-strategy set
$$\mu_K=(x_{RR},x_{RK},x_{KR},x_{KK})$$ $$x_j\ge0,\qquad \sum_jx_j=1$$

The set is $\Delta(\{RR,RK,KR,KK\})$. Uniform mixing assigns $1/4$ to each plan.

2. Behavioral strategies and their equivalent mixed plans

Let $p=\Pr(R\mid P)$ and $q=\Pr(R\mid A)$. Then $\beta_K=((p,1-p),(q,1-q))$, with $(p,q)\in[0,1]^2$.

Uniform behavior uses $p=q=1/2$: release or kill with equal probability at either node. The mapping from a mixed plan to behavior is:

$$p=x_{RR}+x_{RK}$$ $$q=x_{RR}+x_{KR}$$

Conversely, an equivalent mixed strategy for behavior $(p,q)$ is:

$$\begin{aligned}x_{RR}&=pq\\x_{RK}&=p(1-q)\\x_{KR}&=(1-p)q\\x_{KK}&=(1-p)(1-q)\end{aligned}$$

Only one of the two kidnapper nodes can be reached. These formulas therefore preserve terminal probabilities against any State strategy. In particular, uniform behavior corresponds to $(1/4,1/4,1/4,1/4)$.

1
Joint probabilities: multiply along each path.

Let the State pay with probability $r$. The four terminal probabilities, in the tree's left-to-right order, are $rp$, $r(1-p)$, $(1-r)q$, $(1-r)(1-q)$.

$$rp+r(1-p)+(1-r)q+(1-r)(1-q)=r+(1-r)=1.$$
2
Expected payoffs: probability × payoff, then sum.

With $p=\Pr(R\mid P)$ and $q=\Pr(R\mid A)$, expected utility is the sum of each terminal payoff multiplied by its probability. Expand the four terms, then simplify:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbb E[g_S]&=rp\cdot0-2r(1-p)\\&\quad +(1-r)q-(1-r)(1-q),\\\mathbb E[g_K]&=rp-r(1-p)\\&\quad +0\cdot(1-r)q-2(1-r)(1-q).\end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned}\mathbb E[g_S]&=-2r(1-p)\\&\quad +(1-r)(2q-1)\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}\mathbb E[g_K]&=r(2p-1)\\&\quad -2(1-r)(1-q)\end{aligned}$$
✓
Substitute the uniform probabilities.

With $p=q=1/2$, these simplify to $\mathbb E[g_S]=-r$ and $\mathbb E[g_K]=r-1$. If the State also randomizes uniformly ($r=1/2$), expected payoffs are $(-1/2,-1/2)$.

Uniform does not mean equilibrium. Here the kidnapper strictly prefers release at both nodes, so uniform behavior is not SPE.

Specify the complete strategies and the normal form.▼
1. Players, actions and observations

Firm 1 chooses $q_1\ge0$ first. Firm 2 observes that quantity and chooses $q_2\ge0$. Payoffs are ordered (firm 1, firm 2). The truncation $P=\max\{a-q_1-q_2,0\}$ prevents negative prices; marginal cost satisfies $a>c>0$.

2. Complete strategy sets

A pure strategy is a complete plan: specify an available action at every node controlled by the player, including nodes not reached on the realized path.

$$S_1=\mathbb R_+,\qquad S_2=\{f:\mathbb R_+\to\mathbb R_+\}$$

A follower strategy gives a response $f(x)$ for every possible leader quantity $x$, including quantities not chosen in equilibrium. A single realized number $q_2$ is not a full strategy.

3. The normal form with a function-valued strategy
$$G_1(q_1,f)=q_1\big[\max\{a-q_1-f(q_1),0\}-c\big]$$ $$G_2(q_1,f)=f(q_1)\big[\max\{a-q_1-f(q_1),0\}-c\big]$$

The normal form exists, but cannot be displayed as a finite matrix. There is a proper subgame after each possible $q_1$, in addition to the whole game.

Find the follower’s complete best-response function.▼

Write $d=a-c>0$. First calculate the follower’s optimal quantity at every possible observed $q_1$. The resulting function will be used for dominance, Nash and backward induction.

Where production is profitable, the follower maximizes:

$$g_2=q_2(a-c-q_1-q_2)$$ $$\frac{\partial g_2}{\partial q_2}=a-c-q_1-2q_2=0$$

The second derivative is $-2<0$. Include the non-negativity constraint:

$$f^*(q_1)=BR_2(q_1)=\max\left\{\frac{a-c-q_1}{2},0\right\}$$

When $q_1\ge a-c$, the best response is zero. Producing where the market price is zero loses $cq_2$, so that region cannot improve on zero output.

For $q_1\ge d$, any positive follower output has price below cost (or zero), so it gives negative profit; $q_2=0$ gives zero. For $q_1<d$, the quadratic optimum is positive and profitable. This verifies both branches of the response function, including the price truncation.

Identify dominance relations between complete strategies.▼
1
Leader: compare optimal quantities against different follower plans.

There is no dominant quantity for the leader across all possible follower response functions.

To verify the leader’s lack of a dominant quantity, consider two possible follower plans. Against $f(x)=0$, its unique best quantity is $d/2$. Against $f(x)=d/3$, its unique best quantity is $d/3$, where $d=a-c>0$. A quantity cannot be a best response to both plans.

2
Follower: compare complete response functions.

For every leader quantity $q_1$, $f^*(q_1)$ maximizes the follower's payoff. Thus the complete plan $f^*$ does at least as well as any alternative plan against every $q_1$. It is weakly dominant. It is not strictly dominant: another plan can agree at one quantity and differ elsewhere.

The follower’s complete best-response function is weakly dominant against every other distinct function: it never performs worse, and performs strictly better at any quantity where the functions differ because the optimal follower quantity is unique. Strict dominance would require a strict improvement at every leader quantity, which fails when the two plans agree somewhere.

Find a pure Nash equilibrium and verify both best responses.▼
1
Specify the candidate complete strategy profile.

For a general profile $(q_1,f)$, Nash requires $f(q_1)=f^*(q_1)$ at the realized node and $q_1\in\arg\max_{x\ge0}G_1(x,f)$. It does not require $f(x)=f^*(x)$ for every off-path $x$. Using the reaction function established in the preceding best-response question, the following calculation exhibits a Nash equilibrium. The subsequent backward-induction steps determine the SPE.

Let $d=a-c$ and consider:

$$q_1^C=\frac d3,\qquad f^C(x)=\frac d3\quad\text{for every }x\ge0$$
2
Leader: verify the best response.

Leader's best response: the follower's announced plan always produces $d/3$. Maximizing $q_1(d-q_1-d/3)$ gives $q_1=d/3$.

The leader’s derivative against the constant plan is $2d/3-2q_1$, and its second derivative is $-2$. Hence $q_1=d/3$ is the unique positive-region optimum. Larger outputs that drive price below cost give nonpositive profit and cannot improve it.

3
Follower: verify the best response at the realized quantity.

Follower's best response at the realized node: against $q_1=d/3$, its optimal output is $(d-d/3)/2=d/3$. Changing the response elsewhere does not affect the realized payoff.

✓
Mutual best responses and payoffs.

Both players are best-responding, so $(q_1^C,f^C)$ is a Nash equilibrium of the dynamic game's normal form.

$$G_1(q_1^C,f^C)=G_2(q_1^C,f^C)=\frac d3\left(d-\frac d3-\frac d3\right)=\frac{d^2}{9}.$$
Find the SPE, equilibrium quantities and profits.▼
1. The whole game and every follower subgame

There is one proper subgame for every observed $q_1=x\ge0$, containing firm 2’s quantity choice and its outcomes. The root adds the whole game. There are infinitely many proper subgames, even though each play contains only two decisions. SPE requires an optimal follower response at every one of them.

2. Backward induction: the leader’s maximization

The follower’s optimal continuation is already established in the follower’s best-response calculation. Backward induction now returns to the leader, who anticipates that entire response rule.

Set $d=a-c>0$. For $0\le q_1\le d$, substitute $q_2=(d-q_1)/2$ into firm 1's profit:

$$\begin{aligned}g_1&=q_1\left(d-q_1-\frac{d-q_1}{2}\right)\\&=\frac{q_1(d-q_1)}2\end{aligned}$$ $$\frac{d g_1}{d q_1}=\frac d2-q_1=0$$ $$\Longrightarrow\quad q_1^*=\frac d2$$

The second derivative is $-1<0$. For $q_1\ge d$, the follower produces zero and the leader's profit is nonpositive, whereas $q_1=d/2$ gives positive profit. Thus this is the global optimum.

The second derivative of $q_1(d-q_1)/2$ is $-1<0$. Its endpoints $q_1=0,d$ yield zero; $q_1=d/2$ yields $d^2/8>0$. For $q_1\ge d$, the follower produces zero and the leader’s price is at most $c$, so profit is nonpositive. The interior optimum is therefore global.

3. The realized follower quantity
$$q_2^*=f^*(q_1^*)=\frac{d-d/2}{2}=\frac d4$$
4. The complete SPE and comparison with Nash

Subgame perfection requires a Nash equilibrium in every subgame, including those off path. Report the complete strategies, then distinguish them from the path and payoffs they generate.

$$q_1^*=\frac{a-c}{2}$$ $$f^*(x)=\max\left\{\frac{a-c-x}{2},0\right\},\quad x\ge0$$
$$Q^*=\frac{3(a-c)}4,\quad P^*=\frac{a+3c}{4}$$ $$g_1^*=\frac{(a-c)^2}{8}$$ $$g_2^*=\frac{(a-c)^2}{16}$$

The quantity pair $(q_1^*,q_2^*)$ describes the outcome. The response function specifies behavior in all off-path follower subgames as well. Under $a>c>0$, the follower's optimum at every node and the leader's optimum are unique, so this pure SPE is unique.

Numerical check: with $a=13$ and $c=1$, $q_1^*=6$, $q_2^*=3$, $P^*=4$, $g_1^*=18$ and $g_2^*=9$.

At the off-path node $q_1=0$, the plan prescribes $d/3$ but the follower's optimum is $d/2$. The follower would deviate there. The constant plan fails the subgame test.

Two NE exhibited: the Stackelberg SPE and the constant-response Cournot profile above. This is not an enumeration of every NE.

The off-path improvement can also be calculated. At $q_1=0$, the constant plan gives $g_2=(d/3)(d-d/3)=2d^2/9$. Choosing $d/2$ instead gives $d^2/4$, a strictly positive gain $d^2/4-2d^2/9=d^2/36$.

5. Interpretation and common mistakes

In this particular quantity model, the leader earns $d^2/8$, compared with $d^2/9$ per firm in simultaneous Cournot; the follower earns $d^2/16$. Here there is a first-mover advantage. It is not a general theorem about dynamic games: the benefit depends on the payoffs and available responses.

Conclusion: The unique pure SPE is the leader quantity $(a-c)/2$ together with the follower’s full best-response function. The realized follower quantity is $(a-c)/4$.

Common mistakes: substituting the simultaneous Cournot quantity for the leader’s commitment choice, dropping $q_2\ge0$, forgetting the zero-price region, or reporting only the realized pair $(d/2,d/4)$ instead of the complete strategy $(d/2,f^*)$.

A simultaneous stage inside a dynamic game

Example 4

Player 1 chooses $S$ (stop), giving $(2,2)$, or $C$ (continue). After $C$, both players simultaneously choose $A$ or $B$ in the matrix below. Payoffs are ordered (player 1, player 2). Find the whole set of SPE.

Player 1 / Player 2AB
A$(2,2)$$(1,1)$
B$(3,1)$$(0,0)$
Find the SPE of the game with a simultaneous continuation.▼
1. Players, complete plans and the normal form

Player 1 acts at the root and, after $C$, in the simultaneous stage. A complete plan specifies both decisions, even if $S$ is chosen: $S_1=\{CA,CB,SA,SB\}$. Player 2 only acts in the simultaneous stage: $S_2=\{A,B\}$. The second player does not observe player 1's simultaneous $A/B$ choice.

Player 1 plan / Player 2AB
CA$(2,2)$$(1,1)$
CB$(3,1)$$(0,0)$
SA$(2,2)$$(2,2)$
SB$(2,2)$$(2,2)$

The $SA$ and $SB$ rows coincide because the stage is not reached. There are two subgames: the whole game and the simultaneous game after $C$. A single choice inside that simultaneous game cannot be detached as a proper subgame.

1
Player 2: strict dominance.

Player 2: against row $A$, column $A$ gives $2>1$; against row $B$, it gives $1>0$. Thus $A$ strictly dominates $B$ in the continuation matrix. Any equilibrium, including a mixed equilibrium, must put probability one on column $A$.

$$\begin{aligned}g_2(A,A)=2&>1=g_2(A,B),\\g_2(B,A)=1&>0=g_2(B,B).\end{aligned}$$
2
Player 1: best response to the surviving column.

Player 1: against column $A$, row $B$ gives $3>2$; against column $B$, row $A$ gives $1>0$. Neither row dominates the other. Against the only possible equilibrium column $A$, the unique best response is $B$.

✓
Pure and mixed Nash equilibria of the continuation.
$$NE_{\text{continuation}}=\{(B,A)\},\qquad g(B,A)=(3,1).$$

There are no other mixed equilibria: player 2's strict dominance forces $A$, and player 1 then has a strict best response.

3. Backward induction and the complete SPE

Replace the continuation matrix by $(3,1)$. At the root, player 1 compares $C:3$ and $S:2$. Since $3>2$, choose $C$.

$$SPE=\{(CB,A)\}.$$

The path is $C$, then $(B,A)$, with payoffs $(3,1)$. The root choice and the continuation equilibrium are both unique, so this is the unique SPE, including mixed strategies.

Find all pure Nash equilibria and compare them with the SPE.▼
1
Best responses in the full normal form.

In the full normal form, player 1's best response to $A$ is $CB$, and to $B$ it is either $SA$ or $SB$. Player 2's best response is $A$ after $CA$ or $CB$, and either column after $SA$ or $SB$.

$$\begin{aligned}BR_1(A)&=\{CB\},& BR_1(B)&=\{SA,SB\},\\BR_2(CA)=BR_2(CB)&=\{A\},&BR;_2(SA)=BR_2(SB)&=\{A,B\}.\end{aligned}$$
✓
Nash equilibria and comparison with SPE.
$$NE_{\rm pure}=\{(CB,A),(SA,B),(SB,B)\}.$$
$$\begin{array}{rccc}\text{Profiles:}&(CB,A)&(SA,B)&(SB,B)\\&\downarrow&\downarrow&\downarrow\\\text{Payoffs:}&(3,1)&(2,2)&(2,2)\end{array}$$

The last two profiles fail the continuation test: player 2 would switch from $B$ to $A$ after $C$. In the whole normal form $A$ only weakly dominates $B$ for player 2, because the two columns tie when player 1 stops. This differs from strict dominance inside the reached-stage subgame.

Common mistake: treating the continuation matrix as sequential changes the game. Solve its simultaneous Nash equilibrium first. Also report player 1's complete plan $CB$, not merely the initial action $C$.

Example 5

Player 1 receives payments at different times in the tree below. Write an equivalent extensive form with payments only at terminal nodes, preserving the total payoff along every play. Would behavior be the same?

Move every payoff to a terminal node. Does behavior stay the same?▼
1. Players, histories and complete strategies

Player 1 acts initially ($a$ or $b$) and after history $ad$ ($g$ or $h$), so $S_1=\{ag,ah,bg,bh\}$. Player 2 acts after $a$ ($c$ or $d$) and after $b$ ($e$ or $f$), so $S_2=\{ce,cf,de,df\}$. Each plan specifies the action at both of its nodes.

The whole game and the subtrees after $a$, $b$ and $ad$ are its four subgames. The five terminal histories are $ac,adg,adh,be,bf$. The displayed numbers concern player 1 only; they are not payoff pairs for two players.

2. All five terminal-payoff calculations

Put zero intermediate payments on the same branches, keep the same players and actions, and write at each terminal the sum along its path:

Terminal historyPayments to player 1New terminal payoff
$ac$$-1+4$$3$
$adg$$-1+2$$1$
$adh$$-1+3$$2$
$be$$-2+5$$3$
$bf$$-2+2$$0$

The transformed tree has the same decision nodes and branches; its intermediate payments are zero and its terminal utilities are those in the table. This table labels all leaves of the transformed tree. Player 2's payoffs, if specified, must be carried over unchanged; none can be inferred from player 1's numbers.

3. Why equivalence depends on time preferences

If player 1 values a path by its undiscounted sum and everyone else's preferences remain unchanged, this transformation preserves preferences in every subgame. At a given history, past payments are common to all continuations and therefore do not affect comparisons. The strategic incentives and the set of SPE are unchanged.

At $ad$, player 1 prefers $h$ to $g$ because $3>2$, equivalently $-1+3>-1+2$. We cannot solve player 2's choices, or give a complete numerical SPE, without player 2's payoffs.

Common mistake: using the displayed terminal numbers as player 2's utilities would invent information and could change the equilibrium.

How does discounting change the comparison between paths?▼

For illustration, date the initial cost at time 1, outcomes after $c,e,f$ at time 2, and outcomes after $g,h$ at time 3. Evaluate from time 1, omitting the common positive factor $1-\delta$:

$adg$$-1+2\delta^2$
$adh$$-1+3\delta^2$
$be$$-2+5\delta$
$bf$$-2+2\delta$

Alternating decisions in a partnership

Example 6

Two associates jointly produce over many periods. Each period's production is worth $2$, received in the next period and shared equally: $1$ for each associate. In odd periods associate 1 chooses whether to stay or exit; in even periods associate 2 chooses. On exit the mover sells their share for the fixed price $G$ and the partnership ends. Model the game, characterize SPE with $T$ periods, and find when always staying is an SPE.

Timing convention: at the start of a period the mover chooses; staying produces that period's output, paid next period. Exiting pays $G$ immediately to the exiting associate and prevents new production. Previously earned payments remain due. After the final production period $T$, its payment at $T+1$ is still received. Assume $G\ge0$ and a common $0<\delta<1$. These timings must be fixed before comparing values.

Model the partnership and characterize its finite-horizon SPE.▼
1. The extensive form and full strategy plans

At each active node $t$, Exit ends the game with the immediate sale payment to the mover; Stay leads to node $t+1$ and entitles each associate to $1$ at that date. The mover is $i(t)=1$ for odd $t$ and $2$ for even $t$.

A strategy specifies Stay or Exit at every period controlled by the associate, conditional on the partnership still existing. For a finite horizon, $|S_1|=2^{\lceil T/2\rceil}$ and $|S_2|=2^{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}$. Each active node starts a subgame; for $T$ periods there are $T$ such subgames, including the root.

Use unnormalized discounted present values for comparisons. Multiplication by $1-\delta$ would not affect any choice. Payments already earned before a subgame are common to its options and can be left out of the comparison.

2. The backward-induction recursion

Let $V_j(t)$ be associate $j$'s continuation value at the beginning of period $t$, excluding payments already earned. Set $V_j(T+1)=0$. If the mover $i=i(t)$ exits, new continuation values are $G$ for $i$ and $0$ for the other associate. If the mover stays, both receive $1$ next period and the game continues:

$$V_j^{\rm stay}(t)=\delta\bigl[1+V_j(t+1)\bigr].$$ $$\text{Stay is optimal}\iff\delta\bigl[1+V_i(t+1)\bigr]\ge G.$$

Work backward from $T$. With strict inequality choose Stay, with the reverse inequality choose Exit; at equality both are optimal, and randomization is possible. Carry the chosen continuation values backward. This recursion characterizes the SPE, including all tie cases when all maximizing continuations are retained.

3. Finite-horizon SPE and the always-stay condition

At $T$, there is no future decision. Staying pays $1$ next period, worth $\delta$ now; exiting pays $G$ now.

  • If $G>\delta$, the last mover exits. One period earlier, staying would yield only the next production payment $\delta$, because the next mover exits and keeps their own sale proceeds. Hence exit again. Induction implies Exit at every active node: this is the unique SPE and the partnership ends immediately.
  • If $G<\delta$, staying guarantees at least the next payment $\delta>G$ and all later values are nonnegative. Thus Stay at every node is the unique SPE.
  • If $G=\delta$, the final mover is indifferent. Multiple continuation choices can be optimal; use the recursion to retain all of them. Always staying and always exiting are both SPE, but mixed and other history-contingent continuations may also arise.

Under always staying, from period $t$ the mover receives $1$ at dates $t+1,\ldots,T+1$. Its continuation value is

$$V_i^{\rm stay}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{T-t+1}\delta^k=\frac{\delta(1-\delta^{T-t+1})}{1-\delta}.$$

The smallest such value occurs at $t=T$ and equals $\delta$. Therefore

$$\text{Always Stay is an SPE in the finite game}\iff G\le\delta.$$

With no discounting in a finite game ($\delta=1$), replace the geometric sum by $T-t+1$ and the condition by $G\le1$.

When is always staying an SPE over an infinite horizon?▼

If both associates always stay, the mover's future production receipts are $1$ next period and every period thereafter:

$$V_i^{\rm stay}=\delta+\delta^2+\cdots=\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}.$$

Exit instead yields $G$ immediately. The same comparison applies in every active subgame. Since payoffs are bounded and $0<\delta<1$, the one-shot deviation principle applies:

$$\text{Always Stay is an SPE}\iff G\le\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\iff\delta\ge\frac{G}{1+G}.$$

This characterizes the always-stay profile, not all infinite-horizon equilibria. In particular, if $G\ge\delta$, always exiting at every active node is also an SPE: a unilateral Stay followed by the next associate's Exit gives only $\delta$.

Interpretation: a finite endpoint can destroy the incentive to remain when $G>\delta$, whereas sufficiently valuable long-term receipts can sustain staying without an endpoint. Do not forget the one-period delay: replacing $\delta/(1-\delta)$ by $1/(1-\delta)$ would change the timing.

Section 7 · Repeated games

Repeated prisoner’s dilemma

Example 7

Use the prisoner's dilemma with $C$ (cooperate) and $D$ (defect), and the utility payoffs below. Construct a pure strategy profile under which players always cooperate on path. Then find the SPE of its $T$-period repetition for any $\delta\in(0,1]$.

Player 1 / Player 2CD
C$(-3,-3)$$(-10,0)$
D$(0,-10)$$(-5,-5)$
Give a complete strategy profile that cooperates on path.▼

For each player, prescribe grim trigger: choose $C$ in period 1; at every later date choose $C$ if all previous action profiles were $(C,C)$, and choose $D$ otherwise, forever. This defines behavior after every history, including histories containing one's own earlier deviation.

If both follow the plan, the path is $(C,C)$ every period. This establishes the requested cooperative strategy profile; it does not by itself establish equilibrium. An intended path and an incentive-compatible plan are different claims.

Find the SPE and payoffs for every finite horizon and discount factor.▼
1. Backward induction for every finite horizon

Stage game. Against $C$, $D$ yields $0>-3$; against $D$, it yields $-5>-10$. Thus $D$ strictly dominates $C$ for both players, and $(D,D)$ is the unique stage-game NE.

Final period $T$. There is no later punishment. Both choose $D$ after every history. Grim-trigger cooperation at a clean final history fails: a player gains $0-(-3)=3$ from switching to $D$.

Period $T-1$. Whatever happens now, period $T$ will be $(D,D)$. Its discounted payoff is the same across current actions, so each again chooses $D$. Repeating this argument reaches period 1.

$$s_i^*(h^{t-1})=D\quad\text{for every player, history and }t=1,\ldots,T.$$

This is the unique SPE for every $\delta\in(0,1]$. Even a very high discount factor cannot create a future punishment after the last period.

2. Payoffs and the distinction between finite and infinite repetition

The SPE path gives $-5$ to each player every period. Its unnormalized value is

$$-5\sum_{t=1}^T\delta^{t-1}=\begin{cases}-5(1-\delta^T)/(1-\delta),&0<\delta<1,\\-5T,&\delta=1.\end{cases}$$

The normalized weighted-average payoff is $-5$ for each player. Under the cooperative plan the on-path stage payoff would be $-3$, but that plan fails sequential optimality in the finite game.

Common mistakes: checking only the initial period, writing “cooperate always” without specifying responses to deviations, or assuming the same backward argument works over an infinite horizon. Infinite repetition has no final period; it requires a separate incentive analysis.

Support M in the first of two periods

Example 8

Propose an SPE of a two-period repeated game in which both players choose $M$ in period 1.

The stage-game matrix is unspecified. The solution therefore uses symbolic payoffs and states the conditions required for the proposed SPE.

Construct and verify a two-period SPE with M in the first period.▼
1. A complete contingent reward-and-punishment plan

Choose two stage-game Nash equilibria: a reward equilibrium $\sigma^R$ with payoff $r_i$ to player $i$, and a punishment equilibrium $\sigma^P$ with payoff $p_i$. Prescribe $(M,M)$ in period 1. In period 2, play $\sigma^R$ if the first-period profile was exactly $(M,M)$, and $\sigma^P$ after every other first-period history.

This specifies a continuation after all histories, including simultaneous deviations. Both continuations must be actual stage-game equilibria, because period 2 is the last period and its threats must be credible.

2. The derivation of every incentive constraint

For player $i$, let $m_i=g_i(M,M)$ and $d_i=\max_{a_i\ne M}g_i(a_i,M)$ be the best payoff from a genuine first-period deviation. Following the plan yields $m_i+\delta r_i$; the most profitable such deviation yields $d_i+\delta p_i$. Thus both players must satisfy

$$m_i+\delta r_i\ge d_i+\delta p_i\iff\delta(r_i-p_i)\ge d_i-m_i,\quad i=1,2.$$

If $r_i>p_i$ and the deviation gain $d_i-m_i$ is positive, this requires $\delta\ge(d_i-m_i)/(r_i-p_i)$. Check the condition for each player and require the threshold to be at most $1$ in a two-period model with $\delta\in(0,1]$.

When $r_i=p_i$, future equilibrium selection provides no incentive: one needs $m_i\ge d_i$. If $r_i<p_i$, the intended punishment rewards the deviator; use the original inequality rather than a positive-denominator threshold.

In a matrix whose coordinates are ordered (player 1, player 2), calculate the two deviation gains separately:

$$d_1=\max_{a_1\ne M}g_1(a_1,M),\qquad d_2=\max_{a_2\ne M}g_2(M,a_2).$$

The earlier $g_i(a_i,M)$ notation lists the deviator’s own action first; for player 2, vary the column while keeping player 1’s row at $M$.

3. Why these conditions prove subgame perfection

Every proper subgame begins at period 2, where the plan prescribes either $\sigma^R$ or $\sigma^P$, both Nash equilibria. At the initial node, the inequalities prevent any profitable first-period deviation. A player cannot gain by adding a period-2 deviation because the prescribed continuation is already a best response there. The finite one-shot deviation principle therefore establishes SPE.

What a numerical answer still requires: identify the two stage equilibria in the matrix, calculate $m_i,d_i,r_i,p_i$ from its cells, and substitute them into the two inequalities. Without that matrix, neither the existence of these equilibria nor a numerical discount threshold can be asserted.
