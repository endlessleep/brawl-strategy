Dynamic Games with Perfect Information

Modelling sequential strategic interactions and finding subgame-perfect equilibria.

Worked Examples
Example 1

Entry Game

Sections 1–4 · Extensive form, strategies, Nash and subgame perfection

Payoffs (entrant, incumbent)
An entrant chooses $E$ — Enter or $S$ — Stay out. After entry, the incumbent chooses $F$ — Fight or $A$ — Accommodate. Staying out ends the game immediately.
Task: Write the strategy sets and normal form. Find dominance, Nash equilibria, subgames and SPE.

1.Extensive-form game

Model the situation as an extensive-form game.
Show Correction — Q1▼

Player 1 is the entrant and chooses $E$ or $S$ at the root. Player 2 is the incumbent and chooses $F$ or $A$ only after $E$. The incumbent observes entry. Every payoff pair is ordered (entrant, incumbent).

Player 1 moves first; player 2 observes entry.

2.Pure strategies & payoff functions

Write the sets of pure strategies and the payoff functions taking strategy profiles as their argument.
Show Correction — Q2▼

$S_1=\{E,S\}$ and $S_2=\{F,A\}$. Each player has one decision node, hence two pure strategies. The payoff function on complete strategy profiles is

$$\begin{aligned}g(E,F)&=(-1,1),&g(E,A)&=(1,2),\\g(S,F)&=(0,3),&g(S,A)&=(0,3).\end{aligned}$$

The coordinates are ordered (entrant, incumbent). If the entrant stays out, the incumbent does not move.

3.Normal form, dominance & Nash equilibria

Put the game in normal form, find dominance relations (if any) and Nash equilibria, and comment.
Show Correction — Q3▼

A. Normal form

Entrant chooses a row; Incumbent chooses a column.
EntrantFA
E(−1, 1)(1, 2)
S(0, 3)(0, 3)

B. Strict and weak dominance

For player 1, $g_1(S,F)=0>-1=g_1(E,F)$, whereas $g_1(E,A)=1>0=g_1(S,A)$. Neither action dominates the other. For player 2,

$$g_2(E,A)=2>1=g_2(E,F),\qquad g_2(S,A)=3=g_2(S,F).$$

Thus $A$ weakly dominates $F$, but does not strictly dominate it. There is no strictly dominated pure strategy.

C. Pure Nash equilibria

$$\begin{aligned}BR_1(F)&=\{S\},&BR_1(A)&=\{E\},\\BR_2(E)&=\{A\},&BR_2(S)&=\{F,A\}.\end{aligned}$$
$$NE_{\rm pure}=\{(E,A),(S,F)\}.$$

The respective payoffs are $(1,2)$ and $(0,3)$.

D. Nash equilibria with mixing

Let $\sigma_1=pE+(1-p)S$ and $\sigma_2=qF+(1-q)A$. First suppose both players mix. Player 1’s indifference gives

$$\begin{aligned}g_1(E,\sigma_2)&=-q+(1-q)=1-2q,\\g_1(S,\sigma_2)&=0,\\1-2q=0&\iff q=\tfrac12.\end{aligned}$$

Player 2’s indifference gives

$$\begin{aligned}g_2(\sigma_1,F)&=p+3(1-p)=3-2p,\\g_2(\sigma_1,A)&=2p+3(1-p)=3-p,\\3-2p=3-p&\iff p=0.\end{aligned}$$

Hence no equilibrium has both players mixing. With $p=0$, only player 2 can mix. Player 1 must prefer $S$ weakly:

$$g_1(S,\sigma_2)\ge g_1(E,\sigma_2)\iff0\ge1-2q\iff q\ge\tfrac12.$$

Player 2 must actually mix, so $q\in(0,1)$. Therefore all equilibria with mixing are

$$(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)=(S,qF+(1-q)A),\qquad\tfrac12\le q<1.$$

At each of these profiles, player 2 is indifferent and player 1 has no profitable deviation. Only player 1 mixing is impossible: against either pure action of player 2, player 1 has a unique best response. The payoffs are

$$g_1(\sigma)=q\cdot0+(1-q)\cdot0=0,\qquad g_2(\sigma)=q\cdot3+(1-q)\cdot3=3.$$

E. Comment

Nash equilibrium allows an off-path threat of fighting. Subgame perfection additionally requires the incumbent’s action to be optimal after entry.

4.Subgames

Find the subgames of this game.
Show Correction — Q4▼

There are two: the whole game and the proper subgame starting at the incumbent's node after $E$.

5.Subgame-perfect equilibrium

Find the SPE and comment.
Show Correction — Q5▼

After entry, the incumbent chooses $A$ because $2>1$. Anticipating this, the entrant chooses $E$ because $1>0$. Therefore

$$SPE=\{(E,A)\},\qquad g(E,A)=(1,2).$$

The Nash equilibrium $(S,F)$ is not subgame-perfect: after entry the incumbent would deviate from $F$ to $A$. Every equilibrium with player 2 mixing also assigns positive probability to this suboptimal action after entry. The SPE is unique.

Example 2

Kidnapping Game

Sections 1–4 · Extensive form, strategies, Nash and subgame perfection

Payoffs (State, kidnapper)
The State chooses $P$ — Pay or $A$ — Assault. The kidnapper observes this and chooses $R$ — Release or $K$ — Kill. The payoff after $(P,K)$ is $(-2,-1)$.
Task: Write complete plans, construct the normal form, and find dominance, pure Nash equilibria and SPE.

1.Extensive-form game

Model the situation as an extensive-form game.
Show Correction — Q1▼

The State moves once, choosing $P$ or $A$. The kidnapper controls two distinct decision nodes, one after each State action, and can choose $R$ or $K$ at either. The word “Kidnapper” labels the player; the branch label $K$ means the action Kill.

The ransom is worth $1$ and the hostage’s life is worth $2$ to each player. The given payoffs use the normalization $g_S=1-\mathbf1_{\{P\}}-2\mathbf1_{\{K\}}$ and $g_K=\mathbf1_{\{P\}}-2\mathbf1_{\{K\}}$, where indicators equal one when the indicated action occurs. This produces exactly $(0,1),(-2,-1),(1,0),(-1,-2)$.

There are two different kidnapper decision nodes.

2.Pure strategies & payoff functions

Write the sets of pure strategies and the payoff functions taking strategy profiles as their argument.
Show Correction — Q2▼

$S_S=\{P,A\}$. The kidnapper controls two binary decision nodes. A plan lists its action after $P$, then its action after $A$, so

$$S_K=\{RR,RK,KR,KK\},\qquad |S_K|=2\times2=4.$$
$$\begin{aligned}g(P,RR)=g(P,RK)&=(0,1),\\g(P,KR)=g(P,KK)&=(-2,-1),\\g(A,RR)=g(A,KR)&=(1,0),\\g(A,RK)=g(A,KK)&=(-1,-2).\end{aligned}$$

The coordinates are ordered (State, kidnapper). Plans with the same instruction at the reached node give the same outcome, even if their off-path instructions differ.

2b.Mixed, behavioral & uniform strategies

Write the set of mixed strategies and the set of behavioral strategies for the kidnapper. Write the uniform strategy as a mixed strategy and as a behavioral strategy.
Show Correction — Q2b▼

Mixed strategies

A mixed strategy is a probability distribution over the four complete plans:

$$\mu_K=(x_{RR},x_{RK},x_{KR},x_{KK})\in\Delta(S_K),\quad x_j\ge0,\quad\sum_jx_j=1.$$

The uniform mixed strategy is $(1/4,1/4,1/4,1/4)$.

Behavioral strategies

Let $p=\Pr(R\mid P)$ and $q=\Pr(R\mid A)$. A behavioral strategy is

$$\beta_K=((p,1-p),(q,1-q)),\qquad(p,q)\in[0,1]^2.$$

The uniform behavioral strategy has $p=q=1/2$, so each node assigns probability $1/2$ to each action.

Equivalent representations

$$p=x_{RR}+x_{RK},\qquad q=x_{RR}+x_{KR}.$$

Conversely, one equivalent mixed strategy is

$$(x_{RR},x_{RK},x_{KR},x_{KK})=(pq,p(1-q),(1-p)q,(1-p)(1-q)).$$

Setting $p=q=1/2$ gives the uniform mixed strategy. For either State action, the two representations therefore induce the same terminal-outcome probabilities.

3.Normal form, dominance & Nash equilibria

Put the game in normal form, find dominance relations (if any) and Nash equilibria, and comment. Find only pure Nash equilibria.
Show Correction — Q3▼

A. Normal form

State chooses a row; Kidnapper: (after P, after A) chooses a column.
StateRRRKKRKK
P(0, 1)(0, 1)(−2, −1)(−2, −1)
A(1, 0)(−1, −2)(1, 0)(−1, −2)

B. Strict and weak dominance

For the State, $g_S(A,RR)=1>0=g_S(P,RR)$ but $g_S(P,RK)=0>-1=g_S(A,RK)$, so neither action dominates the other. The kidnapper’s payoff vectors against $(P,A)$ are

$$RR:(1,0),\quad RK:(1,-2),\quad KR:(-1,0),\quad KK:(-1,-2).$$

$RR$ strictly dominates $KK$ because $1>-1$ and $0>-2$. It weakly dominates $RK$ (tie after $P$, strict gain after $A$) and $KR$ (strict gain after $P$, tie after $A$). Also, $RK$ and $KR$ each weakly dominate $KK$. Neither $RK$ nor $KR$ dominates the other.

C. Pure Nash equilibria

$$\begin{aligned}BR_S(RR)&=\{A\},&BR_S(RK)&=\{P\},\\BR_S(KR)&=\{A\},&BR_S(KK)&=\{A\},\\BR_K(P)&=\{RR,RK\},&BR_K(A)&=\{RR,KR\}.\end{aligned}$$

These follow from the State’s comparisons $1>0$, $0>-1$, $1>-2$, $-1>-2$, and the kidnapper’s maximum payoffs $1$ after $P$ and $0$ after $A$. Intersecting the best responses gives

$$NE_{\rm pure}=\{(A,RR),(P,RK),(A,KR)\}.$$

The respective payoffs are $(1,0)$, $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$.

D. Comment

The last two equilibria prescribe killing at an unreached node. Those instructions do not affect the Nash payoff comparison, but will fail the subgame-perfection test.

4.Subgames

Find the subgames of this game.
Show Correction — Q4▼

Three subgames: the whole game, the kidnapper's decision after $P$, and the kidnapper's decision after $A$.

The two kidnapper subgames are separate. Test both even when the State’s chosen action reaches only one.

5.Subgame-perfect equilibrium

Find the SPE and comment. Use pure strategies.
Show Correction — Q5▼

After $P$, the kidnapper chooses $R$ since $1>-1$. After $A$, it chooses $R$ since $0>-2$. Its complete optimal plan is therefore $RR$. The State then chooses $A$ since $1>0$. Hence

$$SPE=\{(A,RR)\},\qquad\text{path }A\to R,\qquad g=(1,0).$$

Of the three pure NE, $(P,RK)$ fails after $A$ because $0>-2$, and $(A,KR)$ fails after $P$ because $1>-1$. These unreached nodes must also be solved optimally. All comparisons are strict, so the SPE is unique.

Example 3

Dynamic Cournot Duopoly

Sections 1–4 · Extensive form, strategies, Nash and subgame perfection

Sequential quantities · Stackelberg model
Firm 1 chooses $q_1\ge0$. Firm 2 observes it, then chooses $q_2\ge0$. Both receive their profits after production. The follower can condition its quantity on the leader's choice.
Model assumptions. Both firms have the same constant marginal cost $c$, so $C_i(q_i)=cq_i$, with $a>c>0$.
$$P(Q)=\max\{a-Q,0\}$$ $$g_i(q_i,q_j)=q_i\big[P(q_i+q_j)-c\big]$$
Task: Write the normal-form strategy sets, find the SPE by backward induction, and explain why other Nash equilibria can exist.

1.Extensive-form game

Model the situation as an extensive-form game.
Show Correction — Q1▼

Firm 1 chooses $q_1\ge0$ first. Firm 2 observes that quantity and chooses $q_2\ge0$. Payoffs are ordered (firm 1, firm 2). The truncation $P=\max\{a-q_1-q_2,0\}$ prevents negative prices; marginal cost satisfies $a>c>0$.

1Leader chooses $q_1$
→
2Follower observes $q_1$, chooses $q_2$
→
✓Profits $(g_1,g_2)$

Each possible $q_1$ starts a distinct follower decision node; the tree has infinitely many branches.

2.Pure strategies & payoff functions

Write the sets of pure strategies and the payoff functions taking strategy profiles as their argument.
Show Correction — Q2▼

Firm 1 chooses one quantity; firm 2 specifies a quantity after every possible observation:

$$S_1=\mathbb R_+,\qquad S_2=\{f:\mathbb R_+\to\mathbb R_+\}.$$

For a complete profile $(q_1,f)$, the realized follower quantity is $f(q_1)$ and

$$\begin{aligned}G_1(q_1,f)&=q_1[\max\{a-q_1-f(q_1),0\}-c],\\G_2(q_1,f)&=f(q_1)[\max\{a-q_1-f(q_1),0\}-c].\end{aligned}$$

The function $f$, rather than the realized number $f(q_1)$ alone, is firm 2’s pure strategy.

3.Normal form, dominance & Nash equilibria

Put the game in normal form, find dominance relations (if any) and Nash equilibria, and comment. Exhibit some Nash equilibria; a complete enumeration is not required.
Show Correction — Q3▼

A. Normal form

Firm 1 chooses a quantity $q_1\ge0$. Firm 2 observes this quantity and follows a complete plan $f$: after observing $q_1$, it produces $q_2=f(q_1)$. Thus

$$N=\{1,2\},\qquad S_1=\mathbb R_+,\qquad S_2=\{f:\mathbb R_+\to\mathbb R_+\}.$$

For a strategy profile $(q_1,f)$, total output is $q_1+f(q_1)$. The payoff functions are therefore

$$\begin{aligned}G_1(q_1,f)&=q_1\big[\max\{a-q_1-f(q_1),0\}-c\big],\\G_2(q_1,f)&=f(q_1)\big[\max\{a-q_1-f(q_1),0\}-c\big].\end{aligned}$$

This specifies the normal form. A finite payoff matrix cannot list all quantities and all response plans. The notation $g_i(q_1,q_2)$ denotes the profit from two realized quantities; $G_i(q_1,f)$ denotes the same profit when strategies are the arguments, so $G_i(q_1,f)=g_i(q_1,f(q_1))$.

B. Strict and weak dominance

Player 1. There is no dominant quantity, because the optimal quantity depends on player 2’s plan. For example:

$$\begin{aligned}f(q_1)=0:\quad G_1&=q_1(a-c-q_1),\\\frac{\partial G_1}{\partial q_1}=a-c-2q_1=0 &\iff q_1=\frac{a-c}{2};\\[6pt] f(q_1)=\frac{a-c}{3}:\quad G_1&=q_1\left(a-c-q_1-\frac{a-c}{3}\right),\\\frac{\partial G_1}{\partial q_1}=\frac{2(a-c)}3-2q_1=0 &\iff q_1=\frac{a-c}{3}. \end{aligned}$$

Both second derivatives equal $-2$, and both optima give positive profit. Quantities that reduce price below cost cannot improve them. Since the two unique best responses differ, no quantity is best against every follower plan.

There are nevertheless dominated quantities: $q_1=0$ strictly dominates every $q_1>a-c$, since such output makes price strictly lower than $c$ whatever firm 2 produces. It weakly dominates $q_1=a-c$: this quantity gives zero when $q_2=0$ and a loss when $q_2>0$.

Player 2. Fix the observed quantity $q_1$. Where price is positive, its profit as a function of its own quantity is

$$g_2(q_1,q_2)=q_2(a-q_1-q_2-c)=(a-c-q_1)q_2-q_2^2.$$
$$\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial g_2}{\partial q_2}&=a-c-q_1-2q_2,\\ \frac{\partial g_2}{\partial q_2}=0&\iff q_2=\frac{a-c-q_1}{2},\\ \frac{\partial^2g_2}{\partial q_2^2}&=-2<0. \end{aligned}$$

A quantity cannot be negative. If $q_1\ge a-c$, any positive $q_2$ yields a loss, whereas $q_2=0$ yields zero. Production at a zero price also yields a loss $-cq_2$. Hence the complete optimal response plan is

$$f^*(q_1)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{a-c-q_1}{2},&0\le q_1<a-c,\\[5pt]0,&q_1\ge a-c.\end{cases}$$

For every quantity chosen by firm 1, this plan gives firm 2 its maximum profit. Thus, for any alternative plan $f$,

$$G_2(q_1,f^*)\ge G_2(q_1,f)\qquad\text{for every }q_1\ge0.$$

The inequality is strict wherever $f(q_1)\ne f^*(q_1)$, because the optimal response is unique. Therefore $f^*$ is weakly dominant. It is not strictly dominant: another plan can give the same response at one quantity and differ elsewhere, giving equality at the quantity where the plans agree.

C. Find a pure Nash equilibrium

We look for an equilibrium in which firm 2’s plan is to produce a fixed quantity $k$ after every possible $q_1$:

$$f(q_1)=k\quad\text{for all }q_1\ge0.$$

Firm 2 still observes $q_1$ before acting; this particular plan assigns the same output $k$ after every observation. The value of $k$ is not yet known. This is one possible complete strategy, used to construct an NE; it is not the general best-response plan $f^*$.

1. Player 1’s best response. Holding this constant plan fixed, a change in $q_1$ leaves firm 2’s output equal to $k$. In the positive-price region,

$$\begin{aligned}G_1(q_1,f)&=q_1(a-q_1-k-c)\\&=(a-c-k)q_1-q_1^2,\\ \frac{\partial G_1}{\partial q_1}&=a-c-k-2q_1. \end{aligned}$$

For an interior best response,

$$a-c-k-2q_1=0\iff 2q_1+k=a-c.\tag{1}$$

2. Player 2’s best response. At the quantity $q_1$ actually chosen by firm 1, the prescribed output $k$ must equal firm 2’s optimal output. Using the best response derived in B,

$$k=\frac{a-c-q_1}{2}\iff q_1+2k=a-c.\tag{2}$$

3. Solve the two conditions together.

$$\begin{cases}2q_1+k=a-c,\\q_1+2k=a-c.\end{cases}$$

Subtracting (2) from (1) gives $q_1-k=0$, hence $q_1=k$. Substitution into either equation gives

$$3q_1=a-c\iff q_1=k=\frac{a-c}{3}.$$

Because $a>c$, both quantities are strictly positive. Each profit is strictly concave in the player’s own quantity in the positive-price region, with second derivative $-2$. The resulting profit is positive; producing at a price below cost cannot improve it. Therefore these are global best responses, including the non-negativity and price-truncation constraints.

4. State the complete equilibrium and its payoffs. Denote the resulting constant plan by $f^C$. One Nash equilibrium is

$$\left(q_1^C,f^C\right),\qquad q_1^C=\frac{a-c}{3},\qquad f^C(q_1)=\frac{a-c}{3}\ \text{for every }q_1\ge0.$$

The realized quantities, price and profits are

$$\begin{aligned}q_1^C=q_2^C&=\frac{a-c}{3},\\ P^C&=a-\frac{2(a-c)}3=\frac{a+2c}{3},\\ G_1(q_1^C,f^C)=G_2(q_1^C,f^C)&=\frac{a-c}{3}\left(\frac{a+2c}{3}-c\right)=\frac{(a-c)^2}{9}. \end{aligned}$$

D. Comment: Nash does not require optimality after every history

At the realized quantity, the constant plan agrees with the optimal response:

$$f^*\!\left(\frac{a-c}{3}\right)=\frac{a-c-(a-c)/3}{2}=\frac{a-c}{3}=f^C\!\left(\frac{a-c}{3}\right).$$

Firm 2 therefore cannot gain by changing its strategy when firm 1 keeps $q_1^C$. This is why the weakly dominated plan $f^C$ can appear in a Nash equilibrium: it ties with $f^*$ at the node that is reached.

However, if firm 1 had chosen $q_1=0$, firm 2 would prefer

$$f^*(0)=\frac{a-c}{2}\ne\frac{a-c}{3}=f^C(0).$$

Thus this NE is not subgame-perfect. Q3 constructs a Nash equilibrium by checking unilateral deviations while holding the other player’s complete plan fixed. Q5 additionally requires the follower’s plan to be optimal after every possible $q_1$; it yields the SPE, with realized quantities $(a-c)/2$ and $(a-c)/4$. That SPE is also an NE. The equilibrium constructed here is one example, not an enumeration of all NE.

4.Subgames

Find the subgames of this game.
Show Correction — Q4▼

There is one proper subgame for every observed $q_1=x\ge0$, containing firm 2’s quantity choice and its outcomes. The root adds the whole game. There are infinitely many proper subgames, even though each play contains only two decisions. SPE requires an optimal follower response at every one of them.

5.Subgame-perfect equilibrium

Find the SPE and comment. Use pure strategies.
Show Correction — Q5▼

Let $d=a-c>0$. Backward induction first gives the complete follower strategy $f^*(x)=\max\{(d-x)/2,0\}$. For $0\le q_1\le d$, the leader’s profit is

$$G_1(q_1,f^*)=q_1\left(d-q_1-\frac{d-q_1}{2}\right)=\frac{q_1(d-q_1)}2.$$
$$\frac{dG_1}{dq_1}=\frac{d-2q_1}{2}=0\iff q_1^*=\frac d2,\qquad\frac{d^2G_1}{dq_1^2}=-1<0.$$

The endpoints give zero profit. For $q_1\ge d$, the follower produces zero and the leader’s profit is nonpositive, so the interior solution is globally optimal.

$$SPE=\left\{\left(\frac d2,\ x\mapsto\max\{(d-x)/2,0\}\right)\right\}.$$
$$q_2^*=f^*(d/2)=d/4,\qquad P^*=a-3d/4=c+d/4.$$
$$G_1^*=(d/2)(d/4)=d^2/8,\qquad G_2^*=(d/4)(d/4)=d^2/16.$$

The follower’s equilibrium strategy is the entire function $f^*$; $d/4$ is only its realized quantity. This SPE is an NE. The constant-response NE from Q3 is not subgame-perfect because it fails to best-respond at off-path quantities.

Section 5 · Dynamic games with simultaneous moves

A simultaneous stage inside a dynamic game

Example 4

Player 1 chooses $S$ (stop), giving $(2,2)$, or $C$ (continue). After $C$, both players simultaneously choose $A$ or $B$ in the matrix below. Payoffs are ordered (player 1, player 2). Find the whole set of SPE.

Player 1 / Player 2AB
A$(2,2)$$(1,1)$
B$(3,1)$$(0,0)$
Find the SPE of the game with a simultaneous continuation.▼

The complete strategy sets are $S_1=\{CA,CB,SA,SB\}$ and $S_2=\{A,B\}$. There are two subgames: the whole game and the simultaneous continuation after $C$. In the continuation, player 2 strictly prefers $A$: $2>1$ against row $A$, and $1>0$ against row $B$. Player 1’s best response to $A$ is $B$ since $3>2$. Thus the continuation has the unique NE $(B,A)$, with payoff $(3,1)$; strict dominance also excludes other mixed NE. At the root, $C$ gives player 1 $3>2$, so

$$SPE=\{(CB,A)\},\qquad g=(3,1).$$

Both choices are unique, so this is the whole set of SPE.

Find all pure Nash equilibria and compare them with the SPE.▼
$$\begin{aligned}BR_1(A)&=\{CB\},&BR_1(B)&=\{SA,SB\},\\BR_2(CA)=BR_2(CB)&=\{A\},&BR_2(SA)=BR_2(SB)&=\{A,B\}.\end{aligned}$$

Against $A$, player 1 obtains $3$ with $CB$, exceeding $2$ in every other row. Against $B$, stopping gives $2>1>0$. Player 2 strictly prefers $A$ if continuation is reached and is indifferent if player 1 stops. Consequently

$$NE_{\rm pure}=\{(CB,A),(SA,B),(SB,B)\}.$$

The respective payoffs are $(3,1),(2,2),(2,2)$. Only $(CB,A)$ is subgame-perfect. In each stopping equilibrium, player 2’s prescribed $B$ is suboptimal in the continuation subgame.

Section 6 · Payoffs received at different times

Move dated payments to terminal nodes

Example 5

Player 1 receives payments at different times in the tree below. Write an equivalent extensive form with payments only at terminal nodes, preserving the total payoff along every play. Would behavior be the same?

Move every payoff to a terminal node. Does behavior stay the same?▼

Keep the same tree and replace intermediate payments by zero. Player 1’s new terminal utilities are

$$\begin{aligned}g_1(ac)&=-1+4=3,&g_1(adg)&=-1+2=1,\\g_1(adh)&=-1+3=2,&g_1(be)&=-2+5=3,\\g_1(bf)&=-2+2=0.\end{aligned}$$

If preferences depend on undiscounted sums, this preserves all continuation comparisons: past payments are constant within a subgame. With the other player’s preferences unchanged, the SPE set is unchanged. With time preferences, equal undiscounted sums need not imply equal utilities.

At $ad$, player 1 chooses $h$ since $3>2$. Player 2’s payoffs are unspecified, so a complete numerical SPE cannot be determined.

How does discounting change the comparison between paths?▼

Date the initial cost at time 1, the receipts after $c,e,f$ at time 2 and those after $g,h$ at time 3. Omitting the common positive factor $1-\delta$, the time-1 values are

$$\begin{aligned}v_1(ac)&=-1+4\delta,&v_1(adg)&=-1+2\delta^2,\\v_1(adh)&=-1+3\delta^2,&v_1(be)&=-2+5\delta,\\v_1(bf)&=-2+2\delta.\end{aligned}$$
$$v_1(ac)-v_1(be)=1-\delta>0\quad(0<\delta<1).$$

The two paths have the same undiscounted total at $\delta=1$, but $ac$ is preferred when $0<\delta<1$. An equivalent terminal-utility model must use these discounted stream values, not merely their raw sums.

Alternating decisions in a partnership

Example 6

Two associates jointly produce over many periods. Each period's production is worth $2$, received in the next period and shared equally: $1$ for each associate. In odd periods associate 1 chooses whether to stay or exit; in even periods associate 2 chooses. On exit the mover sells their share for the fixed price $G$ and the partnership ends. Model the game, characterize SPE with $T$ periods, and find when always staying is an SPE.

Timing convention: at the start of a period the mover chooses; staying produces that period's output, paid next period. Exiting pays $G$ immediately to the exiting associate and prevents new production. Previously earned payments remain due. After the final production period $T$, its payment at $T+1$ is still received. Assume $G\ge0$ and a common $0<\delta<1$. These timings must be fixed before comparing values.

Model the partnership and characterize its finite-horizon SPE.▼

Model

At each active period $t$, player 1 moves if $t$ is odd and player 2 if $t$ is even. Exit pays $G$ immediately to the mover and ends production. Stay creates a payment $(1,1)$ next period and continues the game. Previously earned payments remain due. A strategy specifies Stay/Exit at every controlled active node; $|S_1|=2^{\lceil T/2\rceil}$ and $|S_2|=2^{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}$.

Backward induction

Let $V_j(t)$ exclude previously earned payments and set $V_j(T+1)=0$. For mover $i=i(t)$, Exit gives $(G,0)$ in mover/other-player order; Stay gives

$$V_j^{\rm stay}(t)=\delta[1+V_j(t+1)].$$

Choose Stay if $\delta[1+V_i(t+1)]>G$, Exit if it is smaller, and either action or a mixture at equality. Carry the chosen values backward. At $T$, the comparison is $\delta$ versus $G$.

SPE

If $G<\delta$, Stay is uniquely optimal at every active node, because the next receipt alone is worth more than Exit. If $G>\delta$, the last mover exits; anticipating exit next period makes staying worth only $\delta$, so induction gives Exit at every active node. If $G=\delta$, retain all maximizing continuations in the recursion; both always Stay and always Exit are SPE.

$$V_i^{\rm always\ stay}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{T-t+1}\delta^k=\frac{\delta(1-\delta^{T-t+1})}{1-\delta}.$$

This is smallest at $T$, where it equals $\delta$. Hence always Stay is an SPE iff $G\le\delta$. For $\delta=1$, its value is $T-t+1$ and the condition is $G\le1$.

When is always staying an SPE over an infinite horizon?▼

Always staying yields one unit next period and every period thereafter:

$$V_i^{\rm stay}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\delta^k=\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}.$$

Exit gives $G$ immediately. The comparison is identical in every active subgame. With bounded payoffs and $0<\delta<1$, the one-shot deviation principle gives

$$\text{Always Stay is an SPE}\iff G\le\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\iff\delta\ge\frac{G}{1+G}.$$

This characterizes the always-stay profile, rather than every infinite-horizon SPE.

Repeated prisoner’s dilemma

Example 7

Use the prisoner's dilemma with $C$ (cooperate) and $D$ (defect), and the utility payoffs below. Construct a pure strategy profile under which players always cooperate on path. Then find the SPE of its $T$-period repetition for any $\delta\in(0,1]$.

Player 1 / Player 2CD
C$(-3,-3)$$(-10,0)$
D$(0,-10)$$(-5,-5)$
Give a complete strategy profile that cooperates on path.▼

For each player $i$ and every history $h^{t-1}$, prescribe

$$s_i(h^{t-1})=\begin{cases}C,&\text{if no player has ever chosen }D,\\D,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

At the empty history, choose $C$. This is a complete pure strategy profile: both players cooperate on path and defect forever after any defection. It specifies behavior after every history. Cooperation on path alone does not establish that the profile is an SPE.

Find the SPE and payoffs for every finite horizon and discount factor.▼

In the stage game, $D$ strictly dominates $C$: against $C$, $0>-3$; against $D$, $-5>-10$. The unique stage NE is $(D,D)$. At period $T$, every subgame therefore prescribes $(D,D)$. At $T-1$, the final-period payoff is fixed irrespective of the current choices, so both choose $D$ again. Induction gives

$$s_i^*(h^{t-1})=D\quad\text{for every }i,h^{t-1},t=1,\ldots,T.$$

This is the unique SPE for every $\delta\in(0,1]$. Each player receives $-5$ per period, hence the unnormalized value

$$-5\sum_{t=1}^T\delta^{t-1}=\begin{cases}-5(1-\delta^T)/(1-\delta),&0<\delta<1,\\-5T,&\delta=1.\end{cases}$$

The normalized weighted-average payoff is −5 for each player.

Support M in the first of two periods

Example 8

Propose an SPE of a two-period repeated game in which both players choose $M$ in period 1.

The stage-game matrix is unspecified. The solution therefore uses symbolic payoffs and states the conditions required for the proposed SPE.

Construct and verify a two-period SPE with M in the first period.▼

Let $\sigma^R$ and $\sigma^P$ be stage-game Nash equilibria with payoffs $r_i$ and $p_i$. Prescribe $(M,M)$ in period 1, then $\sigma^R$ after $(M,M)$ and $\sigma^P$ after every other history. Set

$$m_i=g_i(M,M),\qquad d_1=\max_{a_1\ne M}g_1(a_1,M),\quad d_2=\max_{a_2\ne M}g_2(M,a_2).$$

Both period-2 continuations are Nash equilibria. At period 1, neither player deviates iff

$$m_i+\delta r_i\ge d_i+\delta p_i\iff\delta(r_i-p_i)\ge d_i-m_i,\quad i=1,2.$$

These conditions establish SPE. If $r_i>p_i$, divide by $r_i-p_i$ to obtain $\delta\ge(d_i-m_i)/(r_i-p_i)$, and require both constraints within the admissible discount-factor range. If $r_i=p_i$, require $m_i\ge d_i$; if $r_i<p_i$, retain the original inequality or reverse its direction when dividing. The stage matrix is missing, so numerical equilibrium choices and thresholds cannot be specified.

Answer the three revision questions.▼
1

There are $2^3=8$ pure strategies: one binary choice at each of three nodes.

2

No. Every SPE is Nash, but a Nash equilibrium may fail in an off-path subgame. The entry profile $(S,F)$ is an example.

3

Subgame perfection requires optimal behavior after payment too. Release must therefore be part of the kidnapper’s complete plan $RR$, even though the State chooses assault.
