Section 5

Dominance in Mixed Strategies

A. Motivation — Extending the Same Comparison

We now allow random behavior when comparing strategies. A pure action may fail to be dominated by any single pure action, yet still be worse than a suitable mixture of other actions.

The mixed extension is itself a normal-form game. Dominance therefore keeps the same meaning as before: compare one strategy with another while holding the opponents’ behavior fixed. The change is that the strategies being compared can now belong to $\Sigma_i$.

Definition

Dominance and the Pure-Profile Test

Definition — Strict and Weak Dominance

A strategy $\sigma_i\in\Sigma_i$ is strictly dominated if another strategy $\tau_i\in\Sigma_i$ gives a strictly higher expected payoff against every possible mixed profile of the opponents:

$$\exists\tau_i\in\Sigma_i,\quad\forall\sigma_{-i}\in\Sigma_{-i},\qquad g_i(\tau_i,\sigma_{-i})>g_i(\sigma_i,\sigma_{-i})$$

The same alternative $\tau_i$ must work against all opponents’ profiles. We cannot choose a different dominating strategy after seeing what the opponents do.

For weak dominance, replace $>$ by $\geq$ for every profile, with a strict improvement for at least one profile. The alternative must never be worse and must sometimes be better.

Notation clarification: the displayed weak-dominance formula in the support only shows the weak inequality. We retain the “strict somewhere” condition already used in the earlier part of this course; otherwise two payoff-equivalent strategies would count as weakly dominating one another.
Proposition — Only Pure Opponent Profiles Need to Be Checked

To verify that $\tau_i$ strictly dominates $\sigma_i$, it is enough to check every pure profile of the opponents:

$$\forall s_{-i}\in S_{-i},\qquad g_i(\tau_i,s_{-i})>g_i(\sigma_i,s_{-i})$$

The analogous test for weak dominance uses $\geq$ everywhere and $>$ for at least one pure opponent profile. The compared strategies $\sigma_i$ and $\tau_i$ may still be mixed: only the opponents’ profiles are restricted to pure profiles for this test.

The reason given in the notes is multi-linearity. Against mixed opponents, the payoff comparison averages the comparisons at pure opponent profiles using non-negative probabilities. A strict improvement at every pure profile remains a strict improvement in that average.

Practical Remarks — What Do We Eliminate?

In practice, we search for pure actions dominated by a pure or mixed strategy. There are infinitely many mixed strategies as soon as a player has at least two actions, so listing and deleting distributions one by one would not simplify the description of the game very much.

Deleting pure actions directly shrinks the available action set. If $s_i$ and $t_i$ are deleted, every mixed strategy supported only on $\{s_i,t_i\}$ is also removed from the reduced game, as the notes emphasize.

For dominant-strategy equilibria, the course continues to use the pure-strategy definitions already introduced.

Qualification to the support’s remark: a strictly dominant strategy must be pure. The blanket statement “dominant strategies are always pure” needs this qualification when weak dominance and payoff ties are allowed.

$L$$M$$R$
$u$$(1,1)$$(0,2)$$(0,4)$
$m$$(0,2)$$(5,0)$$(1,6)$
$d$$(0,2)$$(1,1)$$(2,1)$
Task: Find a strictly dominated strategy in this game.
1
Look at player 2’s payoffs.
The second payoff in each cell gives the following vectors, in row order $(u,m,d)$: $$g_2(L)=(1,2,2),\qquad g_2(M)=(2,0,1),\qquad g_2(R)=(4,6,1).$$ $L$ does not dominate $M$: against $u$, it gives $1<2$. $R$ does not strictly dominate $M$: against $d$, both give $1$. A mixture may improve on $M$ in every row.
2
Use a mixture of L and R.
Let $\tau_2=\lambda L+(1-\lambda)R$, with $\lambda\in[0,1]$. Compare its expected payoff with $M$ against each row: $$\begin{aligned} u:&\quad \lambda\cdot1+(1-\lambda)\cdot4=4-3\lambda>2 &&\Longleftrightarrow\lambda<\tfrac23,\\ m:&\quad \lambda\cdot2+(1-\lambda)\cdot6=6-4\lambda>0 &&\Longleftrightarrow\lambda<\tfrac32,\\ d:&\quad \lambda\cdot2+(1-\lambda)\cdot1=1+\lambda>1 &&\Longleftrightarrow\lambda>0. \end{aligned}$$ Combining these comparisons with $\lambda\in[0,1]$ gives $0<\lambda<2/3$.
3
Choose and verify one admissible mixture.
Take $\lambda=1/2$. The mixture $\tfrac12L+\tfrac12R$ gives: $$\left(\frac{1+4}{2},\frac{2+6}{2},\frac{2+1}{2}\right)=\left(\frac52,4,\frac32\right).$$ Compare component by component with $M$: $$\frac52>2,\qquad4>0,\qquad\frac32>1.$$ Thus $M$ is strictly dominated by $\tfrac12L+\tfrac12R$. Checking the three pure rows is sufficient, even if player 1 randomizes.
Procedure

Iterated Elimination with Mixed Strategies

Definition — IESDS and IEWDS

Start from $\Gamma^1=\Gamma$. At each stage, the current game contains only the actions not yet deleted. Both the comparisons against the opponents and the possible dominating mixtures must use this current game.

1
Search in the current game.
At stage $k$, search for a pure action $s_i\in S_i^k$ dominated by a mixed strategy $\tau_i\in\Delta(S_i^k)$. Check all remaining pure profiles $s_{-i}\in S_{-i}^k$. Use strict dominance for IESDS and weak dominance for IEWDS.
2
Delete the dominated action.
Remove that action from player $i$’s set and leave the opponents’ sets unchanged: $$S_i^{k+1}=S_i^k\setminus\{s_i\},\qquad S_{-i}^{k+1}=S_{-i}^{k}.$$ Keep the same payoff numbers on the remaining profiles. Then repeat the comparison in this smaller game.
3
Stop when no further deletion is possible.
The procedure ends when the reduced game has no remaining pure action dominated in the relevant sense by a pure or mixed strategy.
Remarks — More Deletions May Become Possible

A procedure restricted to domination by pure strategies may stop even though domination by mixtures still allows another deletion. Allowing mixed dominating strategies therefore enlarges the comparisons available at each stage.

The order properties stated earlier remain unchanged: IESDS is order-independent, while IEWDS can depend on the order. For IESDS, this concerns the final reduced game after the elimination procedure has been continued until it stops.

Delete column M.

2
Reconsider player 1 in the reduced game.
Using the first payoffs, the remaining rows are $u=(1,0)$, $m=(0,1)$ and $d=(0,2)$, in column order $(L,R)$. Row $d$ alone is only weakly better than $m$, because they tie at $L$. For strict dominance, try $\tau_1=\alpha u+(1-\alpha)d$: $$g_1(\tau_1,L)=\alpha>0=g_1(m,L),$$ $$g_1(\tau_1,R)=2(1-\alpha)>1=g_1(m,R).$$ The two conditions are $\alpha>0$ and $\alpha<1/2$. With $\alpha=1/4$, the mixture gives $(1/4,3/2)$, strictly above $(0,1)$. Delete $m$.
3
Write the surviving game.
Player 1: rows · Player 2: columns · Payoffs: (player 1, player 2)
$L$$R$
$u$$(1,1)$$(0,4)$
$d$$(0,2)$$(2,1)$
Only $u,d$ and $L,R$ remain. The second occurrence of this example in the professor’s notes labels the last row $b$ rather than $d$; the payoffs are identical. We keep $d$ throughout.
4
Check that strict elimination stops.
For player 1, $u$ gives the highest remaining payoff against $L$ ($1>0$), while $d$ gives the highest against $R$ ($2>0$). A mixture of these two rows cannot strictly improve on $u$ at $L$, or on $d$ at $R$.

For player 2, $L$ gives the highest remaining payoff against $d$ ($2>1$), while $R$ gives the highest against $u$ ($4>1$). No mixture of these columns can strictly improve on the corresponding maximum.

Hence no remaining action is strictly dominated. The elimination sequence is $M$, then $m$, and IESDS stops at this $2\times2$ game. It does not reduce the game to a single profile.
