Nash Equilibrium in Pure Strategies

§ 3.1

Definitions & Best Response

A. Intuition First

A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile where nobody wants to change their own strategy ALONE.

The word alone is paramount: we evaluate only unilateral deviations. Each player holds the actions of all rivals fixed and asks: "Can I do strictly better by switching to another strategy?" If the answer is NO for every single player, the situation is strategically stable: a Nash equilibrium.

B. Formal Definition — Nash Equilibrium

A strategy profile $s^* = (s_1^*, s_2^*, \ldots, s_N^*) \in S$ is a Nash equilibrium if for every player $i \in N$ and for every alternative strategy $t_i \in S_i$:

$$g_i(s_i^*,\, s_{-i}^*) \;\geq\; g_i(t_i,\, s_{-i}^*)$$

Profitable deviation: A strategy $t_i \in S_i$ is a profitable unilateral deviation for player $i$ from profile $s$ if $g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) > g_i(s_i, s_{-i})$. A strategy profile is a Nash equilibrium if and only if no player has a profitable unilateral deviation.

C. Best Response

In words: A best response of player $i$ is a strategy that maximizes her payoff given the choices of the other players.

$$BR_i(s_{-i}) = \left\{ s_i \in S_i \;\mid\; g_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \geq g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) \quad \forall\, t_i \in S_i \right\}$$

Note: $BR_i(s_{-i})$ is a set. It can contain multiple strategies if there are ties.

$$s^* \text{ is a Nash equilibrium} \iff s_i^* \in BR_i(s_{-i}^*) \quad \forall\, i \in N$$
Mutual Best Response: A Nash equilibrium is a profile where every player is simultaneously best-responding to the strategies of all other players.
🔍 The Practical Matrix Underlining Method

For Player 1 (Row player): Go column by column. In each column, identify the maximum first payoff and underline it.

For Player 2 (Column player): Go row by row. In each row, identify the maximum second payoff and underline it.

Conclusion: Any cell where both payoffs are underlined is a Nash equilibrium.

D. Interpretations of Nash Equilibrium

Six interpretations explain why we should expect players to play a Nash equilibrium:

1
Prescription. An external individual (mediator, social planner) proposes a strategy profile. If the proposed profile is a Nash equilibrium, no player has an incentive to deviate unilaterally — the prescription is self-enforcing.
2
Communication. Players agree in advance on a strategy profile. A Nash equilibrium is a sustainable agreement: once the agreement is reached, nobody wants to deviate alone, so no player has a reason to break the deal.
3
Introspection. Players reason strategically about the game and about each other's reasoning. The argument is particularly compelling when there is a unique Nash equilibrium — strategic logic converges to a single prediction.
4
Convention. A social convention can coordinate players on a Nash equilibrium. Example: countries coordinate on driving on the right or on the left — both are Nash equilibria of the driving coordination game, and history or convention selects one.
5
Learning. If players repeatedly encounter similar strategic situations, they may learn over time how others play. Adaptive learning processes can converge toward a profile where nobody gains from changing strategy unilaterally.
6
Evolution. If species (or populations) adapt to one another over time through selection pressure, the resulting ecosystem may be interpreted using Nash-equilibrium logic — organisms that are best-responding to their environment survive.

Exercise 3.2.F · Cournot Duopoly Nash Equilibrium
In the Cournot duopoly, inverse demand is $P(Q) = 13 - q_1 - q_2$ (for $q_1 + q_2 \leq 13$), and marginal cost is $c = 0$. Firm $i$'s profit is $g_i(q_i, q_j) = q_i(13 - q_i - q_j)$.
Task: Derive the reaction (best-response) function $BR_i(q_j)$ for each firm. Solve for the Nash equilibrium $(q_1^*, q_2^*)$, the equilibrium price $P^*$, and the profits $g_1^*, g_2^*$.
1
Write the profit and maximise it.

Fix the rival quantity $q_j$. Firm $i$ chooses $q_i\geq0$:

$$g_i(q_i,q_j)=q_i(13-q_i-q_j)=13q_i-q_i^2-q_iq_j.$$$$\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial g_i}{\partial q_i}&=13-2q_i-q_j,\\[3pt]\frac{\partial^2 g_i}{\partial q_i^2}&=-2<0.\end{aligned}$$

The profit is strictly concave. If $q_j<13$, set the first derivative equal to zero; otherwise the maximum is at $q_i=0$:

$$13-2q_i-q_j=0\iff q_i=\frac{13-q_j}{2}.$$$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}BR_1(q_2)&=\max\{0,\tfrac{13-q_2}{2}\},\\BR_2(q_1)&=\max\{0,\tfrac{13-q_1}{2}\}.\end{aligned}}$$
2
Solve the two best-response equations.

Neither firm produces zero at equilibrium: if $q_1=0$, then $q_2=13/2$, but $BR_1(13/2)=13/4>0$. The same argument applies to firm 2. Therefore:

$$\begin{cases}2q_1+q_2=13,\\q_1+2q_2=13.\end{cases}$$

Subtract the second equation from the first:

$$q_1-q_2=0\Rightarrow q_1=q_2=q.$$

Substitute into either equation:

$$3q=13\iff q=\frac{13}{3}.$$$$\boxed{NE=\{(\tfrac{13}{3},\tfrac{13}{3})\}.}$$
3
Compute the price and profits. $$\begin{aligned}Q^*&=\frac{13}{3}+\frac{13}{3}=\frac{26}{3},\\[3pt]P^*&=13-\frac{26}{3}=\frac{39-26}{3}=\frac{13}{3},\\[3pt]g_i^*&=(P^*-0)q_i^*=\frac{13}{3}\cdot\frac{13}{3}=\frac{169}{9}.\end{aligned}$$

The equilibrium is the intersection of the two best-response curves. The exact fractions are the final answers.

Exercise · Cournot — General Best Responses
We now derive best responses in the general Cournot duopoly with linear demand $Q = a - P$ and constant marginal cost $c$, extending the $c = 0$ numerical example above.
Task: Derive $BR_i(q_j)$ for a general Cournot duopoly with demand $P = a - q_1 - q_2$ and constant marginal cost $c$. Plot the best-response curves and find the symmetric Nash equilibrium.
1
Profit and best response.

Fix $q_j$. The firm chooses $q_i\geq0$:

$$\begin{aligned}g_i(q_i,q_j)&=q_i(a-c-q_i-q_j),\\[3pt]\frac{\partial g_i}{\partial q_i}&=a-c-2q_i-q_j,\qquad\frac{\partial^2 g_i}{\partial q_i^2}=-2<0.\end{aligned}$$$$a-c-2q_i-q_j=0\iff q_i=\frac{a-c-q_j}{2}.$$

If this quantity is negative, the constrained maximum is zero. Hence:

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}BR_1(q_2)&=\max\{0,\tfrac{a-c-q_2}{2}\},\\BR_2(q_1)&=\max\{0,\tfrac{a-c-q_1}{2}\}.\end{aligned}}$$
2
Equilibrium when $a>c$.

A zero quantity cannot be an equilibrium response: if $q_1=0$, firm 2 chooses $(a-c)/2$, which gives firm 1 the positive best response $(a-c)/4$. Thus both equilibrium quantities are positive:

$$\begin{cases}2q_1+q_2=a-c,\\q_1+2q_2=a-c.\end{cases}$$

Subtracting the equations gives $q_1=q_2=q$. Then:

$$3q=a-c\iff\boxed{q_1^*=q_2^*=\frac{a-c}{3}.}$$

If $a\leq c$, $BR_i(q_j)=0$ for every $q_j\geq0$, so the equilibrium is $(0,0)$.

3
Draw the best-response curves for $a>c$.

Use $q_1$ horizontally and $q_2$ vertically. In the positive-output region:

$$\begin{aligned}BR_1:\quad &q_2=a-c-2q_1,\\[3pt]BR_2:\quad &q_2=\frac{a-c-q_1}{2}.\end{aligned}$$

The first line joins $(0,a-c)$ to $((a-c)/2,0)$. The second joins $(0,(a-c)/2)$ to $(a-c,0)$. Their intersection is the equilibrium.

Beyond these intercepts, the zero-output best responses follow the corresponding axes.

Proposition 1 — Strict Dominance & Nash Equilibrium

(1) If $s^*$ is an equilibrium in strictly dominant strategies, then $s^*$ is the unique Nash equilibrium of $\Gamma$.

(2) For any IESDS sequence $(\Gamma^k)$: $NE(\Gamma^k) = NE(\Gamma)$ for all $k$. IESDS never eliminates any Nash equilibrium.

(3) If $\Gamma$ can be solved by iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, then the strategy profile obtained at the end of the procedure is the unique Nash equilibrium of the game.

1
Proof of (1) — Existence: We first show that $s^*$ is a Nash equilibrium.

Because $s_i^*$ is strictly dominant for player $i$, it gives player $i$ a strictly higher payoff than any other strategy $t_i$, whatever the other players choose:
$$g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}) > g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) \qquad \text{for every } t_i \neq s_i^* \text{ and every } s_{-i}$$
• $s_i^*$ = player $i$’s strictly dominant strategy
• $t_i \neq s_i^*$ = any other strategy player $i$ could deviate to (a possible unilateral deviation)
• $s_{-i}$ = strategies chosen by all the other players
Now consider the particular situation in which the other players also play their strictly dominant strategies, so $s_{-i} = s_{-i}^*$. Therefore: $$g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) > g_i(t_i, s_{-i}^*) \qquad \text{for every } t_i \neq s_i^*$$
OptionPayoffComparison
STAY with $s_i^*$$g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*)$Strictly optimal
DEVIATE to $t_i \neq s_i^*$$g_i(t_i, s_{-i}^*)$Strictly worse ($<$)
Since $\text{STAY} > \text{DEVIATE}$ for every possible deviation $t_i$, player $i$ has no profitable unilateral deviation. This holds for every player $i$. Therefore, $s^*$ is a Nash equilibrium.
Nash condition: No player can improve their payoff by changing strategy alone.
Here, strict dominance gives an even stronger result: every deviation $t_i$ gives a strictly lower payoff.
2
Proof of (1) — Uniqueness: Now we show that $s^*$ is not only a Nash equilibrium, but the only Nash equilibrium.

Suppose, for contradiction, that there is another Nash equilibrium $\hat{s} \neq s^*$.
If the two profiles are different, then at least one player $j$ must be playing a different strategy: $$\hat{s}_j \neq s_j^*$$ But $s_j^*$ is player $j$’s strictly dominant strategy. Therefore, whatever the other players are doing — including when they play $\hat{s}_{-j}$ — player $j$ strictly prefers $s_j^*$: $$g_j(s_j^*, \hat{s}_{-j}) > g_j(\hat{s}_j, \hat{s}_{-j})$$

At $\hat{s}$, player $j$ is currently playing $\hat{s}_j$. But player $j$ can switch alone from $\hat{s}_j$ to $s_j^*$ and obtain a strictly higher payoff. So player $j$ has a profitable unilateral deviation.

Therefore $\hat{s}$ cannot be a Nash equilibrium, which contradicts our assumption.
Hence no other Nash equilibrium can exist: $$\mathbf{NE(\Gamma) = \{s^*\}}$$
Why unique? Any profile different from $s^*$ contains at least one player who is not playing their strictly dominant strategy. That player would want to deviate to their strictly dominant strategy. So any other profile cannot be Nash.
3
Proof of (3) — Solvability by IESDS:

We want to prove: If $\Gamma$ can be solved by IESDS, then the surviving strategy profile $s$ is the unique Nash equilibrium of $\Gamma$.

Step 1 — Use statement (2):
From statement (2), eliminating strictly dominated strategies does not eliminate Nash equilibria. Therefore, after each elimination, every Nash equilibrium of the original game must still be present in the reduced game. In particular, for the final reduced game $\Gamma^k$:
$$NE(\Gamma) \subseteq NE(\Gamma^k)$$
What does this inclusion mean? It means: "If a strategy profile is a Nash equilibrium of the original game $\Gamma$, then it must still be a Nash equilibrium of the reduced game $\Gamma^k$." So IESDS may remove strategies and strategy profiles, but it cannot remove a Nash equilibrium.

Step 2 — Use the fact that $\Gamma$ is solvable by IESDS:
By assumption, $\Gamma$ can be solved by IESDS. "Solvable by IESDS" means that after repeatedly eliminating strictly dominated strategies, only one strategy remains for each player. Therefore, in the final reduced game $\Gamma^k$, there is only one possible strategy profile. Call this profile $s$.

Step 3 — Combine the two ideas:
From Step 1, $NE(\Gamma) \subseteq NE(\Gamma^k)$. Every Nash equilibrium of the original game $\Gamma$ must be found among the Nash equilibria of the final reduced game $\Gamma^k$. But from Step 2, $\Gamma^k$ contains only one possible strategy profile: $s$. There is therefore no other profile available in $\Gamma^k$ that could be a Nash equilibrium. Hence, if $\Gamma$ has a Nash equilibrium, the only possible candidate is $s$.

Step 4 — Why is $s$ actually a Nash equilibrium?
At the end of IESDS, each player has only one remaining strategy in $\Gamma^k$. Therefore, no player has another remaining strategy $t_i$ to deviate to, so no player has a profitable unilateral deviation in $\Gamma^k$. Hence: $$s \in NE(\Gamma^k)$$ And because statement (2) tells us that Nash equilibria are preserved through the successive eliminations ($NE(\Gamma^k) = NE(\Gamma)$), $s$ is a Nash equilibrium of the original game $\Gamma$.

Conclusion:
There is only one surviving strategy profile $s$, and this profile is the Nash equilibrium. Therefore:
$$\mathbf{NE(\Gamma) = \{s\}}$$
Hence, $s$ is the unique Nash equilibrium of $\Gamma$. $\blacksquare$
Takeaway: IESDS preserves Nash equilibria. If only one profile survives, that profile must therefore be the unique Nash equilibrium.
Proposition 2 — Weak Dominance & Nash Equilibrium

(1) If $s^*$ is an equilibrium in weakly dominant strategies, then $s^*$ is a Nash equilibrium of $\Gamma$ (though not necessarily unique).

(2) Under iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies, $NE(\Gamma^{k+1}) \subseteq NE(\Gamma^k)$. The inclusion may be strict: weak elimination can remove valid Nash equilibria.

1
Let $s^*$ be an equilibrium in weakly dominant strategies. By definition, for every player $i \in N$ and every alternative strategy $t_i \in S_i$ (a possible unilateral deviation), strategy $s_i^*$ yields at least as high a payoff as $t_i$ against all opponent profiles $s_{-i}$: $$g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}) \geq g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) \qquad \forall\, t_i \in S_i,\; \forall\, s_{-i} \in S_{-i}$$ In particular, when all other players also play their equilibrium strategies $s_{-i} = s_{-i}^*$: $$g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \geq g_i(t_i, s_{-i}^*) \qquad \forall\, t_i \in S_i$$ Comparing STAY ($g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*)$) with DEVIATE to $t_i$ ($g_i(t_i, s_{-i}^*)$), no player has a profitable unilateral deviation. This is precisely the definition of a Nash equilibrium. $\blacksquare$
1
Setup: Let $\Gamma^{k+1}$ be obtained from $\Gamma^k$ by deleting a weakly dominated strategy $s_i$ of player $i$. By definition, there exists another strategy $t_i$ that remains available in $\Gamma^{k+1}$ such that: $$g_i(t_i, s_{-i}) \;\geq\; g_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \qquad \forall\, s_{-i} \in S_{-i}^k$$
2
Take any Nash equilibrium $s^*$ of the reduced game $\Gamma^{k+1}$.

For every player $j \neq i$: the strategy set of $j$ has not changed between $\Gamma^k$ and $\Gamma^{k+1}$, so player $j$ has no profitable deviation in $\Gamma^k$ either.

For player $i$: $s_i^*$ is a best response among all strategies remaining in $\Gamma^{k+1}$. In particular: $$g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \;\geq\; g_i(t_i, s_{-i}^*)$$
3
Show the deleted strategy cannot be a profitable deviation in $\Gamma^k$:

Since $t_i$ weakly dominates the deleted strategy $s_i$ in $\Gamma^k$: $$g_i(t_i, s_{-i}^*) \;\geq\; g_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*)$$ Combining both inequalities: $$g_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \;\geq\; g_i(t_i, s_{-i}^*) \;\geq\; g_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*)$$ Therefore $s_i$ (the deleted strategy) is not a profitable deviation from $s^*$ in $\Gamma^k$.
4
Conclusion:
Every player has no profitable deviation from $s^*$ in $\Gamma^k$. Thus $s^*$ is a Nash equilibrium of $\Gamma^k$, proving: $$NE(\Gamma^{k+1}) \;\subseteq\; NE(\Gamma^k)$$ The inclusion may be strict: eliminating a weakly dominated strategy can delete a Nash equilibrium in which that weakly dominated strategy was played. $\blacksquare$

Each remaining action is a best response to an opponent action. No further strict elimination is possible.

Find mutual best responses. $$\begin{aligned}BR_1(A)&=\{A\}\quad(1>0),& BR_1(B)&=\{C\}\quad(1>0),\\[3pt]BR_2(A)&=\{A\}\quad(1>0),& BR_2(C)&=\{B\}\quad(1>0).\end{aligned}$$

The mutual best responses are $(A,A)$ and $(C,B)$. Strict elimination preserves equilibria, so these are also the pure equilibria of the original game:

$$\boxed{NE_{\mathrm{pure}}=\{(A,A),(C,B)\}.}$$

Both give payoffs $(1,1)$. The mixed equilibrium is derived in Section 7 on the mixed-Nash page.

Exercise · Cournot Duopoly with $c = 1$
Consider the Cournot duopoly with inverse demand $P = 13 - q_1 - q_2$ and constant marginal cost $c = 1$ for both firms.
Task: Derive the best-response functions $BR_i(q_j)$. Solve for the Nash equilibrium $(q_1^*, q_2^*)$, the equilibrium price $P^*$, and the equilibrium profits $g_1^*, g_2^*$.
1
Write the profit.

Firm $i$ chooses its quantity $q_i\geq0$. The other firm’s quantity $q_j$ is fixed when firm $i$ decides. Here $P=13-q_i-q_j$ and $c=1$:

$$\begin{aligned}g_i(q_i,q_j)&=(P-c)q_i,\\[3pt]&=(13-q_i-q_j-1)q_i,\\[3pt]&=12q_i-q_i^2-q_iq_j.\end{aligned}$$
2
Find the best response.

Differentiate with respect to $q_i$ only:

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial g_i}{\partial q_i}&=12-2q_i-q_j,\\[3pt]\frac{\partial^2 g_i}{\partial q_i^2}&=-2<0.\end{aligned}$$

Profit is strictly concave, so the first-order condition gives the maximum whenever the quantity is positive:

$$12-2q_i-q_j=0\iff 2q_i=12-q_j\iff q_i=6-\frac{q_j}{2}.$$

If $q_j\geq12$, producing zero is optimal. Therefore:

$$\boxed{BR_i(q_j)=\max\{0,6-\tfrac{q_j}{2}\}.}$$

For example, $BR_i(0)=6$ and $BR_i(6)=3$: more output from the rival reduces the firm’s best response.

3
Solve for the Nash equilibrium.

At equilibrium, each firm chooses a best response to the other. Neither quantity can be zero: $q_1=0$ would imply $q_2=6$, but then $BR_1(6)=3\neq0$. The same argument applies to firm 2. Hence:

$$\begin{cases}q_1=6-\frac{q_2}{2},\\q_2=6-\frac{q_1}{2}.\end{cases}$$

Multiply each equation by $2$ and collect the quantities:

$$\begin{cases}2q_1+q_2=12,\\q_1+2q_2=12.\end{cases}$$

Subtract the second equation from the first:

$$q_1-q_2=0\Rightarrow q_1=q_2=q.$$

Substitute into $2q_1+q_2=12$:

$$3q=12\iff q=4.$$

Check for each firm:

$$BR_i(4)=6-2=4.$$

Thus:

$$\boxed{NE=\{(4,4)\}.}$$
4
Compute the price and profits. $$\begin{aligned}Q^*&=q_1^*+q_2^*=4+4=8,\\[3pt]P^*&=13-Q^*=13-8=5,\\[3pt]g_i^*&=(P^*-c)q_i^*=(5-1)\cdot4=16.\end{aligned}$$$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}(q_1^*,q_2^*)&=(4,4),\\P^*&=5,\\(g_1^*,g_2^*)&=(16,16).\end{aligned}}$$
5
IESDS: the first two rounds.

Round 1. For every $q_j\geq0$, $BR_i(q_j)\leq6$. Profit decreases beyond its maximum, so $q_i=6$ gives a strictly higher payoff than any $q_i>6$. Delete quantities above $6$:

$$\mathbb R_+\longrightarrow[0,6].$$

Round 2. Now $q_j\in[0,6]$, so:

$$BR_i(q_j)=6-\frac{q_j}{2}\geq6-\frac62=3.$$

Profit increases up to its maximum. Thus $q_i=3$ gives a strictly higher payoff than any $q_i<3$. Delete quantities below $3$:

$$[0,6]\longrightarrow[3,6].$$

These two rounds do not finish IESDS.

6
IESDS: find the limit.

On the current interval $[a,b]$, the smallest best response is to $b$ and the largest is to $a$:

$$[a,b]\longrightarrow\left[6-\frac b2,\,6-\frac a2\right].$$

For example, $[3,6]\longrightarrow[3,\frac92]$. Quantities outside the new interval are strictly dominated by its nearest endpoint. Its length is:

$$\left(6-\frac a2\right)-\left(6-\frac b2\right)=\frac{b-a}{2}.$$

The intervals are nested and their lengths are halved each time. Both endpoints therefore converge to the same limit $\ell$:

$$\ell=6-\frac\ell2\iff2\ell=12-\ell\iff3\ell=12\iff\boxed{\ell=4}.$$

Continued IESDS leaves $\{4\}$ for each firm in the limit, consistent with the NE $(4,4)$.

Master Revision Map — Solving Any Static Game

The 3-Step Exam Algorithm
1
Step 1: Check for Strictly Dominant Strategies

Does any player have a strategy that beats all their other strategies against every opponent profile? If every player has one $\implies$ that profile is the unique strictly dominant strategy equilibrium and the unique Nash equilibrium. Stop.

2
Step 2: Run Strict IESDS

Iteratively eliminate strictly dominated strategies. Remember: strict IESDS never eliminates Nash equilibria and is order-independent. If the procedure reduces the game to a single profile $\implies$ it is the unique Nash equilibrium. Stop.

3
Step 3: Apply the Best Response Method

On the remaining matrix: underline Player 1's maximal first payoff(s) in each column, and Player 2's maximal second payoff(s) in each row. Any cell where both payoffs are underlined is a pure Nash equilibrium. If no cell has both underlined $\implies$ no pure strategy Nash equilibrium exists.
